# Come scoppiano le guerre: La seconda guerra mondiale

## Versailles and the double illusion

Good evening. Tonight we have a rather difficult road ahead of us. You may say, “Well, last night was hardly a picnic either.” True. But the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story.

The only comic notes—and there will be a few—come from Italy’s part in it. Whatever one may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and the rest of them, in those very dramatic weeks they are the ones who supply almost the only touches of comedy. You’ll see.

So: how did the Second World War break out?

It has been said that, in reality, the Second World War breaks out in 1919, when the Treaty of Versailles is signed. Versailles ends the First World War, yes, but it ends that war while creating an enormous number of grievances and frustrations: territorial disputes, minority disputes, resentments, and political myths ready to be exploited.

You remember that even Italy—even victorious Italy, which gets Trento and Trieste and swallows up South Tyrol—isn’t satisfied. In Italy people begin speaking of the *mutilated victory*. The phrase grows into a nationalist political myth. D’Annunzio’s original line, published in October 1918 before the armistice and before the Paris settlement, is addressed to Victory itself: “Our victory, you will not be mutilated. No one can break your knees or clip your wings. Where are you running? Where are you rising?”

Later, that becomes the compact slogan: the mutilated victory. And even victorious Italy then sinks toward something close to civil war and into dictatorship, amid the consequences of a war that many Italians feel has ended badly. So just imagine the countries that have lost.

Germany comes out of the First World War and the Versailles settlement frustrated, with a powerful sense that it has suffered an injustice. What injustice?

To begin with, Germany has to surrender territory to a new country. Versailles creates or re-creates a whole series of states, among them Poland, and territories containing German populations pass under Polish sovereignty. Then there are arrangements that seem almost designed to remain permanently contentious.

There is one city—today the name may make you think of Poland and of Lech Wałęsa, if it makes you think of anything at all—but at the time everyone talks about it: Gdańsk, then generally called Danzig. It is a major port with an overwhelmingly German population, situated beside the new Polish state. Versailles does not simply give Danzig to Poland. It removes the city from German sovereignty and makes it a Free City under League of Nations protection, with special Polish rights. In other words, neither fully German nor Polish: precisely the sort of compromise that leaves everyone arguing over it.

Many of Danzig’s German inhabitants want union with Germany, and they aren’t permitted to have it. In Germany an irredentist campaign develops—the sort of thing Italians know very well from the prewar campaigns over Trento and Trieste: the separated brothers, the people left outside the nation.

And so the cry goes up: “Danzig remains German!”

And around it comes the wider revisionist refrain: Danzig is German; Versailles has wronged us; one day this will have to be put right. The Nazi program states the larger demand more bluntly: “We demand equal rights for the German people in relation to the other nations, and the annulment of the peace treaties of Versailles and Saint-Germain.”

But Danzig isn’t the only problem. Other problems are created by the dissolution of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The empire is broken up; new states appear or reappear—Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia—and each of them comes with minority questions.

Austria is now a small, overwhelmingly German-speaking country. Many Austrians think: well, if this is how the war has ended, why shouldn’t we join Germany? We’re Germans too. But the victors refuse to allow the union. Austria is to remain independent.

Then there is Czechoslovakia: the state of Czechs and Slovaks, but also the home of a very large German-speaking population in lands that had belonged to the Habsburg monarchy. Those people had once been German-speaking citizens of Austria; now they find themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, and many do not feel Czechoslovak at all.

So there is a whole bundle of problems left by the peace settlement, and some of them, taken by themselves, are perfectly understandable. They are very often problems of borders and minorities living outside the state with which they identify. The fact that the grievances are understandable does not mean every proposed solution is legitimate, still less that they cause the war on their own. But if we ignore them, we understand nothing about what comes next.

I would have liked to begin tonight by saying: very well, it is 10 August 1939, and this happens, and then that happens. We’ll get there. But first we have to spend a little time on these more distant causes. Otherwise the events of August make no sense.

And the distant background is what I’ve just described: a frustrated Germany with claims involving Poland and Danzig; Austria, because so many Austrians are German-speaking and want union; and Czechoslovakia, because millions of Germans live there and many feel alienated or discriminated against.

Then, in 1933, the Nazis become Germany’s largest party and Hitler is appointed chancellor after political bargaining among conservative elites. They have never won a majority in a free election, but revision of Versailles is central to their appeal and policy.

Hitler comes to power with a program containing all sorts of splendid things, as such programs do—but the point many Germans perhaps feel most strongly is this: Versailles must be undone. The Europe created at Versailles must be remade. It is unjust; Germany has been wronged; the borders must be changed. And, as we’ve just seen, the Nazi program doesn’t merely ask for a mild review. It demands annulment of the Versailles and Saint-Germain treaties.

The story of how the Second World War begins is therefore, in one important sense, the story of a double illusion.

The first is the illusion of the great democratic powers—Britain and France—that some of these grievances can be satisfied without producing a general upheaval. They look at certain claims and think: on this point there is something to negotiate. Perhaps an agreement can be found. Danzig here, an adjustment there—something limited, something peaceful, something that will settle the dispute rather than explode the whole European order.

It is an illusion, as we shall see. But it isn’t merely foolishness. Britain and France are also constrained by military weakness, the memory of the First World War, public opinion, imperial commitments, and the hope that negotiation might buy time for rearmament.

The other illusion is Hitler’s: that he can take back everything Germany has lost—and rather more besides—that he can again make Germany the dominant great power in Europe, and that no one will oppose him seriously.

So these are reciprocal miscalculations, though not equal responsibilities. Hitler supplies the expansionist initiative; Britain and France repeatedly misjudge how far he intends to go and whether limited concessions can stop him. The democracies’ illusion, and Hitler’s illusion. That is where our story begins.

## Halifax and the climate of appeasement

I have decided to begin the story on 19 November 1937. Less than two years remain before the war. We know that; they did not.

On that day Lord Halifax, then Lord President of the Council, visits Hitler at the Berghof near Berchtesgaden. Halifax tells him that Britain does not insist on preserving every detail of the Versailles settlement: “There were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today ... If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.”

And Halifax says something else: Germany is “the bulwark of the West against Bolshevism.”

We must remember the silent guest at every European table: the Soviet Union. Stalin is in power. We know that he has put world revolution in a drawer; Western politicians do not. To them this is not merely the country of communists but of Bolsheviks, dangerous revolutionaries, a country to be kept at arm’s length. Hitler’s Germany, ferociously anti-communist, with German communists already in concentration camps, can therefore look useful to influential people in Britain and France. Not to everyone—but to enough people to matter.

In France there is a strong Communist Party, a strong Socialist Party, and the Popular Front. We have forgotten how many hopes—and how many terrors—the Popular Front arouses. Among part of the French bourgeoisie, industrialists, officers, clergy, the sentiment is: “Hitler rather than Blum.”

Today the name of François Hollande hardly summons visions of social revolution. The Popular Front is another matter. To its supporters it promises a new society; to its enemies it looks like the first door opening toward Bolshevism. So when they look at Hitler they see brutality, of course, but some also see the man who has smashed the German left. And they think: unpleasant, certainly—yet useful.

Hitler smells the air. There are things one might attempt. Perhaps one need only wait for the opportunity.

People have argued endlessly over whether he had planned every step from the beginning, all the way to Poland. You know the line from Woody Allen’s character in *Manhattan Murder Mystery*: “I can’t listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” But Poland is the last act of this story, not the first.

In the 1960s A. J. P. Taylor causes a scandal by arguing that Hitler waits upon events, expects others to provide opportunities, and seizes them; in foreign policy he seeks German predominance, as other great powers had sought predominance. But Hitler does not merely wait for opportunities. He creates them.

## Austria: creating the opportunity

He begins with Austria. There is an Austrian Nazi Party that wants union with Germany and takes orders from Berlin. Hitler gives it the signal: agitation, violence, terrorism, disorder, political murder. In March 1938—four months after Halifax’s visit—the Austrian Nazis seize power under German pressure, and Berlin manufactures an invitation for German troops to enter and “restore order.” On 12 March the troops cross the frontier. The Anschluss: annexation.

The German army is hardly the irresistible machine it will soon appear to be: roughly thirty percent of the tanks break down on the road. But no foreign power intervenes.

There is genuine enthusiasm in Austria, and then there is a plebiscite under occupation, coercive propaganda, and exclusions. The official result is 99.73 percent for union. Cardinal Innitzer and the other Austrian bishops declare: “We are also convinced that the work of the National Socialist movement has warded off the danger of utterly destructive, godless Bolshevism ... On the day of the plebiscite it is for us bishops a self-evident national duty, as Germans, to declare ourselves for the German Reich.”

Britain and France protest, but do not act. *The Times* writes that Austria “was destined sooner or later to find herself in close association with the German Reich,” comparing it to the old union of Scotland and England. Hitler takes note: Austria annexed, no intervention.

## The Sudeten crisis and Munich

Within days Hitler summons Konrad Henlein, leader of the Sudeten German movement in Czechoslovakia. Again: demands designed to remain unacceptable, street violence, agitation, propaganda, chaos. The German press pounds away: Germans oppressed, discriminated against, arrested, tortured; the Czech police portrayed as criminal. Some grievances existed, but many atrocity claims were propaganda, and contemporary reporting contradicted them.

Day after day the German papers tell readers that Sudeten Germans suffer unbearable oppression. Arrested. Beaten. Tortured. Their women violated. Their national life strangled. Repeat it often enough and the dispute no longer looks like a problem to negotiate; it looks like an emergency demanding rescue.

Britain and France press Prague to concede. They say: the Germans are not entirely wrong; surely the Czechoslovak government can meet them halfway; we are not going to unleash another war over this.

One government publicly proposes that the powers act together to guarantee Czechoslovakia: the Soviet Union. Litvinov calls for “immediate practical measures.” The proposal depends on French action and military access, and it is discounted. Britain’s ambassador in Moscow concludes that Russia can effectively be treated “as outside European politics.”

Notice the difficulty of seeing a changing world. “European politics” still means Britain and France. The United States is absent; Japan is somewhere else; the Soviet Union supposedly does not count.

For months the German press keeps beating the same drum—Sudeten suffering, Sudeten suffering—while Britain and France keep telling Prague: make concessions. By September the crisis is so severe that London and Paris decide a conference must be arranged. Today we would call it a summit, a negotiating table; those expressions had not yet become compulsory. They ask Mussolini to approach his friend Hitler. Hitler agrees.

How do you get Hitler to the table? Ask Mussolini. They are friends; perhaps the Duce can present the proposal in a form Hitler will accept. So Britain and France approach Rome and, in substance, say: ask your friend whether all of us might meet and arrange concessions to Germany. Notice what is already assumed. The purpose of the conference is not to decide whether Hitler should receive territory. It is to decide how.

## Munich and peace for our time

On 29–30 September 1938 Hitler, Mussolini, Neville Chamberlain, and Édouard Daladier meet in Munich. Czechoslovakia is not a negotiating party; its representatives wait outside to learn what has been decided about their country. The agreement orders evacuation and German occupation of the Sudeten territories.

Chamberlain and Daladier sincerely want to avoid another slaughter. They belong to the generation haunted by 1914–18. Everything but another war. Never again, at almost any price. Their motives also include unreadiness, alliance weakness, empire, public opinion, and the hope of buying time—but the nightmare is real.

Anything but war. Never again. Peace at almost any price.

And what is the price? Czechoslovakia must surrender territory. Better that, they think, than millions dead. Hitler takes note: they have not merely failed to stop me; they have helped me take part of Czechoslovakia.

It is hard to speak of this without tragic hindsight. These are first-rank politicians acting under immense constraints, and they do not understand what they face.

Chamberlain returns to London, a return for which he will be mocked for decades. As he comes down the aircraft steps, with photographers and a crowd around him, he waves the agreement. At Heston aerodrome he says: “The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is in my view only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace.”

He also says that he met Hitler again that morning and holds up the paper bearing both their signatures: they regard the agreement signed the previous night as a symbol of the desire of their two peoples never to go to war with one another again. Later, outside Downing Street: “My good friends, for the second time in our history, a British Prime Minister has returned from Germany bringing peace with honour. I believe it is peace for our time. We thank you from the bottom of our hearts. Go home and get a nice quiet sleep.”

Eleven months remain before war.

The policy acquires a name that later becomes almost an insult in British politics: appeasement. The tragic irony is that concessions intended to prevent war failed to deter Hitler and weakened Czechoslovakia, though they also bought Britain time to rearm. Appeasement contributed to the disaster; it did not mechanically cause it by itself.

Appeasement: pacification, settling grievances, removing causes of conflict. At the time it can sound responsible and humane. Later the word becomes an accusation. We will meet it again when we talk about Margaret Thatcher: nobody in British politics wants to be the new Chamberlain, the leader who mistakes surrender for peace.

Hitler is exceptionally skilled at misleading his interlocutors. Before Munich he tells Chamberlain, and on 26 September proclaims publicly: “It is the last territorial demand which I have to make in Europe.” He has said it. He sounds sincere. So: sleep soundly.

## Poland and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

Chamberlain and Daladier are not the only people who misread him. Mussolini believes him too. Even Poland believes him—and Poland will soon be the victim.

As Czechoslovakia is dismembered, Poland delivers an ultimatum and occupies Zaolzie, a disputed district with a Polish majority. Warsaw opportunistically takes part in the dismantling. Poland is militarist, governed by colonels, half a democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and as anti-communist as can be. The Poles think friendship with Hitler is possible because both sides are anti-communist. Who is more anti-communist than Hitler? The Poles think they are. At the end of 1938 and the beginning of 1939, they believe they are Hitler’s friends.

On 30 January 1939, seven months before the world war begins, Hitler tells the Reichstag that friendship between Germany and Poland is “one of the reassuring factors in the political life of Europe.” He also says: “The Polish state respects the national conditions in the Free City of Danzig, and Germany respects Polish rights.”

In the January talks Ribbentrop tempts Foreign Minister Józef Beck with Ukrainian compensation and access toward the Black Sea. The prospect is an eastward expansion into Soviet Ukraine, where there are Polish minorities. Beck listens, resists alignment, and still believes Poland can manage its dangerous neighbor.

Meanwhile, through January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia comes apart. It has lost the whole German-populated belt, most of its industry, and its frontier fortifications. Slovakia wants independence—an old story, and Slovakia is independent today. Czechoslovakia sinks further and faces a Hungarian invasion. Hitler decides to liquidate the problem.

On the night of 14–15 March 1939 President Emil Hácha is summoned to Berlin. Hitler tells him German troops will enter at six in the morning and resistance will be crushed. Göring threatens to bomb Prague. Hácha collapses and receives medical treatment. At last, under coercion, he signs a declaration placing the fate of the Czech people and country “trustingly in the hands of the Führer.”

German troops occupy Bohemia and Moravia. Slovakia has already declared independence under German pressure. Nobody in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, nobody has intervened. This is no longer a claim about bringing Germans into Germany. Hitler has destroyed what remained of a non-German state.

You may say: it was about time. Chamberlain begins to think that perhaps Hitler cannot be trusted after all. On 17 March in Birmingham—not in Parliament—he speaks as though the words are things he does not want to say: if solemn and repeated guarantees can so easily be ignored, what reliance can be placed on other guarantees from the same source? “Is this the last attack upon a small State or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” The doubt has begun. Prague is a turning point in British policy, though rearmament and doubts had already begun.

## Danzig and the failed Soviet opening

Now Hitler turns to Poland. Germany had been pressing since October 1938 for Danzig’s return and an extraterritorial road and railway across the Corridor, offering in exchange a longer non-aggression declaration and a guarantee of Poland’s frontier. Poland refuses unilateral alteration while leaving negotiation open; German pressure intensifies.

Three months after calling German-Polish friendship a reassuring foundation of European politics, Hitler speaks again in the same place. On 28 April he calls Danzig perhaps Germany’s most painful problem. He says he thought a peaceful solution would help remove tension from Europe, but the Polish government had refused his offer; he deeply regrets this “incomprehensible” attitude. He tells the Reichstag that he had offered Poland “a solution which, in view of the intimate relations between Germany and Poland, solved the questions under discussion in the most favourable manner for Poland.” He renounces the 1934 declaration.

From then on, conflict gathers around Danzig and its border with Poland: incidents, shootings, Nazi militias in the city, the Polish customs post outside, incidents every day. Propaganda turns each one into proof of Polish persecution.

Britain and France now begin to think that perhaps something should be done. What? They propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Even before it is signed, Chamberlain gives Poland an assurance. On 31 March he says Britain, with France in the same position, will lend Poland all support in its power if action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland considers it vital to resist. If Poland is attacked by someone—who could that be?—Britain and France are ready to defend it. It is conditional and initially unilateral; the reciprocal Anglo-Polish agreement comes on 25 August. France already has obligations to Poland.

With hindsight one says: it was about time. What else could they do? At the time, however, this is extremely difficult to sell to public opinion in Britain and France. War for Danzig? On 4 May the French politician Marcel Déat publishes the famous headline: “Die for Danzig?” His conclusion is sharper: fight beside our Polish friends for common defense, yes—“But to die for Danzig, no!” The phrase travels everywhere and becomes part of the crisis.

In April and May London and Paris do something else that had seemed unheard-of: they explore an agreement with the Soviet Union, a military defense agreement in case war comes. The negotiation drags on for months. Its history is sad and depressing, because one thinks: if only they had made that agreement. Yet nobody seems truly to want it. They are all afraid of one another.

The telegrams and replies show the rhythm. Whenever Britain and France make a proposal, the Russians reply the next day. Then weeks pass. Weeks, weeks. At last the British and French take the next step. The Russians reply the next day, and the negotiation drags forward like this.

Stalin takes note. They are asking him to negotiate, but do they mean it? Or do they want to draw him in and then leave him alone to deal with Germany? He decides he cannot take that risk. Britain and France appear lukewarm.

Stalin lets the Germans know that, although their countries are enemies—one communist, the other Nazi—they have commercial interests. Perhaps negotiations for a German-Soviet trade agreement would be a good idea. The Germans agree. Through the summer Stalin negotiates at two tables without anyone knowing: with Britain and France at one, and with Germany at the other, waiting to see what happens.

## Italy’s alliance without the means for war

Italy is Nazi Germany’s closest friend. On 22 May 1939 the two countries sign the Pact of Steel. The Fascist press erupts with eternal, unbreakable friendship: the two peoples are joined by “an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.”

In reality Mussolini is worried. The Nazis have assured him that they do not want war for the moment, but he is no longer so sure.

Why is Mussolini worried? Italy has no resources left. The Ethiopian war is a triumph, and the intervention beside Franco in Spain also goes well enough. But wars cost terrifying sums. Ethiopia and Spain have opened a chasm in the Italian budget. There is no money left for anything, especially modern weapons.

Everyone has understood which way the wind is blowing, and every country is testing new weapons. Countries are putting new fighters such as the Spitfire and new tanks into production; equipment that was good two or three years earlier ages rapidly. Italy alone has no money to do it. Mussolini knows this, though at times he prefers to forget it. In the summer of 1939 he fears that the Germans may decide to go to war.

Galeazzo Ciano is Mussolini’s son-in-law and foreign minister. Five years later he will be shot after the Verona trial, but that is another story. In early August he writes in his diary that the time has come to see how matters stand: the stakes are too high. He flies to Germany.

## Ciano discovers that Germany wants war

On 11 August Ciano meets his German counterpart, Ribbentrop, at Fuschl near Salzburg. Ciano has been sent to explain that war at that moment would be madness and must absolutely be avoided. He asks what all this agitation over Poland is about: “What do you want? Danzig?” Ribbentrop answers: “We no longer want Danzig. We want war.”

They are at a reception, everyone holding champagne glasses. Ciano is bursting to tell his collaborators what Ribbentrop has just said, but they are surrounded by guests. At last he passes one of them and whispers in his ear: “We are coming to blows.”

Ciano speaks with Ribbentrop again. Yes, Ribbentrop says, we will make war on Poland. It is decided. We will crush Poland. And Britain and France? They will not intervene.

The wager returns, as in 1914. History repeats itself first as tragedy and then as farce, and this time they actually bet. Ribbentrop says: if I win, you owe me a fine Italian Renaissance painting. If you win, I will give you a collection of antique German weapons. But I will win; they will not intervene. We will make war, and it will not become a world war, because Britain and France will not dare intervene.

The gala dinner ends, the reception ends, the toasts end. Ciano returns to the hotel with his collaborators, burning to tell them everything. Then he wonders whether the Germans have put microphones in the room. They lock themselves in the bathroom, and there Ciano tells them: the Germans have decided to make war. The countdown begins: 12 August.

## 12–18 August: two negotiations racing

Saturday, 12 August. Summer holidays. Milan empties; half a million people take trains toward the sea. The Venice Film Festival is under way; Maria Bellonci wins the Viareggio Prize with *Lucrezia Borgia*. Everyday summer life continues, and in Moscow the Anglo-French military mission finally arrives after a slow voyage.

The Soviets ask about powers. Marshal Voroshilov displays authority to sign. The British admiral, Reginald Drax, can negotiate but not sign. The French general, Aimé Doumenc, has Daladier’s authority to conclude a convention, though he remains dependent on Paris. So the missions are not identical—but the British weakness and the general lack of political freedom disappoint Moscow.

The first Soviet question is almost embarrassingly practical: can you sign? Drax cannot. Doumenc can go further, but decisive political questions still travel back to Paris. After months of delay, Voroshilov sees delegates who can talk, calculate divisions, discuss maps—and still may have to ask permission at the decisive moment.

Today “full powers” can sound like a ceremonial technicality. There are telephones, telegraphs, aircraft—surely instructions can arrive. But in a crisis every exchange takes time, every clarification opens another argument, and everyone understands the difference between a man who can conclude an agreement in the room and a man who can only say, “I shall report to my government.”

At almost the same moment the Soviet government signals that Ribbentrop might usefully come to Moscow.

So watch the irony. The British and French have finally reached Moscow, and on the same day Moscow opens the door to the German foreign minister. One delegation has come by slow stages with limited freedom. The other may arrive by aircraft empowered to make a bargain.

So watch the two tables. At one table British and French officers have arrived after months of discussion, carrying restricted powers and asking for fresh instructions. At the other, Berlin and Moscow exchange short messages and immediately ask when the foreign minister can fly. One table crawls. The other begins to race.

On 12 August Hitler explains the timetable to Ciano. Poland, he says, must be dealt with quickly. Operations should end by mid-October, before autumn weather cripples roads, airfields, and mechanized forces; the campaign may take six weeks. Hence the intended opening before the end of August. Ciano writes on 13 August: “They have deceived us and lied to us.”

Count backward with Hitler. By mid-October the rains arrive. Roads turn to mud, airfields become difficult, mechanized movement slows. Six weeks to defeat Poland. Therefore: attack by the end of August. Ciano realizes that he is hearing a military calendar, not diplomatic bluster.

Monday, 14 August. Hitler attends Mozart in Salzburg. In Moscow the Soviet soldiers put the central question on the table: if we are to defend Poland, will Poland allow the Red Army to cross Polish territory to meet the Germans? The Anglo-French officers must refer home.

The question is logical and terrifying. How can Soviet troops fight Germany without reaching Poland? But if the Red Army enters Poland, who guarantees that it will leave? For Moscow, passage proves that the alliance is serious. For Warsaw, passage may be another name for occupation.

And “refer home” means exactly what it sounds like. A question is asked in Moscow. The general and admiral write it down. It is encoded, transmitted, decoded, taken through ministries, discussed with the Poles, and returned. Everyone knows time is running out, yet the machinery still moves at peacetime speed.

Bonnet summons the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz, back from the seaside. The ambassador’s answer is predictably hostile: Poland fears Soviet entry perhaps more than German attack. He boasts: “It is Poland which, from the first days, will carry the war into Germany.”

Where is the ambassador on 15 August? At the seaside. They call him back to Paris. Bonnet says: grant passage if Germany attacks. Łukasiewicz says he must ask Warsaw, but he knows the answer. And when Bonnet warns him that Hitler expects to crush Poland quickly, the ambassador produces that extraordinary confidence: Poland will carry the war into Germany.

Transit becomes the immediate impasse. Delay, weak mandates, mutual distrust, Soviet bargaining and the German alternative have all done their damage; now, without passage, there is no credible Soviet military plan for Poland.

On 15 August the German Foreign Ministry tests Britain. Ambassador Nevile Henderson answers with brutal clarity: it is “quite untrue” that Britain will not fight for Poland; if Germany uses force, Britain will fulfill its obligations.

This is important. Hitler’s illusion does not survive because nobody warns him. He is warned. The warning is explicit. The error is that he does not believe it.

By 16 August Berlin is writing formally about Ribbentrop’s journey. German officials are also asking the decisive question: Britain has made promises—but will Britain truly fight? Henderson has answered. Hitler hears the answer and discounts it.

Yet the Fascist press tells another story. On 18 August *Corriere della Sera* announces that Polish provocation is worsening the crisis and that Danzig demands reunion with Germany.

And Poland still refuses Soviet passage. Marshal Śmigły-Rydz captures the fear: “With the Germans we risk losing our freedom; with the Russians we lose our soul.”

## 19–23 August: the pact with the devil

Saturday, 19 August. News reaches Moscow that Poland will not accept an agreement with Russia, and the negotiation runs aground. Molotov writes to Berlin: Ribbentrop should come; shall they meet on the 26th or 27th?

On Sunday, 20 August, Hitler sends Stalin a personal telegram. It is the first time a Western head of government has written personally to the Soviet leader; until then the Soviet Union had been treated like a country of plague victims, addressed indirectly and under the table. Hitler says he is pleased Stalin has agreed to receive Ribbentrop, but asks why they should wait until the 26th or 27th. Make it the 22nd or 23rd.

Monday, 21 August. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for a way to avoid war. They think of doing what they did the year before: a fine international conference at Munich, where everyone can reach agreement. They telephone Berlin to propose the idea to Ribbentrop.

According to Ciano’s diary, Ribbentrop cannot be found. At last he answers that afternoon: he cannot discuss these matters now because he is waiting for an important message from Moscow.

Stalin receives Hitler’s telegram at three that afternoon and replies at five: certainly, let us meet sooner. Ribbentrop can come to Moscow the day after tomorrow, the 23rd, and they will sign a German-Soviet non-aggression pact. “The Soviet Government have instructed me to inform you that they agree to Herr von Ribbentrop arriving in Moscow on August 23.”

It is an absolutely unexpected and shocking decision, one that contributes powerfully to what follows. To people at the time, Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union appear to be the two most opposed and hostile countries in existence. And they are. The agreement demonstrates the extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and coldness of both the Nazis and Stalin’s group. The world is stunned.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are too weak and do not really want an agreement, so he will make one with the Nazis instead. He needs security, even at the cost of a pact with the devil—for the moment, the pact is useful.

There is another piece of context that almost nobody knew then and few remember now. In those same days the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared border war with Japan in Mongolia, at Khalkhin Gol. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets and borders Japanese-controlled Manchukuo. This is a full-scale war involving entire army corps.

The fighting costs the Soviet side 25,655 losses, and Japanese losses are also severe. Both governments keep the scale quiet; the frontier war barely registers in the European crisis.

Japanese forces cross from China into Mongolia; the Mongols ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. They win decisively. With the battle still under way on the 21st, Stalin decides to make the pact with Germany: at least one frontier must be secured.

Ciano understands what Germany has achieved. “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke,” he writes. “The European situation is upset.”

Ciano and Mussolini are shocked but also admiring: these Germans are formidable. Ciano observes that Britain, and France even more, are full of communists. How will they now fight Germany, which has suddenly become the Soviet Union’s friend? He concludes that Italy should wait and, if possible, be ready to take its own share of the spoils.

On Tuesday, 22 August, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. The moment is right, as in 1914, but he is not speaking about the armies’ readiness. War must come as soon as possible, while he is alive: Germany will never again have a leader with his power and following, and a criminal or an idiot could kill him at any moment. “Essentially all depends on me, on my existence, because of my political talents.”

And war must come while Mussolini is still there. “The second favourable personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive.” As long as Mussolini remains, Hitler says, Germany can trust Italy; he is the only man in Italy with steady nerves. Hitler still trusts his old friend at the very moment Mussolini has decided to stay out.

On Wednesday, 23 August, the agreement is announced in the world press. *Corriere della Sera* calls it “the grand political success of the Axis,” says the dark maneuver of the democratic encirclers has been broken, and reports astonishment and dismay in the “anti-totalitarian countries.” “Anti-totalitarian” is a bad word in Fascist usage: Italy is totalitarian; Mussolini invented the conception. The soft French and the others are anti-totalitarian.

The same newspaper says Poland must seek salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany. Poland is beginning to yield and wonder whether an agreement even with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible. But it is late.

That day Ribbentrop flies to Moscow. He signs the public non-aggression pact and also the secret protocol, which is not made public, dividing spheres in Eastern Europe. In Poland the line is approximately the Narew, Vistula, and San, with the final shape of any Polish state left for the two powers to decide together. Partition is there in substance.

## 23–26 August: Italy finds a way out

Hitler receives the British ambassador that day because his remaining concern is to keep Britain out. Their conversation about alleged Polish atrocities against Germans becomes morbid. Hitler says he has reports of many Germans being castrated by Poles. Henderson happens to know one case: the German concerned was a sexual maniac who had been treated as he deserved. Hitler answers that he knows six cases, not one.

Hitler also says Poland is the last problem. “I am an artist, not a warrior.” Once the Polish question is settled, he says, he will retire from politics and devote himself to art. Henderson reports the conversation to London.

Friday, 25 August. Hitler orders the attack for the following day. Then he receives a letter in which Mussolini tells him the truth for the first time: Italy is not ready for war. But Mussolini does not have the courage to tell the whole truth. He writes that Italy cannot fight now without large supplies of raw materials from Germany. If Germany attacks Poland and the conflict remains local, Italy will give political and economic support; if it provokes general war, Italy can intervene only if Germany immediately supplies the matériel Italy lacks.

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He postpones the attack planned for 26 August by a few days and asks Mussolini exactly which raw materials Italy needs. Mussolini’s warning matters, but so does the Anglo-Polish alliance signed that day; Hitler is still trying to detach Britain from Poland.

Saturday, 26 August. Mussolini and the chiefs of staff meet at Palazzo Venezia to draw up the list. Ciano has briefed the generals: in compiling it, no criminal optimism. In other words, lay it on thick. It becomes known as the molybdenum list.
It asks for six million tonnes of coal—twice Italy’s annual production—two million tonnes of steel, one year’s Italian production, seven million tonnes of mineral oils, one million tonnes of timber, and then copper, sodium nitrate, potash salts, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium.

The list goes to Ambassador Attolico in Berlin, who takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks at it and asks when Italy needs all this material. Attolico has received no instructions. He understands the atmosphere and improvises: immediately. It has been calculated that moving it would require 17,000 trains.

Ribbentrop goes to Hitler, and at last they understand: Italy will not join them and will remain outside. Very well, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini that Germany will fight alone even if Italy cannot enter, but asks Italy not to advertise too loudly that it will remain out; discretion itself will help Germany.

Hitler adds one small request, indicative of the future: Italy could help by sending workers to Germany. Quietly, a long story begins. The Germans have opened their eyes: they cannot count on Italian military help, but they can count on workers. Organized recruitment has existed since 1938; now the flow expands, eventually carrying roughly half a million Italian civilians to Germany by 1943, before the mass coercion of Italian military internees.

## 27–30 August: negotiation performed to fail

Sunday, 27 August. Germany introduces ration cards for food. Monday the 28th, Britain and France still think the Poles might be persuaded to negotiate. They insist: go and ask the Germans exactly what they want, and let us see whether it can be discussed.

Tuesday the 29th, Italy bans private cars and imposes austerity in restaurants: menus may offer one dish, meat or fish, but only one. Henderson goes to Hitler to see whether negotiation is still possible at the last moment. Wednesday the 30th, Italy suspends the world cycling championships at Varese. Coffee can no longer be sold to private customers, not even by the cup at a bar; it is all reserved for the armed forces. France suspends train traffic and evacuates the Vincennes zoo to save the animals.

In every capital air-raid shelters are being built and gas masks distributed. Governments are certain that war will begin with terrible aerial bombardments of cities using poison gas.

Henderson reports that Hitler was not pleasant that evening; the interview was stormy. Hitler kept saying that he desired British friendship more than anything in the world but could not sacrifice Germany’s vital interests. Still, if Britain guaranteed that the Poles were truly willing to negotiate, they could send an emissary with full powers to Berlin the next day, and negotiation could continue.

On Wednesday, 30 August, Halifax—the same man who two years earlier had told Hitler that Germany’s claims were understandable—writes to Henderson: “It is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.” Henderson replies that he had made the same observation to Hitler the previous evening. Hitler answered that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in an hour and a half.

Desperate to avoid war, the British put heavy pressure on Poland: do not provoke the Germans; stop the Polish newspaper articles against Germany and the anti-German campaign; send a plenipotentiary to Berlin at once. Henderson tells Ribbentrop that Britain is doing everything possible and advising Poland to make peace and reach an agreement. Ribbentrop says British advice has so far had “cursed little effect.” Henderson replies that he is surprised to hear such language from a foreign minister.

Then begins a new comedy, a rather black German comedy. Ribbentrop says Germany has prepared terms for agreement with Poland and begins reading Henderson a very long document in German, extremely fast. Henderson assumes he will be given the document afterward, so he does not listen very closely. When Ribbentrop finishes, Henderson asks for the terms. Ribbentrop refuses: it is too late; the Polish plenipotentiary should have arrived the day before.

Could he not give them to the Polish ambassador in Berlin? Never; Germany will never summon him. And if the ambassador himself asks to be received? In that case, Ribbentrop says, perhaps they could see. Henderson reports that the conversation was extremely unpleasant and that throughout the evening Ribbentrop had been “aping Herr Hitler at his worst.”

## 31 August–1 September: the order and the false flag

On 31 August the British tell the Poles: send a plenipotentiary to Berlin, or at least send your ambassador to knock on the door and obtain the German terms. The Poles answer that they will not send the ambassador; it would be too humiliating.

At 12:40 p.m. Hitler signs the directive: attack Poland at 4:45 the next morning. At one o’clock the Poles reconsider. Without telling the British, they order Ambassador Lipski to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms of negotiation. The meeting finally occurs at 6:30 p.m., after the war order.

Ribbentrop asks: “Do you have authority to negotiate?” Lipski answers no. Then there is nothing to discuss, and he is sent away.

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. After everything that has happened, one might not believe the Germans still need a pretext for war with Poland. They do, and the German security services under Reinhard Heydrich have prepared one. Heydrich is a senior SS figure and chief of the security services; Czechoslovak agents will later kill him in Prague.

For two weeks a unit has been ready near the German radio station at Gleiwitz, close to the Polish frontier, equipped with Polish uniforms and documents. On the morning of 31 August Heydrich sends the code word: “Grandmother died.”

The unit goes into action on German territory. They attack the station, fire shots, seize the radio, and broadcast a raving statement in Polish. They leave behind a dead man dressed as a Pole. There has now supposedly been a Polish attack on German territory. Germany can claim the right to invade Poland, and does so a few hours later. Elsewhere, men in Polish uniforms stage related incidents. Gleiwitz is one part of the German false flag.

At dawn Germany invades Poland.

Meanwhile, during the night, Halifax still hopes the Germans are sincere and that a real negotiation with Poland can begin. He is worried because the Poles will not send their ambassador; he does not know that Lipski has already gone and been dismissed.

Halifax cables the British ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw. He cannot see why the Polish government is making difficulties: its ambassador should accept the German document, and refusal would be gravely misunderstood by public opinion. His Majesty’s Government is ready to do everything to assist mediation. He tells the ambassador in Warsaw to insist that the Polish ambassador go to Ribbentrop immediately to save the peace.

The telegram is deciphered in Warsaw at four in the morning. Kennard answers: “M. Lipski had already called on the German Foreign Minister at 6:30 p.m. yesterday. In view of this fact, which was followed by German invasion of Poland at dawn to-day, it was clearly useless for me to take the action suggested.” The instruction has been overtaken by invasion.

## War

Britain and France do not declare war at the first gunshot. Ultimatums and one last sequence of diplomatic exchanges fill the next two days. On 3 September Britain’s ultimatum expires at eleven in the morning; France’s at five in the afternoon. Both are at war with Germany.

For two more days there remains the desperate, naïve, good-faith hope that the catastrophe can still be avoided. At eleven on Sunday morning the British interval ends. At five the French interval ends.

Italy chooses “non-belligerency.” Mussolini and Ciano had already calculated on 13–15 August that if the democracies fought, Italy must disengage honorably; if they backed down, Italy should seek “our share of the spoils,” specifically in Croatia and Dalmatia and ultimately against Belgrade.

And how does the Italian public learn that war has begun? Let us end with the afternoon edition of *Corriere della Sera*, 1 September 1939. Across the page:

“THE DECISIVE HOUR STRIKES.”

“Hitler’s proposals for Danzig and the Corridor—loyal, reasonable, and eminently feasible—foolishly allowed to lapse by Warsaw and London.”

“England and company nailed to their tremendous responsibilities.”

And beneath it: “The publication of this page of history confirms Germany’s conscious wisdom and moderation in the face of the blind intransigence of the other parties to the conflict.”

These are the newspapers our fathers read in our happy country in 1939.
