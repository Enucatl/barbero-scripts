# How Wars Break Out: The Second World War

## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

<!-- transcript: U-00001–U-00103; omissions: none -->

Well, good evening. We have a pretty rough journey ahead of us tonight. You might say the previous episode was no picnic either, but the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story. The only lighter moments come from Italy’s part in it. Whatever you may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and the rest, they provide the only comedy in those otherwise dramatic weeks, as you’ll see.

So how does the Second World War break out? Some have argued that it really begins in 1919, with the signing of the Treaty of Versailles. That treaty ends the First World War, but in doing so it creates endless resentment and frustration. Remember, even Italy isn’t satisfied, though it has gained Trento and Trieste and swallowed up South Tyrol. Instead, Italians talk about a “mutilated victory”: “Our victory, you will not be mutilated. No one can break your knees or clip your wings. Where are you running? Where are you rising?” [Q-001] Even Italy, one of the victors, then sinks into something close to civil war and into dictatorship, all because of a war that has ended badly. Just imagine how the defeated countries feel. [C-001]

Germany has lost, and it emerges from the First World War and the Treaty of Versailles frustrated, convinced it has been treated unjustly. What injustice? For one thing, Germany has been forced to give territory to a new country. The Treaty of Versailles creates many new states, including Poland. Germany gives Poland—or rather, is forced to give Poland—a large stretch of land inhabited by Germans. And on top of that, the treaty creates situations that are impossible to manage.

There’s one city that nobody cares much about today: Gdańsk, in Poland. We’ve barely heard about it since the days of Lech Wałęsa, but back then everyone is constantly talking about it. Danzig is a major port, an important German city on the border between the new Poland and Germany. Both countries claim it. The victors at Versailles decide: all right, we’ll make it a Free City—independent, neither German nor Polish. Danzig is populated entirely by Germans who want to be part of Germany, but they aren’t allowed to be.

A public campaign begins in Germany almost immediately, somewhat like the one in Italy before the war: Trento, Trieste, irredentism, our separated brothers. Germans rally around the cause: Danzig—Danzig is German. Danzig is German, and they didn’t give it to us. It’s a disgrace. One day we’ll have to revisit the issue. [Q-002]

Then there are the other problems created by the collapse of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The empire is broken up, and a number of new states emerge: Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia. They bring plenty of problems with them. Austria is small and populated entirely by Germans. Most Austrians would like to say: all right, since this is how things have turned out, let’s join Germany. We’re Germans too. But the victors won’t allow it. Austria has to remain independent.

Then there’s Czechoslovakia, the country of the Czechs and Slovaks. But under the Austrian Empire, large numbers of Germans had lived in those regions—Austrian citizens who spoke German. Now they find themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, but they don’t feel Czechoslovak. So Versailles creates this entire set of problems, and the problems themselves are understandable. They mostly involve minorities living outside their own countries.

Tonight I need to spend a little time on the deeper causes of the Second World War. I would have liked to start by saying: all right, it’s August 10, 1939, and here’s what happens. We’ll get there. But first we need to talk about those deeper causes, or none of what follows will make sense. And they’re exactly what I’ve just described: Germany emerges frustrated, with claims against Poland over Danzig; against Austria, because Austrians are all Germans, so why shouldn’t they be part of Germany? And against Czechoslovakia, because the Germans living there feel discriminated against.

In 1933, the Nazis win the elections in Germany, and Hitler comes to power. His program includes all sorts of wonderful things, but this may be the point Germans feel most strongly about: we have to revise the Treaty of Versailles. We have to remake the Europe Versailles created. It’s an unjust Europe, built on an unfair treaty. Germany has suffered, and that isn’t right. We have to revisit the settlement and change the borders. [C-002]

At its core, the story of how the Second World War breaks out is the story of two illusions. The first belongs to the great democratic powers, Britain and France. They believe Hitler can be given some of what he wants because, on certain issues, he does have a point. They think they can say: all right, let’s negotiate. Let’s reach an agreement—Danzig goes here, and so on—without causing any major upheaval. It’s an illusion, as we’ll see.

The other illusion is Hitler’s. He believes he can take everything back, and then take a little more, and once again turn Germany into Europe’s great power without anyone putting up serious resistance. So there’s Hitler’s illusion, and there’s the illusion of the Western democracies—France and Britain. [C-003]

## 2. Halifax, anti-Bolshevism, and Hitler’s reading of the climate

<!-- transcript: U-00104–U-00181; omissions: none -->

Then comes a moment when their self-deception becomes impossible to miss. In the end, I decided to begin tonight’s story on this date: November 19, 1937. The Second World War is less than two years away. We know that. They don’t.

On November 19, 1937, the British foreign secretary, Lord Halifax, goes to Berlin and meets with Hitler. There, Halifax expresses sympathy for Germany’s claims. By this point, a generation in Britain is saying: yes, we went too far at Versailles. We imposed unfair terms on Germany. You can understand why the Germans want them revised.

Halifax tells Hitler that Britain understands Germany’s claims against Poland, Austria, and Czechoslovakia. Changes may be possible in time, he says, as long as they happen peacefully and don’t cause major upheaval. In his own words: “I said that there were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today, but we were concerned to avoid such treatment of them as would be likely to cause trouble. If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.” [Q-003]

Lord Halifax tells Hitler something else too: Germany is Europe’s bulwark against Bolshevism. [Q-004]

To understand how people think in those years, we can’t forget one other factor. To the east of Europe stands an unspoken presence, one everyone pretends doesn’t exist and mentions as little as possible: the Soviet Union. By 1937, Stalin is in power there. We know he has put world revolution away in a drawer, but they don’t.

To Western politicians, the Soviet Union is still the country not just of communists, but of Bolsheviks—extremely dangerous revolutionaries. It’s a country to keep at arm’s length. So the Germany of Herr Hitler, as British diplomats call him in their reports—they always call him that, Herr Hitler—is useful, all things considered. It is fiercely anti-communist, and it has thrown German communists into concentration camps. [C-004]

That’s true in Britain, and it’s true in France. Unlike Britain, France has a strong Communist Party and a strong Socialist Party, and the left-wing Popular Front is in government. We’ve forgotten it today, but at the time the Popular Front inspires—forget Hollande—enormous hopes and enormous fears in France. The country also has a ruling class of industrialists, generals, and clergy. Faced with a communist government, they say: well, the Nazis are better. Much better. Better Hitler than Stalin. “Nothing about the conduct of this fraction of the French bourgeoisie can be understood unless one hears it murmuring under its breath: ‘Hitler rather than Blum.’” [Q-005]

So Hitler senses that the climate is favorable. Certain things are now possible. He just has to wait for the opportunity. Historians have debated whether Hitler planned everything from the start, whether he already had detailed plans that led all the way to invading Poland. You know Woody Allen? “I can't listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” [Q-006] But the invasion of Poland is only the final act. Had Hitler already decided everything from the beginning? Perhaps not.

In the 1960s, the English historian A. J. P. Taylor publishes a famous book. His argument is that, criminal though Hitler is, his foreign-policy goals are fairly conventional. Everyone wants their country to be great and powerful. Hitler does too, and he waits for the right opportunities, sniffing the air. The wind is blowing his way. “There was here no concrete plan, no directive for German policy in 1937 and 1938. Or if there were a directive, it was to wait upon events. [...] Hitler did not make plans—for world conquest or for anything else. He assumed that others would provide opportunities, and that he would seize them. [...] His foreign policy was a different matter. He aimed to make Germany the dominant Power in Europe and maybe, more remotely, in the world. Other Powers have pursued similar aims, and still do.” [Q-007]

But Hitler doesn’t just wait for opportunities. He creates them too. Taylor doesn’t know much about that; documents that surface later make it clear. Hitler creates his opportunities. It starts with Austria. Austria is a German country. [C-005]

## 3. Anschluss: engineered crisis and non-response

<!-- transcript: U-00182–U-00211; omissions: none -->

So Austria has a Nazi Party that wants the country annexed by Germany and takes its orders from Hitler. Hitler gives the Austrian Nazis the go-ahead: stir up unrest. Carry out attacks, terrorism, political murders. Create disorder. [C-006]

And then it’s March 1938, four months after Lord Halifax visits Berlin and tells Hitler: well, your position is understandable. Just four months. In Vienna, the Austrian Nazi Party stages a coup and invites German troops into Austria to guarantee security. On March 12, 1938, German troops enter the country. This is the Anschluss, as it’s called: the annexation.

The German army is so unprepared for war at this point that, by Guderian’s later account, roughly thirty percent of its tanks break down before reaching Vienna. [N-006] [C-007] Yet no one abroad reacts.

In Austria, there’s widespread public enthusiasm. A plebiscite approves annexation to Nazi Germany with ninety-nine percent of the vote. Vienna’s cardinal, Innitzer, declares that Austrian Catholics must thank the Lord and obey the new government, which has freed them from the communist threat: “We are also convinced that the work of the National Socialist movement has warded off the danger of utterly destructive, godless Bolshevism. The bishops accompany this work into the future with their best blessings and will also exhort the faithful in this spirit. On the day of the plebiscite it is for us bishops a self-evident national duty, as Germans, to declare ourselves for the German Reich, and we also expect all faithful Christians to know what they owe their people.” [Q-008] And no one abroad bats an eye. [C-008]

In Britain, the London *Times* writes: this is a little like what we did two centuries ago, when Scotland joined England. These things happen. It’s what the people want. Austria, it writes, “she was destined sooner or later to find herself in close association with the German Reich.” [Q-009]

Hitler takes note: Austria annexed, and nobody reacts. Only a few days pass—really, just a few days. It’s still March 1938 when—

## 4. Sudeten crisis and the exclusion of the Soviet Union

<!-- transcript: U-00212–U-00256; omissions: none -->

Hitler summons the leader of the Czechoslovak Nazis. Czechoslovakia also has a Nazi Party, drawn from the German minority in the Sudetenland. That’s what they’re called—I can never remember why—but the Germans in Czechoslovakia are known as the Sudeten Germans.

This Nazi Party among Czechoslovakia’s Germans takes its orders from Hitler. In March 1938, Hitler gives the signal: start the street violence, the agitation, the terrorism. Czechoslovakia begins to sink into chaos. [C-009]

The German press launches a relentless campaign. Germans in Czechoslovakia are being oppressed, it says. They’re a persecuted minority. The criminal Czechoslovak police arrest them, rape them, torture them. Germans in Czechoslovakia are suffering. This cannot go on. [Q-010]

Acting on instructions from their government, the British ambassadors in Berlin and Prague say: well, the Germans aren’t entirely wrong. Their position is understandable. They keep saying the German claims have some basis and that the Czechoslovak government needs to meet the Germans partway. We certainly don’t want to start another war, do we? So the Czechoslovak government had better make concessions. [C-010]

As the Nazis in Czechoslovakia start playing the same game they played in Austria, only one country takes a public stand. Only one calls on all the powers to come together and guarantee Czechoslovakia: the Soviet Union.

And of course no one listens, because the Soviet Union is the Bolshevik country, the communist country. It’s out of the game. During those weeks, the British ambassador in Moscow sends a report to London. He concludes, and I quote: “The Soviet Union must […] for the time being, be counted out of European politics in so far as the exercise of a decisive influence one way or the other is concerned.” [Q-011] They can treat Russia as if it doesn’t exist. [C-011]

Notice again how hard it is for them to grasp the way the world is changing. They keep talking about “European politics” as if Britain and France still make all the decisions. The United States isn’t there. Japan isn’t there. The Soviet Union isn’t there. Nothing. It’s still “European politics,” and Russia doesn’t count.

This goes on for months. Czechoslovakia is in chaos, and the German press keeps pounding away, pounding away, pounding away at the suffering of the Sudeten Germans. Meanwhile, Britain and France pressure the government in Prague: come on, make some concessions.

## 5. Munich, peace at any price, and appeasement’s failure

<!-- transcript: U-00257–U-00358; omissions: none -->

Finally, we reach September. The situation is now so serious that the British and French governments decide they have to force the Czechoslovaks to make concessions to Hitler. They need to come up with something. How about a conference? Today we’d call it bringing everyone to the negotiating table. Back then, they haven’t invented the “table” yet—or the “summit.” Let’s hold a conference, they say.

How do they persuade Hitler to negotiate? Ask Mussolini, since the two men are friends. The British and French approach Mussolini and tell him, in essence—I’m summarizing here—to sound Hitler out. See if he’ll agree to bring everyone together and work out a deal that meets Germany’s demands on Czechoslovakia. Hitler agrees.

On September 29 and 30, 1938, we come to an episode that will go down as one of the low points of European politics, though at least two of the four people involved are acting in good faith: the Munich Conference. Four leaders meet in Munich, in Bavaria: Hitler, Mussolini, the British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, and the French prime minister, Daladier. [C-012]

At least Chamberlain and Daladier are acting in good faith. It’s hard to doubt that. They’re haunted by the same nightmare as the entire generation that lived through the First World War. Whenever a crisis erupts, the politicians of this era—the generation that witnessed that slaughter—have only one thought: anything but another war. Never again, no matter the price. Or, if you prefer: “our policy was the policy of peace at any price.” [Q-012] [C-013]

What does “any price” mean? Well, if Czechoslovakia has to give Germany a piece of its territory, so be it. That’s better than war. At the Munich Conference, Britain, France, Italy, and Germany meet to decide which part of Czechoslovakia Germany will get. The Czechoslovaks wait outside the door to find out what the others have decided. And the decision is this: the entire German-populated region, the Sudetenland, goes to Germany.

Hitler takes note. Not only do they stay out of my way; they actually help me take a piece of Czechoslovakia.

And here—I don’t know. It’s hard to talk about, because part of you wants to say what people said for fifty years: what a tragedy, to have politicians of that caliber. And yet these are first-rate politicians. They simply don’t understand what’s happening, and they’re acting in good faith.

Chamberlain returns to London, and people have mocked this homecoming from Munich ever since. As he comes down the airplane steps, he’s already waving the piece of paper with the agreements on it. Picture the scene: the photographers, the crowd, everyone gathered around. Chamberlain waves the paper and addresses them.

Here’s Chamberlain’s speech to the crowd that day, as he steps off the plane from Munich: “The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is, in my view, only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace. [...] We regard the agreement signed last night and the Anglo-German naval agreement as symbolic of the desire of our two peoples never to go to war with one another again. [...] My good friends, this is the second time in our history that there has come back from Germany to Downing Street peace with honour. I believe it is peace for our time. [...] And now I recommend you to go home and sleep quietly in your beds.” Remember, the Second World War is only eleven months away. That’s what Chamberlain tells the London crowd when he returns from Munich. [Q-013]

The Second World War is eleven months away. Keep that in mind. Chamberlain says he met again that morning with the German chancellor, Herr Hitler, and here is the treaty with both their signatures. They regard the agreement signed the night before as a symbol of their peoples’ desire never to go to war with each other again. My good friends, the British prime minister has come home from Germany with peace and honor. He believes it is peace for their time. Go home and sleep soundly. That’s his message to the London crowd when he returns from Munich.

Chamberlain’s policy has a specific name. It later becomes an ugly word in British politics—we’ll hear it again in the next episode, when we talk about Mrs. Thatcher: *appeasement*. Pacification. Peace at any price.

And today, *appeasement* really is shocking. They don’t understand. No, they simply don’t understand. The tragic irony is that they’re willing to do anything to keep war from breaking out. And the result is that they cause it to break out. [C-014]

Mind you, Hitler is good at this. He’s extraordinarily skilled at running people in circles and deceiving them. At Munich, Hitler tells Chamberlain—and listen to this; Chamberlain himself reports it, apparently. Chamberlain says: I spoke with Hitler, and Hitler told me with great sincerity: “In the first place he repeated to me with great earnestness what he had said already at Berchtesgaden, namely, that this was the last of his territorial ambitions in Europe and that he had no wish to include in the Reich people of other races than Germans.” [Q-014]

Hitler said it, and he sounds so sincere. So go ahead: sleep peacefully.

## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

<!-- transcript: U-00359–U-00443; omissions: none -->

But Chamberlain and Daladier aren’t the only ones who fall for it. Mussolini does too. And so does Poland. Remember, Poland will become the victim once the war breaks out. But when Czechoslovakia is carved up under the Munich settlement, Poland takes advantage of the situation and grabs a small piece for itself. Poland is a militaristic country run by colonels, a kind of half-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and intensely anti-communist. At this point, the Poles are convinced they can be friends with Hitler because they’re all anti-communists. Who could be more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles could—even more so. So in late 1938 and early 1939, the Poles believe they’re Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

And Hitler? On January 30, 1939, with seven months to go before the world war breaks out, he tells the Reichstag, the German parliament: “Even during the troubled months of the past year, German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life.” [Q-015]

At the end of January 1939, Polish foreign minister Józef Beck meets Ribbentrop in Warsaw—not Hitler in Berlin, as I just made it sound. According to Ribbentrop: “M. Beck made no secret of the fact that Poland had aspirations directed toward the Soviet Ukraine and a connection with the Black Sea; but at the same time he called attention to the supposed dangers to Poland that in the Polish view would arise from a treaty with Germany directed against the Soviet Union.” [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, while all these lovely conversations are going on in January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia is falling apart. It has lost the entire German-speaking border region, most of its industry, and its border defenses. Now Slovakia wants independence. You know the story: it eventually gets it, and today Slovakia is an independent country. Czechoslovakia is collapsing, and Hungary is about to invade. Hitler decides it’s time to get rid of the problem. [C-016]

On March 14, 1939, Hitler orders the Czechoslovak president, Emil Hácha, to come meet with him in Berlin. Hácha obeys. Shortly after one in the morning on March 15, Hitler tells him that German troops will move in at six and crush any resistance. It’s Göring, not Hitler, who threatens to bomb Prague. Hácha collapses and is given medical treatment and injections, though he isn’t diagnosed with a heart attack, and the doctors don’t deliberately keep him awake. He signs a declaration. It isn’t literally a request for German intervention. It reads: “The President of the Czecho-Slovak State declared that, in order to serve this end and to reach a final pacification, he confidently placed the fate of the Czech people and of their country in the hands of the Fuehrer of the German Reich.” [Q-017] German forces occupy Bohemia and Moravia on March 15, after Slovakia declares independence under German pressure on March 14. [N-014] [C-017]

On March 15, the Germans invade Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, no one has stepped in. [C-017]

And you’ll say: well, it’s about time. Chamberlain is starting to think that maybe Hitler can’t be trusted after all. Two days later, on March 17, 1939, Chamberlain gives a speech in Birmingham. [N-015] I’ll translate it for you. He’s clearly saying things he would much rather not say. You can hear it in the way he builds his sentences. Chamberlain says:

“Does not the question inevitably arise in our minds, if it is so easy to discover good reasons for ignoring assurances so solemnly and so repeatedly given, what reliance can be placed upon any other assurances that come from the same source? [...] Is this the end of an old adventure, or is it the beginning of a new? Is this the last attack upon a small State, or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” [Q-018]

Chamberlain is clearly hoping someone will tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt is starting to creep in. And the proof comes right away. The Germans invade Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on March 15. [C-018]

## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Ten days later, on March 26, Hitler receives the Polish government’s formal rejection of Germany’s demand to bring the Free City of Danzig into the Reich. Germany has already been pressuring Poland over Danzig for months. At the end of April 1939, Hitler speaks to the Reichstag. Three months earlier, standing in that very same place, he had called German-Polish friendship one of the reassuring features of European political life. Now, three months later, he publicly makes the case for Germany’s broader territorial demands and addresses Poland’s access to the sea: “This is, as I have emphasized before, perhaps the most difficult of all Germany’s problems. [...] I saw the peaceful resolution of this question as a further contribution to a lasting relaxation of tensions in Europe. [...] The Polish government rejected this offer of mine. [...] I sincerely regretted this attitude of the Polish government, which I could not understand.” [Q-019] [C-019]

From that point on, tensions rise in Danzig and along its border with Poland. There are clashes, shootings, incidents every day. Nazi militias form inside Danzig, with a Polish customs post just outside. But this time, the British and French governments are starting to think that maybe they should do something. But what?

Britain and France propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain goes even further and gives Poland an official guarantee. Even though the treaty hasn’t been signed yet, Britain promises all the support it can provide if an action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland decides it has to resist. France takes the same position. Looking back now, you might say: well, it’s about time. What else could they do? [C-020]

At the time, though, selling this to the public is extremely difficult. Danzig? We’re supposed to go to war over Danzig? The French newspaper *L’Œuvre* runs a famous headline: “Morire per Danzica?”—“Die for Danzig?” No. The slogan doesn’t speak for everyone in Britain and France, but it becomes influential. Still, now that the French and British governments have discovered Hitler might not be trustworthy, they press ahead. And in April and May 1939, they do something else unheard-of. [Q-020] [C-020]

They start looking for some kind of agreement with the Soviet Union. The negotiations go on for months as they try to work out a military-defense pact in case war breaks out. It’s a sad, depressing story, because you can’t help thinking: if only they had reached that agreement. But no one seems to want it badly enough. They’re all afraid of one another, and there’s mistrust and obstruction on every side. Still, the telegrams and replies reveal a striking pattern. Again and again, Britain and France make a proposal, and the Russians answer the next day. Then weeks go by—week after week—before the British and French finally take the next step. The Russians answer the next day, and the negotiations keep dragging on. [C-021]

Meanwhile, Stalin is taking notes. They’re asking me to negotiate, but do they mean it? Or are they trying to pull me in, only to leave me alone with Germany when the decisive moment comes? Stalin decides the risk is too great. He’s negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French don’t seem very committed.

Stalin lets the Germans know that even though their countries are enemies—one is communist, the other is, well...—they do have business interests in common. Maybe it would be a good idea to start negotiating a trade agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The Germans are interested. And so, over the summer, Stalin quietly works both sides, negotiating with Britain and France at one table while exploring an agreement with Germany at another. The German track starts with uneven economic contacts, develops into a political channel later, and accelerates sharply in August. Stalin waits to see what happens. [C-021]

## 8. Italy’s alliance without the means for war

<!-- transcript: U-00522–U-00560; omissions: none -->

And then there’s Italy. Italy and Nazi Germany are the best of friends. On May 22, 1939, they sign the Pact of Steel, and the press is ecstatic. Hitler sends Victor Emmanuel III a telegram: “In this historic hour I wish to express to Your Majesty my profound satisfaction that our two peoples are bound to one another by an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.” [Q-021]

But Mussolini is worried. Ribbentrop has repeatedly assured the Italians that Germany doesn’t want a war anytime soon. Those assurances are only verbal—there’s nothing about that in the treaty—and Mussolini is starting to have doubts. [C-023]

And the first thing we need to understand—this isn’t always made clear, even though it’s well documented—is why Mussolini has those doubts. At this point, Italy is desperately short of resources. It has fought the Ethiopian War and intervened in the Spanish Civil War on Franco’s side. Everything has gone well. The Ethiopian War was a triumph, and Spain went pretty well too. But wars cost staggering amounts of money. The wars in Ethiopia and Spain have blown a hole in Italy’s budget and drained its reserves of military equipment and weapons. Italy is running short of everything, especially modern weapons. [C-022]

And over the past few years, every country has seen which way the wind is blowing. They’re all testing new weapons and putting a new generation of fighter planes into production—planes like the Spitfire—along with new tanks. Weapons that were perfectly adequate just two or three years ago are quickly becoming obsolete. Italy doesn’t have the industrial capacity or the resources to modernize fast enough. Mussolini knows this, even if he sometimes prefers to forget it. But in the summer of 1939, he knows it. And he has one great fear: that the Germans will decide to start a war. [C-022]

In early August, Ciano writes in his diary. You know Galeazzo Ciano: the foreign minister, Mussolini’s son-in-law. Five years later, he’ll be shot after the Verona trial, but that’s another story. So in early August, Ciano, our foreign minister, makes a note in his diary.

## 9. Ciano learns Germany wants war

<!-- transcript: U-00561–U-00595; omissions: none -->

In his diary on August 4, Ciano writes: “I am beginning to consider the advisability of meeting Ribbentrop. The time has come to find out what the situation really is. The stakes are too high for us to wait passively for developments.” [Q-022]

So Ciano flies to Germany. On August 11, he meets his counterpart, German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop. Ciano has been sent to tell him that starting a war now would be insane. They absolutely cannot do it. Ciano later recalls asking Ribbentrop as the two of them walk through the garden: “‘Well, Ribbentrop,’ I asked him as we walked side by side in the garden, ‘what do you want? The Corridor or Danzig?’ ‘Not anymore,’ and he fixed me with those cold Musée Grévin eyes of his, ‘we want war.’” [Q-023]

They’re at a reception, all standing around with champagne glasses in their hands. Ciano is dying to tell his staff what Ribbentrop has just said, but they’re stuck there at the reception. Ciano writes all of this down later. Finally, he gets close enough to one of his aides to whisper in his ear: “We’re headed for a fight.”

Ciano keeps talking with Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop tells him the decision has been made: Germany will go to war with Poland and crush it. And Britain? France? Ribbentrop is sure they won’t intervene. It’s the same gamble we saw in 1914, in the previous episode: they won’t intervene. Except this time—you know how history repeats itself, first as tragedy and then as farce?—they literally place a bet on it.

Ciano later writes: “They were making a fundamental error of calculation: they were certain that France and England would stand idly by while Poland was slaughtered. On this subject Ribbentrop wanted, during one of those gloomy meals we took at the Oesterreichischer Hof in Salzburg, to make a wager with me. I was to give him an Italian painting in the event of Anglo-French neutrality; he was to give me a collection of antique weapons in the event of intervention. Many witnesses were present, and not very long ago we were speaking about it with Ambassador von Mackensen. Ribbentrop, however, preferred to forget that wager and never paid his debt.” [Q-024] That’s the idea: Germany can go to war without setting off a world war because France and Britain won’t dare step in. [C-024]

The gala dinner ends, then the reception, the toasts, all of it. Ciano goes back to the hotel with his aides, dying to tell them everything. Then he wonders whether the Germans might have planted microphones in their room. So they lock themselves in the bathroom, and Ciano tells them there: the Germans have decided to go to war. And that’s when the countdown begins: August 12.

## 10. 12–18 August: rival negotiations and Polish refusal

<!-- transcript: U-00596–U-00727; omissions: none -->

The day after Ciano’s meeting with Ribbentrop is Saturday, August 12. It’s Ferragosto, the summer holiday season. This war, like the First World War, will break out right in the middle of summer. People don’t take as much vacation time as they do today, but they do take some, and at Ferragosto they leave town. That Saturday, for example, Milan empties out. Half a million people take the train to the coast.

That same day, after months of talk, an Anglo-French delegation finally arrives in Moscow to negotiate a treaty. The Soviets are bitterly disappointed. The delegation consists of a French general, Joseph Doumenc, and a British admiral, Reginald Drax. Neither is a top-ranking figure. The Russians ask them, “Do you have full powers to sign a treaty?”

Let me stop for a second, because these “full powers” are crucial to understanding diplomacy in this period. Of course they have telephones, telegraphs, and airplanes. But everyone still considers it essential to have someone there in person who can sign. So the Soviets say: You’ve come to negotiate, excellent. This is urgent. Do you have full powers? Drax can negotiate but can’t sign. Daladier has authorized Doumenc to conclude a military convention, but Doumenc doesn’t have the usual formal credentials granting him full powers, and he still depends on Paris. So the practical answer is: “You tell us, and we will report back.” The Soviets are deeply disappointed. [C-025]

That same day, the Soviet government sends an invitation: Ribbentrop should come to Moscow. They have things to discuss. Then comes Sunday, August 13. It’s high summer, vacation season. The Venice Film Festival is underway, and Maria Bellonci wins the Viareggio Prize for *Lucrezia Borgia*.

That same day, Hitler speaks with Mussolini. It’s exactly what Ciano said: Germany is going to war with Poland. At the first incident, it will attack. And that’s not all. Hitler explains his timetable. It’s mid-August, and his generals estimate that defeating Poland will take a month and a half—six weeks at most. By mid-October, fall will set in, turning Poland’s roads and airfields into unusable mud. So the campaign has to be over by then. If they need six weeks, they have to attack by the end of August. [C-026]

Mussolini and the others in Italy are stunned. In his diary on August 13, Ciano writes: “I return to Rome disgusted with Germany, its leaders, and their way of acting. They have deceived us and lied to us. And today they are about to drag us into an adventure that we did not want and that could imperil the regime and the country.” [Q-025]

Monday, August 14. The vacation mood continues, and the Salzburg Festival is underway. Hitler is in the mountains and goes to see Mozart’s *The Abduction from the Seraglio* at the festival.

In Moscow, the Russians meet with the Anglo-French delegation and put their cards on the table. You want an agreement to defend Poland? Fine. But the Soviets want proof that everyone is serious. If war breaks out, Poland has to let Soviet troops onto its territory. Are the Soviets acting in good faith or bad faith? Are they already thinking about swallowing up Poland? Or do they honestly believe that if they’re going to fight a war, they need to fight it seriously? We don’t know. Either way, that’s their demand. And what do the British admiral and the French general say? “That is not within our authority. We must report back. We will report to Paris and London, and then we shall see.” The immediate deadlock is over the passage of Soviet troops, but that isn’t the only reason the talks fail. Anglo-French delays and limited mandates matter too, along with Poland’s fear of Soviet occupation, Soviet bargaining, and the possibility of a German deal. [C-027]

That same Monday, August 14, Ribbentrop decides the Russians have a good idea: he should go to Moscow. He’ll leave on Tuesday, August 15—Ferragosto. Meanwhile, the French general has written that the Russians won’t sign without a guarantee that Poland will let them in if war breaks out. So now they have to ask the Poles.

French foreign minister Georges Bonnet goes looking for the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz. And where do you suppose the Polish ambassador is on Ferragosto? At the beach. They bring him back to Paris and tell him: let the Russians pass through Poland if there’s a war with Germany. The ambassador says he’ll have to ask Warsaw, but he expects the answer to be no. The Poles are even more afraid of the Russians than they are of the Germans.

Bonnet warns the Polish ambassador that, according to French information, Hitler has said he’ll wipe out Poland in three weeks. The ambassador’s answer tells you a lot about the mood at the time and the nature of the Polish regime. Bonnet later recalls Łukasiewicz saying: “On the contrary, it is the Polish army that will invade Germany from the very first days.” [Q-026]

Wednesday, August 16. Ribbentrop writes to Moscow: “I am coming.” For the first time, they’ve put it in writing: he’s going to Moscow to sign a treaty with the Soviets. Meanwhile, the Germans in Berlin are starting to worry about one thing. As we saw in the previous episode, history sometimes repeats itself. The Germans are asking: what will Britain do?

On the evening of August 15, British ambassador Sir Nevile Henderson visits State Secretary Ernst von Weizsäcker at the German Foreign Ministry. The German argument goes like this: surely Britain isn’t going to support Poland? Look at these crazy Poles and all their provocations. The German minority in Poland is being oppressed. It’s all the fault of those unreasonable Poles. The British aren’t really going to help a bunch of madmen, are they?

Henderson answers: “I told Baron von Weizsäcker that the last was a very dangerous theory and sounded like Herr von Ribbentrop who had never been able to understand the British mentality. If the Poles were compelled by any act of Germany to resort to arms to defend themselves there was not a shadow of doubt that we would give them our full armed support. We had made that abundantly clear and Germany would be making a tragic mistake if she imagined the contrary.” [Q-027] [C-028]

Thursday, August 17. Incidents multiply along the German-Polish border: shootings, illegal border crossings. The German press launches a campaign—remember Czechoslovakia the year before?—about “Polish terror against the German minority”: arrests, murders. In Moscow, Ribbentrop’s answer arrives. He’s ready to come and sign an agreement.

Friday, August 18. As you know, Mussolini and Ciano are terrified because Italy isn’t ready for war. But the official line handed down to the Italian press is obviously very different. Italy has signed the Pact of Steel, and the newspapers have clear orders: in this crisis, Germany is right.

So let me read you the front-page display from the August 18 afternoon edition of the *Corriere della Sera*:

“POLAND AT THE DECISIVE TURNING POINT OF ITS HISTORY / The tragic responsibility of the democracies / which unleashed and sustained Polish arrogance / Unshakable firmness of the Axis against the warmongering cloaked in hypocrisy by the encirclers” [Q-028]

Democracy, of course, is a dirty word in Fascist Italy. And the “encirclers” are the so-called “demo-pluto-Jewish-ocracies”: France, Britain, and the Soviet Union, supposedly laying siege to other peoples. Well, you know. We know this story in Italy.

That same Friday, August 18, the Polish government has to decide whether to form an alliance with the Soviet Union and let Soviet troops pass through Poland. The government says no: “We have no military agreements with the Soviet Union and do not wish to have any.”

Around August 19 or 20, a famous remark that later becomes proverbial is attributed to Poland’s commander-in-chief, Marshal Edward Śmigły-Rydz: “With the Germans we may lose our freedom, with the Russians we shall lose our soul.” [Q-029] And so there’s no agreement. [C-027]

## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, August 19, word reaches Moscow that the Poles don't want a deal with Russia, so the negotiations grind to a halt. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: all right, send Ribbentrop. How about the 26th or 27th?

Sunday, August 20: Hitler sends Stalin a telegram. Keep in mind, this is the first time in history that a Western head of government has written personally to the head of the Soviet government. Until now, the Soviet Union has been treated like a country with the plague. People deal with it only indirectly, under the table. And the first Western ruler to send Stalin a telegram is Hitler. On August 20 he tells him: “Hitler: 'I accept the draft of the non-aggression pact that your Foreign Minister, Herr Molotov, delivered but consider it urgently necessary to clarify the questions connected with it as soon as possible. [...] I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23.' Stalin: 'I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23.'” [Q-030]

Monday, August 21. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for some way to prevent the war. Then they think: why not do what we did last year? Hold another nice conference in Munich, get everyone around a table, and work something out. So they call Berlin, ask for Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and pitch this wonderful idea. According to Ciano's diary, Ribbentrop is nowhere to be found. He finally calls back that afternoon and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That same day, August 21, Stalin gets Hitler's telegram at three in the afternoon and answers at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let's stop here for a moment. This is a crucial decision, one that does a great deal to make everything that follows possible. And it comes as a complete shock. We need to be very clear about that because—and this is my opinion; some of you may disagree—for years we've been told that all totalitarian regimes were alike, that Stalin's Soviet Union and Hitler's Germany were essentially the same. That makes this agreement seem less surprising. But to people at the time, it is an enormous shock. Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union look like the two most bitterly opposed countries on earth. And they are. The pact shows the extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and cold-bloodedness of both the Nazis and Stalin and his inner circle. The world is stunned.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are dragging their feet and don't really want a deal. Fine, then he'll make one with the Nazis, just to spite them. He needs security. And let me add one detail that few people know, perhaps including many of you. It helps explain the situation in those days. Things are going very badly—as Ciano puts it, “we are heading for a fight”—and Stalin decides he needs security at any cost, even if that means making a pact with the devil. They won't honor the pact forever, but they need it right now. The Soviets distrust the West and need to avoid being isolated. Germany is also offering neutrality and territorial gains. [C-029]

So what's this piece of context that no one knows at the time and few people remember even today? At that very moment, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war against Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets, and it borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war is raging there, with entire army corps involved. Some estimates put the total number of dead and wounded at 70,000, though the figures are disputed. The two governments agree to keep the war quiet, so the rest of the world knows nothing about it. Who, after all, is paying attention to the border between Mongolia and Manchuria? But this is a real war. The Japanese move from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. And the Soviets, by the way, win decisively. On the 21st, with the battle still raging, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany. He has to secure at least one border. The fighting shapes his calculation, though it isn't the only reason for his decision. [C-030]

The news becomes public that same day. Mussolini and Ciano are as stunned as everyone else by the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. Stunned, yes, but also impressed: these Germans are good. Ciano writes in his diary: “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke. The European situation has been overturned. Can France and England, which based their entire anti-Axis policy on alliance with the Soviets, still count on the unconditional support of the extremist masses? And will the encirclement system built through the small states still hold now that its Moscow cornerstone has collapsed? In any case, we should not rush our decisions: wait, and if possible remain ready to take our own share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.” [Q-031]

That final thought tells you everything about the regime's language and its moral standards.

On Tuesday, August 22, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. He says the time is right, just as it was in 1914. But here's the interesting part: he isn't talking about whether the armies are ready. He has something else in mind. In this famous speech to his generals on August 22, Hitler says: “My own personality and that of Mussolini. Essentially it depends on me, my existence, because of my political activities. Furthermore the fact that probably no one will ever again have the confidence of the whole German people as I do. There will probably never again be a man in the future with more authority than I have. My existence is therefore a factor of great value. But I can be eliminated at any time by a criminal or an idiot. The second personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive. If something happens to him, Italy's loyalty to the alliance will no longer be certain. [...] The Duce is the man with the strongest nerves in Italy.” [Q-032] Hitler's whole political world revolves around terrorism and assassination. To him, getting killed is simply something that can happen. So he has to start the war while he's still alive—and while Mussolini is still around. Hitler still trusts his old friend. He is fooling himself about Mussolini at the very moment Mussolini has already decided to back out. [C-031]

Wednesday, August 23. Newspapers around the world announce the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The *Corriere della Sera* proclaims: “THE GERMAN-SOVIET AGREEMENT / The Axis's great political success / has broken the murky manoeuvre of the democratic encirclers / A completely new situation, rich in highly important developments - Von Ribbentrop has left by air for Moscow - The enormous worldwide response - Astonishment and dismay in the anti-totalitarian countries - Poland will have to seek its salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany” [Q-033]. “Anti-totalitarian,” of course, is an insult. *We* are totalitarian—Mussolini invented the whole idea of the totalitarian state—while those spineless French and the rest are “anti-totalitarian.”

Poland is starting to give in and wonder whether a deal with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible. But it's too late. On August 23, Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the non-aggression pact, which is announced publicly. He also signs a secret protocol, which of course is not made public. In it, Germany and the Soviet Union divide Polish territory into spheres, effectively setting up the partition of Poland while leaving the final borders unsettled. [C-032]

## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

That day, Hitler meets with the British ambassador. His one remaining concern is keeping Britain out of the war. He tells the ambassador about the atrocities the Poles are supposedly committing against the German minority in Poland. Now, this next detail is one I could have left out, along with many others. But once again, I couldn't quite bring myself to cut too much. I'll have to speed up; I'm taking too long. In any case, the conversation between Hitler and the British ambassador gets downright morbid. Henderson's report says: “He then violently attacked the Poles, talked of 100,000 German refugees from Poland, excesses against Germans, closing of German institutions and Polish systematic persecution of German nationals generally. [...] He had mentioned the castration of Germans. I happened to be aware of one case. The German in question was a sex-maniac, who had been treated as he deserved. Herr Hitler's retort was that there had not been one case but six.” [Q-034]

And on they go. Hitler also tells him that Poland is the last issue. Henderson reports Hitler's words this way: “Among various points mentioned by Herr Hitler were: [...] that he was by nature an artist not a politician, and that once the Polish question was settled he would end his life as an artist and not as a war-monger; [...] that once the Polish question was settled he himself would settle down.” [Q-035] And the ambassador sends that back to London.

Friday, August 25. Hitler orders the attack for the next day. Then a letter arrives from Mussolini, who finally tells him the truth: Italy isn't ready for war. But Mussolini still doesn't have the nerve to tell him the whole truth. He writes: “If Germany attacks Poland and Poland's allies counterattack Germany, I put before you the advisability of my not taking the initiative in military operations, given the present condition of Italian military preparations, which has repeatedly and promptly been made known to you, Führer, and to von Ribbentrop. Our intervention can nevertheless be immediate if Germany immediately gives us the war materiel and raw materials needed to withstand the blow that the French and British will direct mainly against us. In our meetings war had been envisaged for after 1942, and by then I would have been ready on land, at sea, and in the air, according to the agreed plans.” [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He puts off the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what raw materials Italy needs. Mussolini's letter helps trigger the delay. So does the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance, along with Hitler's continued effort to pry Britain away from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, August 26. Mussolini meets at Palazzo Venezia with the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces. They're there to draw up a list of the raw materials Italy will ask Germany for. Ciano has already coached the generals: no criminal optimism when you make this list. In other words, don't hold back. So they make the list, and I'll read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—to give you some idea, that's two years' worth of Italian production; two million tonnes of steel, a full year of Italian production; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rosin, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. On paper, this is what Italy needs to fight for twelve months. The list goes down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to Attolico, the Italian ambassador in Berlin, and he takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks it over and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” Now Attolico is in trouble. No one has told him what to say, and he has no instructions. But he understands what they're trying to do, so he improvises: “Immediately.” Ciano estimates that it would take 17,000 trains to ship all of it to Italy. That's his contemporary estimate, not a modern calculation. [C-034]

Ribbentrop takes the list to Hitler, and at last they get it. The penny drops: Italy isn't coming in. Italy is staying out. Fine, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini: “I have received the information concerning your final attitude. I respect the motives and influences determining your resolution. Perhaps it will nevertheless be for the best. Of course, in my opinion, this will be true only if the world does not receive any inkling of the intended attitude of Italy, at least not till actual fighting starts. [...] You and your people could help me most in this hard struggle by supporting me with Italian manpower, manpower for industrial as well as agricultural purposes.” [Q-038]

And just like that, almost in the background, a much longer story begins to unfold. It shows you how quickly things change. The Germans suddenly realize they can't count on military help from Italy, but they can count on Italian workers. Organized labor migration from Italy to Germany had already begun in 1938. This request expands that flow, which will become increasingly coercive. It is separate from the mass forced labor imposed on Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]

## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, August 27, Germany introduces rationing: food cards. Monday, August 28. Britain and France still think they may be able to persuade the Poles to negotiate. They lean on the Polish government: go ask the Germans exactly what they want, and then let's talk about it.

Tuesday, August 29. Italy bans private cars from the roads and imposes austerity measures on restaurants. A menu can have only one main course: meat or fish, but not both. The British ambassador goes to see Hitler and find out whether, even at this late hour, real negotiations are still possible. Here is how the documents record the exchange: “Henderson: 'Interview this evening was of a stormy character and Herr Hitler far less reasonable than yesterday. [...] He kept saying that he wanted British friendship more than anything in the world, but he could not sacrifice Germany's vital interests therefor.' Henderson's accompanying report: 'German Government, note adds, counts on arrival of such plenipotentiary to-morrow, Wednesday, 30th August. [...] I remarked that this phrase sounded like an ultimatum.' Hitler's written reply: 'The German Government, accordingly, in these circumstances agree to accept the British Government's offer of their good offices in securing the despatch to Berlin of a Polish Emissary with full powers. They count on the arrival of this Emissary on Wednesday, the 30th August, 1939.'” [Q-039]

Wednesday, August 30. Italy suspends the world cycling championships in Varese. Coffee can no longer be sold to private citizens. You can't even have a cup at a café; all of it is reserved for the armed forces. France suspends rail traffic, and the Vincennes Zoo is evacuated to save the animals. Across the capitals of Europe, people start building air-raid shelters and handing out gas masks. Everyone is convinced the war will begin with massive air raids dropping poison gas on cities. Preparations for air raids and gas attacks are widespread, with the exact measures varying from place to place. [C-036]

That same day, Wednesday, August 30, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax writes to the British ambassador in Berlin. This is the same Lord Halifax who, two years earlier, had told Hitler that his demands were basically understandable. Here is the exchange: Halifax: ‘We shall give careful consideration to German Government's reply, but it is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.’ Henderson: ‘I had made similar observation to Herr Hitler yesterday evening, his reply being that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in one and a half hours.’ [Q-040]

Now the British are desperate to prevent war, and they put intense pressure on the Polish government. Don't provoke the Germans. Stop the Polish newspapers from running articles against Germany. End the anti-German campaign. Send someone with full authority to Berlin right away and negotiate. The British ambassador goes back to Ribbentrop and tells him they're doing everything they can. They're urging the Polish government to make peace, to reach a deal. Ribbentrop answers that British advice has had “damned little effect” so far. The ambassador responds—and later tells London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

And now another farce begins, a kind of German black comedy. Ribbentrop says they have drawn up terms for an agreement with Poland. The British ambassador reports: “Herr von Ribbentrop's reply was to produce a lengthy document which he read out in German aloud at top speed. Imagining that he would eventually hand it to me I did not attempt to follow too closely the sixteen or more articles which it contained. [...] When I asked Herr von Ribbentrop for text of these proposals in accordance with undertaking in the German reply of yesterday, he asserted that it was now too late as Polish representative had not arrived in Berlin by midnight. [...] In the most violent terms Herr von Ribbentrop said that he would never ask the Ambassador to visit him. He hinted that if Polish Ambassador asked him for interview it might be different. [...] We parted on that note, but I must tell you that Herr von Ribbentrop's whole demeanour during an unpleasant interview was aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] The deadline is arbitrary. Ribbentrop races through the terms out loud, then refuses to hand over the text. Everything about it is designed to prevent real negotiations. [C-037]

## 14. 31 August–1 September: The Attack Order, Gleiwitz, and Diplomatic Lag

<!-- transcript: U-01015–U-01095; omissions: none -->

On August 31, the British tell the Poles: send someone with full authority to Berlin. Or at least have your ambassador knock on the door and ask for the German terms. The Poles refuse. Sending their ambassador would be too humiliating. At 12:40 that afternoon, Hitler signs the order to attack the next morning. At one, the Poles change their minds. Without telling the British, they instruct their ambassador in Berlin, Józef Lipski, to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms. So Lipski goes. According to Ribbentrop's account, he asks Lipski whether he has authority to negotiate. Lipski says no. Ribbentrop answers that he had expected Lipski to be authorized, and says he will report the approach to Hitler. [Q-043] But Hitler has already given the order to attack by the time the Poles make this last-minute move. [C-038]

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. And here's what makes it so strange: after everything I've told you, you wouldn't think the Germans still needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. But they want one, and the German security services under Heydrich have already prepared it. You know Reinhard Heydrich: an SS big shot, head of the security services, who will be killed four years later by Czechoslovak agents in Prague. He has had a commando waiting for two weeks at Gleiwitz, a German radio station near the Polish border. The group consists of five or six men in civilian clothes. [N-033] On the morning of August 31, the time comes. Heydrich sends them the code phrase: “Grandmother died.” [Q-044]

The commando goes into action on German soil. They seize the Gleiwitz radio station, fire shots, and broadcast a rambling statement in Polish—as we used to say in the days of the Red Brigades. They leave a body behind; exactly who the victim was and what he was wearing remain uncertain. Then the whole episode is presented as a Polish attack on German territory. The Germans claim the right to invade Poland, and a few hours later, at dawn on September 1, they do. [C-039]

Meanwhile, through the night, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax still hopes real negotiations with Poland can begin. He's worried because he thinks the Poles won't send their ambassador. He doesn't know the Polish ambassador has already gone to Ribbentrop and been turned away. During the night leading into September 1, Halifax sends instructions to the ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw. They are separate messages, but together they may be the most pointless telegram a British government has ever sent. Here is the exchange: “Halifax: 'I do not see why the Polish Government should feel difficulty about authorising Polish Ambassador to accept a document from the German Government, and I earnestly hope that they may be able to modify their instructions to him in this respect. [...] On the other hand, a refusal by them to receive proposals would be gravely misunderstood by outside opinion. [...] I should have thought that the Polish Ambassador could surely be instructed to receive and transmit a document [...]. If negotiations are initiated, His Majesty's Government will at all times be ready, if desired, to lend any assistance in their power to achieve a just settlement. [...] Please speak to M. Beck immediately in the above sense.' Kennard: 'Your telegram of 1st September was decyphered at 4 a. m. to-day. M. Lipski had already called on the German Foreign Minister at 6:30 p. m. yesterday. In view of this fact, which was followed by German invasion of Poland at dawn to-day, it was clearly useless for me to take the action suggested.'” [Q-045]

Halifax's instructions are already out of date. Events have overtaken them. [C-040]

## 15. Delayed Declarations and Fascist Propaganda

<!-- transcript: U-01096–U-01117; omissions: none -->

Another two days pass before Britain and France finally declare war on Germany. They still cling to the wild, naïve, but sincere hope that somehow this catastrophe can be avoided. The diplomacy continues, and both countries issue ultimatums. Britain's expires at eleven in the morning on September 3; France's expires at five that afternoon. [C-041]

Meanwhile, back in Italy, Mussolini is calm. Hitler has accepted that Italy is staying out, so the Italians wait to see whether they too can get what Ciano calls a share of the spoils. The Fascist government calls its position non-belligerency, not neutrality. And this is a conditional calculation, not a firm plan to annex territory. Ciano had laid it out in his diary on August 13 and 15: “13 August: 'The Duce's reactions are varied. At first he agrees with me. Then he says that honor obliges him to march with Germany. Finally he states that he wants his share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.' 15 August: 'For in his view it is still possible—though difficult—that the democracies will back down. In that case it would not suit us to have clashed with Germany, since we too must take our share of the spoils. We must therefore find a solution that allows us: (a) if the democracies attack, to disengage ourselves “honorably” from the Germans; (b) if the democracies swallow it, to seize the opportunity to settle accounts with Belgrade once and for all.'” [Q-046] [C-042]

Naturally, the Italian press gets its instructions. It can't say any of this. It has to tell a different story. So I'll close with the *Corriere della Sera* on September 1: “THE DECISIVE HOUR STRIKES / Hitler's proposals for Danzig and the Corridor—loyal, reasonable, and eminently feasible—foolishly allowed to lapse by Warsaw and London / England and company nailed to their tremendous responsibilities / The publication of this page of history confirms Germany's conscious wisdom and moderation in the face of the blind intransigence of the other parties to the conflict” [Q-047]. These are the newspapers our fathers were reading in this happy country of ours in 1939.

Thank you.
