# How Wars Break Out: The Second World War

## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

<!-- transcript: U-00001–U-00103; omissions: none -->

Well, good evening. We have quite a journey ahead of us tonight, and it is, how shall I put it, a pretty grim one. You may say last night was no picnic either, but the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story. The only comic relief comes from Italy's part in it. Whatever you may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and the rest of them, they provide the only comedy in those otherwise very dramatic weeks, as you'll see.

So how does the Second World War break out? People have written that it really begins in 1919, with the signing of the Treaty of Versailles. The treaty that ends the First World War does so by creating endless grievances and frustrations. You'll remember that even Italy, which had after all gained Trento and Trieste and swallowed up South Tyrol, is not satisfied. Quite the opposite: Italians speak of the “mutilated victory”: “Our victory, you will not be mutilated. No one can break your knees or clip your wings. Where are you running? Where are you rising?” [Q-001] Even victorious Italy then comes close to sinking into civil war and falls into dictatorship, all because of a war that ended badly. Imagine how the defeated countries feel. [C-001]

Germany, having lost, emerges from the First World War and the Treaty of Versailles frustrated, convinced that it has suffered an injustice. What injustice? For one thing, Germany has been forced to give territory to a new country. Versailles creates many new states, including Poland. Germany gives Poland—or rather, is forced to give Poland—a large stretch of land inhabited by Germans. And on top of that, the treaty creates situations that are impossible to manage.

There is one city that means nothing to anyone today: Gdańsk, in Poland. We've hardly heard its name since the days of Lech Wałęsa, but back then people talk about it constantly. Danzig is a major port, an important German city, right on the border between the new Poland and Germany. Both countries claim it. The victors at Versailles decide: all right, let's make it a Free City, independent, neither German nor Polish. Danzig is inhabited entirely by Germans who want to belong to Germany, but they aren't allowed to.

Straight away, a public campaign begins in Germany, rather like the one in prewar Italy over Trento, Trieste, irredentism, and the separated brothers. Germans campaign on the slogan: Danzig, Danzig is German, Danzig is German and they didn't give it to us. It's a disgrace. One day we'll have to reopen the question. [Q-002]

Then there are all the other problems created by the end of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The empire is dissolved and many new states appear: Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia. And they bring plenty of problems with them. Austria is small and entirely German-speaking. Most Austrians would have liked to say: all right, now things have turned out this way, let's join Germany; we Austrians are Germans too. But the victors won't allow it. Austria has to remain independent.

Then there's Czechoslovakia, the state of the Czechs and Slovaks. But under the Austrian Empire, a great many Germans had lived in those regions—that is, Austrian citizens who spoke German. Now they find themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, but they don't feel Czechoslovak. So Versailles leaves behind this whole collection of problems, problems that are understandable in themselves. To a large extent, they're the problems of minorities living outside their own country.

Tonight I'll have to spend a while on the deeper causes of the Second World War. I would have liked to say: right, it's 10 August 1939, and this is what happens. We'll get there, but first we need to talk a little about those deeper causes, or none of what follows will make sense. And those causes are precisely the ones I've just described: a frustrated Germany with claims against Poland over Danzig; against Austria, because the Austrians are all Germans and it's hard to see why they shouldn't belong to Germany; and against Czechoslovakia, because of the Germans who live there and feel discriminated against.

In 1933 the Nazis win the elections in Germany and Hitler comes to power. Hitler takes power with a program containing many fine promises, but the point Germans perhaps feel most strongly is this: we must revise the Treaty of Versailles; we must revise the Europe that came out of Versailles. It's an unjust Europe, an iniquitous treaty. Germany has suffered, and that's not right. We must reopen the issue and change the borders. [C-002]

At heart, the story of how the Second World War breaks out is the story of two illusions. The first is the illusion of the great democratic powers, Britain and France: that Hitler can be given some satisfaction because, on certain points, he is right after all. They imagine they can say, very well, let's negotiate, let's reach an agreement—Danzig here, and so on—without causing any great upheaval. An illusion, as we'll see.

The other illusion is Hitler's: that he can take everything back, and a little more besides, and turn Germany once again into Europe's great power without anyone seriously standing in his way. The democracies' illusion—the illusion of the Western democracies, France and Britain. [C-003]

## 2. Halifax, anti-Bolshevism, and Hitler’s reading of the climate

<!-- transcript: U-00104–U-00181; omissions: none -->

There comes a moment when it's perfectly clear that they're fooling themselves. So, in the end, I've decided to begin our story tonight on this date: 19 November 1937. The Second World War is less than two years away. We know that; they don't.

On 19 November 1937, the British foreign secretary, Lord Halifax, travels to Berlin and meets Hitler. There, the British minister expresses sympathy for Germany's claims. Because by now there's a generation in Britain saying: yes, after all, we went too far at Versailles; we imposed unfair terms on Germany. You can understand why the Germans want them revised.

Halifax tells Hitler that Britain understands Germany's claims against Poland, Austria, and Czechoslovakia, and that changes may be possible in time, provided they're made peacefully, without causing upheaval. His words were: “I said that there were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today, but we were concerned to avoid such treatment of them as would be likely to cause trouble. If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.” [Q-003]

Lord Halifax tells Hitler something else as well: Germany is Europe's bulwark against Bolshevism. [Q-004]

Because if we want to understand how people reason in those years, we mustn't forget this other factor. In the East of Europe, there's another presence—one nobody mentions, which everyone pretends isn't there and talks about as little as possible: the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union is there, and by 1937 Stalin is in power. We know Stalin has put world revolution away in a drawer, but they don't.

To them—to Western politicians—the Soviet Union is still the country not even of the communists, but of the Bolsheviks, of extremely dangerous revolutionaries. It's a country to be kept at arm's length. So Herr Hitler's Germany, as British diplomats call it in their reports—they always call him that, Herr Hitler—with its ferocious anti-communism and all those concentration camps where it has locked up German communists, is, when all is said and done, a useful country. [C-004]

That's true in Britain, and it's true in France. Unlike Britain, France has a strong Communist Party and a strong Socialist Party, and a left-wing government, the Popular Front. We've forgotten it today, but at the time the Popular Front arouses—never mind Hollande—enormous hopes and enormous fears in France. And France has a ruling class of industrialists, generals, and clergy who, faced with a communist government, say: well, the Nazis are better, much better; better Hitler than Stalin. “Nothing about the conduct of this fraction of the French bourgeoisie can be understood unless one hears it murmuring under its breath: ‘Hitler rather than Blum.’” [Q-005]

So, so Hitler senses a climate in which certain hopes are possible. Certain things can be done; he only has to wait for the opportunity. There's been a great deal of debate about whether Hitler plans everything from the start, whether he has a precise plan leading all the way to the invasion of Poland. You know Woody Allen? “I can't listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” [Q-006] But invading Poland is only the final act. So the question has been debated endlessly: had Hitler decided everything right from the beginning? Perhaps not.

In the 1960s, a famous book appears by the English historian A. J. P. Taylor. Taylor says that, criminal though Hitler was, what he wanted in foreign policy was perfectly normal. Everyone wanted their own country to be great and powerful; Hitler wanted the same, and he waited for the right opportunities, sniffing the air. The wind was blowing his way. “There was here no concrete plan, no directive for German policy in 1937 and 1938. Or if there were a directive, it was to wait upon events. [...] Hitler did not make plans—for world conquest or for anything else. He assumed that others would provide opportunities, and that he would seize them. [...] His foreign policy was a different matter. He aimed to make Germany the dominant Power in Europe and maybe, more remotely, in the world. Other Powers have pursued similar aims, and still do.” [Q-007]

Waiting for opportunities—but creating them too. Taylor didn't know much about that; documents that emerge later make it clear: Hitler creates the opportunities. It begins with Austria. Austria is a German country. [C-005]

So Austria has a Nazi party that wants the country annexed to Germany and takes its orders from Hitler. At a certain point, Hitler gives the Austrian Nazis the go-ahead: stir up unrest, carry out attacks and acts of terrorism, create disorder, commit political murder. [C-006]

Then, finally, we reach March 1938. Four months have passed since Lord Halifax visited Berlin and told Hitler: well, after all, your position is understandable. Four months go by. In Vienna, the Austrian Nazi Party stages a coup and invites German troops into Austria to guarantee security. On 12 March 1938, German troops enter Austria. Yes, the Anschluss, as it's called: the annexation.

Yes, the German army is so unprepared for war at this point that, according to Guderian's later account, roughly thirty percent of its tanks break down before reaching Vienna—not seventy percent of all its military vehicles. [N-006] [C-007] And yet nobody abroad reacts.

In Austria, there's widespread enthusiasm. A plebiscite is held, and ninety-nine percent vote to approve annexation to Nazi Germany. The cardinal of Vienna, Innitzer, declares that Austrian Catholics must thank the Lord and obey the new government, which has saved them from the communist threat: “We are also convinced that the work of the National Socialist movement has warded off the danger of utterly destructive, godless Bolshevism. In Britain, the London *Times* writes: after all, it's rather like what we did two centuries ago, when Scotland joined England; this is all fairly normal. It's what the people wanted. Hitler takes note: Austria annexed, and nobody reacts. A few days pass—really, only a few days. We're still in March 1938—

## 4. Sudeten crisis and the exclusion of the Soviet Union

Hitler summons the leader of the Czechoslovak Nazis. Because Czechoslovakia, too, has a Nazi party among the German minority in the Sudetenland. That's what they're called—I can never remember why—but the Germans of Czechoslovakia are known as the Sudeten Germans.

The Germans in Czechoslovakia have a Nazi party that takes its orders from Hitler, and in March 1938 Hitler gives the signal: start the street violence, the agitation, the terrorism. Czechoslovakia begins to descend into chaos. [C-009]

The German press launches an extremely aggressive campaign. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are being oppressed; they're a persecuted minority, a downtrodden minority. The criminal Czechoslovak police arrest them, rape them, torture them. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are suffering; this is intolerable. [Q-010]

Acting on instructions from their government, the British ambassadors in Berlin and Prague say: well, after all, the Germans aren't entirely wrong; you can understand their position. They keep saying that Germany's claims are, in the end, justified, and that the Czechoslovak government must try to meet the Germans halfway. We certainly don't want to unleash another war, do we? So the Czechoslovak government had better make concessions. [C-010]

At that moment, as the Nazis in Czechoslovakia begin playing the same game they played in Austria, only one country takes a public stand and says that all the powers should come together to guarantee Czechoslovakia. That country is the Soviet Union.

And of course nobody listens, because the Soviet Union is the Soviet Union—the Bolshevik, communist country—and it has no place in the game. During those weeks, the British ambassador in Moscow sends a report to London that concludes, and I quote: “The Soviet Union must […] for the time being, be counted out of European politics in so far as the exercise of a decisive influence one way or the other is concerned.” [Q-011] Russia can be treated as though it doesn't exist. [C-011]

Notice again how hard they find it to grasp that the world is changing. They go on talking about “European politics” as though politics were still always made by Britain and France. No: the United States isn't there, Japan isn't there, the Soviet Union isn't there—nothing. It's still European politics as usual, and Russia doesn't count.

This goes on for months. Czechoslovakia sinks into chaos, the German press pounds away, pounds away, pounds away at the suffering of the Sudeten Germans, and Britain and France keep pressing the government in Prague: come on, make some concessions.

## 5. Munich, peace at any price, and appeasement’s failure

<!-- transcript: U-00257–U-00358; omissions: none -->

At last we reach September. The situation is now so serious that the British and French governments decide the Czechoslovaks must be forced to make concessions to Hitler. They need to come up with something. They ought to arrange a conference—what we'd call a negotiating table today. Back then, they hadn't yet invented the “table,” or the “summit.” They should hold a conference, they say.

But how do they persuade Hitler to come to the negotiating table? Ask Mussolini, since the two are friends. The British and French approach Mussolini and say, in effect—I'm summarizing—sound Hitler out. See if he'll agree to get everyone together and reach a settlement that accommodates Germany's demands on Czechoslovakia. Hitler agrees.

And so, on 29 and 30 September 1938, we come to an episode later remembered—how shall I put it—as one of the low points of European politics, even though at least two of the four protagonists are acting in good faith: the Munich Conference. Hitler and Mussolini meet in Munich, Bavaria, along with the British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, and the French prime minister, Daladier. [C-012]

At least Chamberlain and Daladier are acting in good faith. It's hard to doubt that. They're haunted by the same nightmare as the whole generation that witnessed the First World War. Like everyone who saw that slaughter, the politicians of those years have only one thought whenever a crisis breaks out: anything but another war. Never again, whatever the price. Or, if you prefer: “our policy was the policy of peace at any price.” [Q-012] [C-013]

And what does “any price” mean? Well, if Czechoslovakia has to hand over a piece of its territory to Germany, so be it; that's better than war. At the Munich Conference, Britain, France, Italy, and Germany meet to decide which part of Czechoslovakia Germany will receive. The Czechoslovaks wait outside the door to be told what has been decided. And the conference decides that the whole German-populated region, the Sudetenland, will be ceded to Germany.

Hitler takes note. Not only have they not stood in my way; they've actually helped me take a piece of Czechoslovakia.

And here, how shall I put it—as I said—I don't know. It's hard to talk about this, because on the one hand you feel like repeating what people said for the next fifty years: what a tragedy, politicians behaving at that level. And yet, and yet, these are, how shall I put it, first-rate politicians who fail to understand what's happening, and they're acting in good faith.

Chamberlain returns to London. People have mocked that return from Munich ever since. Chamberlain comes back to London, and as soon as he steps off the plane, he's already waving the sheet of paper with the agreement on it. You see the scene? The photographers are there, the crowd, everyone around him. Chamberlain waves the paper and addresses the crowd.

I'll translate Chamberlain's speech to the crowd that day, as he steps off the plane on his return from Munich:

“The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is, in my view, only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace. [...] We regard the agreement signed last night and the Anglo-German naval agreement as symbolic of the desire of our two peoples never to go to war with one another again.
[...] My good friends, this is the second time in our history that there has come back from Germany to Downing Street peace with honour.
I believe it is peace for our time. [...] And now I recommend you to go home and sleep quietly in your beds.” We are eleven months away from the outbreak of the Second World War, remember. This is Chamberlain speaking to the London crowd after returning from Munich. [Q-013]

We are eleven months away from the outbreak of the Second World War; keep that in mind. Chamberlain says that that morning he'd had another meeting with the German chancellor, Herr Hitler, and here is the treaty with Hitler's signature and his own. They see the agreement signed the night before as a symbol of their two peoples' desire never to go to war with each other again. My dear friends: the British prime minister has returned from Germany bringing peace with honor. He believes it is peace for their time. Go home and sleep soundly. That's what Chamberlain tells the London crowd when he returns from Munich.

Chamberlain's policy has a specific name, one that later becomes an ugly word in British political vocabulary—we'll hear it again tomorrow night when we talk about Mrs Thatcher: *appeasement*. Pacification. Peace at any cost.

And *appeasement* does seem shocking today. They didn't understand. No, they didn't understand. The tragic irony is that they were willing to do anything to stop war from breaking out. The result is that they make it break out. [C-014]

Mind you, Hitler is very good at this. He's extraordinarily skilled at running rings around people and deceiving whoever he's dealing with. At Munich, Hitler tells Chamberlain—and listen to this; Chamberlain himself reports it, apparently. He says: I spoke to Hitler. And speaking with great sincerity, Hitler told me: “In the first place he repeated to me with great earnestness what he had said already at Berchtesgaden, namely, that this was the last of his territorial ambitions in Europe and that he had no wish to include in the Reich people of other races than Germans.” [Q-014]

Hitler said it. And he was so sincere. So: sleep soundly.

## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

But Chamberlain and Daladier aren’t the only ones taken in—Mussolini believes it too. And so does Poland. Poland, remember, will itself become a victim when war breaks out. Yet when Czechoslovakia is partitioned under the Munich settlement, Poland takes advantage of the situation and seizes a small piece of Czechoslovakia for itself. Poland is a militaristic country, governed by colonels, a kind of semi-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and rabidly anti-communist. At this point, the Poles are convinced they can be friends with Hitler because they’re all anti-communists. After all, who is more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles are—even more so. And so, in the last months of 1938 and the first months of 1939, the Poles believe they’re Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

And Hitler? On 30 January 1939, with seven months to go before the outbreak of the world war, he tells the Reichstag, the German parliament: “Even during the troubled months of the past year, German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life.” [Q-015]

At the end of January 1939, the Polish foreign minister, Józef Beck, meets Ribbentrop in Warsaw—not Hitler in Berlin, as I’ve just made it sound. In Ribbentrop’s account: “M. Beck made no secret of the fact that Poland had aspirations directed toward the Soviet Ukraine and a connection with the Black Sea; but at the same time he called attention to the supposed dangers to Poland that in the Polish view would arise from a treaty with Germany directed against the Soviet Union.” [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, as these pleasant conversations continue through January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia is falling apart. It has lost the entire German-populated belt, most of its industry, and its border fortifications. Slovakia now wants independence. It’s an old story, as you know: that does eventually happen, and today Slovakia is an independent country, having separated. Czechoslovakia is collapsing and is on the point of being invaded by Hungary. At a certain point, Hitler decides to settle the matter. [C-016]

On 14 March 1939, Hitler orders the Czechoslovak president, Emil Hácha, to come and see him in Berlin. Hácha obeys. After one in the morning on 15 March, Hitler tells him that German troops will enter at six, and that any resistance will be crushed. It is Göring, not Hitler, who threatens Prague with an air attack. Hácha collapses and is given medical attention and injections; the primary records consulted don’t establish that he had a diagnosed heart attack or that doctors deliberately kept him awake. He signs a declaration—not a literal request for German intervention—which reads: “The President of the Czecho-Slovak State declared that, in order to serve this end and to reach a final pacification, he confidently placed the fate of the Czech people and of their country in the hands of the Fuehrer of the German Reich.” [Q-017] German forces occupy Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March, after Slovakia declares independence under German pressure on 14 March. [N-014] [C-017]

On 15 March, the Germans invade Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, no one has intervened. [C-017]

But surely, you’ll say, by now Chamberlain is beginning to wonder whether Hitler can be trusted after all. Two days later, on 17 March 1939, Chamberlain speaks in Birmingham. [N-015] I’ll translate the speech for you. He is clearly saying things he would much rather not have to say; you can hear it in the syntax. Chamberlain says:

“Does not the question inevitably arise in our minds, if it is so easy to discover good reasons for ignoring assurances so solemnly and so repeatedly given, what reliance can be placed upon any other assurances that come from the same source? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” [Q-018]

Chamberlain is plainly hoping that someone will tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt is beginning to creep in. And the proof comes immediately. The Germans have invaded Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on 15 March. [C-018]

## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Ten days later, on 26 March, Hitler receives the Polish government’s formal rejection of Germany’s demand to incorporate the Free City of Danzig into the Reich—although German pressure over Danzig has actually been building for months. At the end of April 1939, Hitler addresses the Reichstag. Three months earlier, in the same place, he has called German-Polish friendship one of the reassuring features of European political life. Now, three months later, he publicly presents his case like this: “This is, as I have emphasized before, perhaps the most difficult of all Germany’s problems. [...] I saw the peaceful resolution of this question as a further contribution to a lasting relaxation of tensions in Europe. [...] The Polish government rejected this offer of mine. [...] I sincerely regretted this attitude of the Polish government, which I could not understand.” The opening sentence refers to the broader territorial settlement and Poland’s access to the sea, not just to Danzig; this is Hitler’s public justification for Germany’s demands, not a neutral account of them. [Q-019] [C-019]

From this point on, the conflict intensifies in Danzig and along the border between Danzig and Poland: incidents, shootings. Nazi militias form in Danzig; just outside is the Polish customs post. Incidents keep happening. But this time, the British and French governments are beginning to think that perhaps they ought to do something. What?

Britain and France propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain even goes so far as to issue an official guarantee. No treaty has yet been signed, but Britain—with France taking the same position—will give Poland all the support in its power if an action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland considers it vital to resist. Looking back today, of course, you say: about time. What else could they do? [C-020]

At the time, though, this is very hard to sell to the public. Danzig? Are we really supposed to go to war over Danzig? A famous headline appears in the French newspaper *L’Œuvre*: “Morire per Danzica?”—“Die for Danzig?” No. It doesn’t represent all British and French opinion, but the slogan becomes influential. Even so, the French and British governments—undaunted, now that they’ve discovered Hitler may not be trustworthy—do something else unprecedented in April and May 1939. [Q-020] [C-020]

They begin exploring whether some kind of agreement can be reached with the Soviet Union. Negotiations begin and drag on for months, as they try to work out whether a military defense agreement can be concluded in the event of war. The story of these negotiations is sad and depressing, because you can’t help thinking: if only they had reached that agreement. Instead, it seems that no one wants it quite enough; they’re all afraid of one another. One apparently objective pattern emerges from the telegrams and replies: whenever Britain and France make a proposal to the Russians, the Russians answer the next day. Then weeks go by—weeks and weeks—before the British and French finally take the next step. The Russians answer the next day, and the negotiations drag on. This is an illustrative pattern, not an exact rule for every single exchange, and there is mistrust and obstruction on all sides. [C-021]

Meanwhile, Stalin takes note. They’re asking me to negotiate—but do they mean it? Or are they trying to draw me in, only to leave me facing Germany alone when the decisive moment comes? Stalin takes note and decides the risk is too great. He is negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French seem decidedly lukewarm.

Stalin lets the Germans know that, although the two countries are enemies—one communist, the other, well...—they do have commercial interests. So perhaps it would be a good idea to open negotiations for a trade agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The Germans are willing. And over the summer, without anyone knowing, a situation develops in which Stalin is negotiating with Britain and France at one table and with Germany at another, waiting to see what happens. More precisely, economic contacts revive unevenly and only later develop into a political channel, before accelerating dramatically in August. [C-021]

## 8. Italy’s alliance without the means for war

<!-- transcript: U-00522–U-00560; omissions: none -->

And then there’s Italy. Italy is Nazi Germany’s closest friend. On 22 May 1939, the two countries sign the Pact of Steel amid tremendous enthusiasm in the press. In Hitler’s telegram to Victor Emmanuel III, he describes the bond between the two peoples this way: “In this historic hour I wish to express to Your Majesty my profound satisfaction that our two peoples are bound to one another by an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.” [Q-021]

But in reality, Mussolini is worried. Ribbentrop has repeatedly assured the Italians in person that Germany doesn’t want a war just yet—although the treaty itself contains no such guarantee—and Mussolini is beginning to feel less certain. [C-023]

The first thing we need to understand clearly—something that isn’t always stated so plainly, though it is well known and well documented—is why Mussolini is so uncertain. At this point, Italy is desperately short of resources. Italy has fought the Ethiopian War and intervened on Franco’s side in the Spanish Civil War. Everything has gone well: the Ethiopian War is a triumph, and things have gone fairly well in Spain too. But wars cost terrifying amounts of money. The wars in Ethiopia and Spain have torn a hole in the Italian budget and drained the country’s reserves of matériel and weapons. There is a severe shortage, especially of modern weapons. [C-022]

Now, as it happens, over the last few years every country has seen which way the wind is blowing and has begun experimenting with new weapons. Everywhere, new kinds of fighter aircraft, like the Spitfire, and new tanks are going into production. Weapons that were perfectly adequate just two or three years ago are quickly becoming obsolete. Italy doesn’t have the industrial capacity or the resources to modernize fast enough. Mussolini knows this, even if he sometimes prefers to forget it. But in the summer of 1939, he knows. And he has one great fear: that the Germans might get it into their heads to start a war. [C-022]

In early August, Ciano writes in his diary—you know Galeazzo Ciano, the foreign minister, Mussolini’s son-in-law, who will be shot five years later after the Verona trial, but that’s another story. In early August, Ciano, our foreign minister, makes a note in his diary.

## 9. Ciano learns Germany wants war

<!-- transcript: U-00561–U-00595; omissions: none -->

In his diary entry for 4 August, Ciano writes: “I am beginning to consider the advisability of meeting Ribbentrop. The time has come to find out what the situation really is. The stakes are too high for us to wait passively for developments.” [Q-022]

So Ciano flies to Germany. On 11 August, he meets his German counterpart, Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop. Ciano has been sent to explain that starting a war now would be madness, and that they absolutely must not do it. In a later recollection, Ciano describes asking Ribbentrop as the two men walk together in the garden: “‘Well, Ribbentrop,’ I asked him as we walked side by side in the garden, ‘what do you want? The Corridor or Danzig?’ ‘Not anymore,’ and he fixed me with those cold Musée Grévin eyes of his, ‘we want war.’” [Q-023]

They’re at a reception, all standing around with champagne glasses in their hands. Ciano is desperate to tell his staff what Ribbentrop has just said to him, but the reception is still going on. Ciano later writes all this down. At last, he passes close to one of his aides and whispers in his ear, “We’re headed for a fight.”

Ciano talks further with Ribbentrop. The message is clear: war with Poland has been decided, and Poland will be crushed. And Britain? France? Ribbentrop believes they won’t intervene. And then comes the wager, just as in 1914—for those of you who were here last night. The wager is that they won’t intervene. Except this time—you know how history repeats itself, first as tragedy and then as farce?—they actually place a bet.

In Ciano’s later account: “They were making a fundamental error of calculation: they were certain that France and England would stand idly by while Poland was slaughtered. On this subject Ribbentrop wanted, during one of those gloomy meals we took at the Oesterreichischer Hof in Salzburg, to make a wager with me. I was to give him an Italian painting in the event of Anglo-French neutrality; he was to give me a collection of antique weapons in the event of intervention. Many witnesses were present, and not very long ago we were speaking about it with Ambassador von Mackensen. Ribbentrop, however, preferred to forget that wager and never paid his debt.” [Q-024] The idea is that Germany can go to war without setting off a world war, because France and Britain won’t dare intervene. [C-024]

The gala dinner ends, then the reception, the toasts, everything. Ciano returns to the hotel with his aides, desperate to tell them the whole story. Once they’re there, he starts wondering whether the Germans might have hidden microphones in the room. So they lock themselves in the bathroom, and there Ciano tells his aides that the Germans have now decided on war. And this is where the countdown begins: 12 August.

## 10. 12–18 August: rival negotiations and Polish refusal

<!-- transcript: U-00596–U-00727; omissions: none -->

The day after Ciano’s meeting with Ribbentrop is Saturday, 12 August. It’s the Ferragosto holiday season; this war, like the First World War, breaks out in the middle of summer. People don’t take as many holidays as they do now, but they do take some, and at Ferragosto they go away. On Saturday, 12 August, for example, Milan empties out: half a million people take the train to the seaside.

That same day, after months of talk, the Anglo-French delegation finally arrives in Moscow to negotiate a treaty. The Soviets are deeply disappointed. The delegation consists of a French general, Joseph Doumenc, and a British admiral, Reginald Drax—neither of them at the very highest level. The Russians ask, “Do you have full powers to sign a treaty?”

Let me stop for a moment. This question of full powers is extremely important if we want to understand diplomatic exchanges in this period. Of course, the telephone, the telegraph, and the airplane all exist. Even so, everyone considers it essential to have someone there in person who can sign. So the Soviets ask: “You’ve come to negotiate—excellent. This is urgent. Do you have full powers?” Drax can negotiate, but he can’t sign. Doumenc has Daladier’s authority to conclude a military convention, although he doesn’t carry the usual plenipotentiary credentials and remains dependent on Paris. In practical terms, the answer is: “You tell us, and we’ll report back.” The Soviets are deeply disappointed. [C-025]

That same day, the Soviet government sends an invitation: it would be a good idea for Ribbentrop to come to Moscow, because they have things to discuss. Sunday, 13 August: the height of summer, the holiday season, the Venice Film Festival; Maria Bellonci wins the Viareggio Prize for *Lucrezia Borgia*.

That same day, Hitler speaks with Mussolini. It is exactly as Ciano has said: Germany is going to make war on Poland, and will attack at the first incident. And that isn’t all. Hitler explains that it is now mid-August. His generals calculate that it will take a month and a half—six weeks at most—to defeat Poland. By mid-October, autumn will arrive, and Poland’s roads and airfields will be unusable because of the mud. So the campaign has to be over by mid-October. They need a month and a half, which means they have to attack by the end of August. This is Hitler’s operational estimate and the reasoning behind his timetable. [C-026]

Mussolini and the others in Italy are appalled. In his diary entry for 13 August, Ciano writes: “I return to Rome disgusted with Germany, its leaders, and their way of acting. They have deceived us and lied to us. And today they are about to drag us into an adventure that we did not want and that could imperil the regime and the country.” [Q-025]

Monday, 14 August: the holiday atmosphere continues, with the Salzburg Festival under way. Hitler, who is up in the mountains, goes to the festival to see Mozart’s *The Abduction from the Seraglio*.

In Moscow, the Russians meet the Anglo-French delegation and put their cards on the table. Do they want an agreement to defend Poland? Very well. But the Soviets want a guarantee that the agreement will be taken seriously: if war comes, the Poles must allow Soviet troops onto Polish territory. Are the Soviets acting in good faith or bad faith? Are they already thinking about swallowing Poland, or do they sincerely believe that, if a war has to be fought, it has to be fought seriously? We don’t know. In any case, that is their demand. And what do the British admiral and the French general say? “That isn’t within our authority. We have to report back. We’ll report to Paris and London, and then we’ll see.” The transit issue is the immediate impasse, but it isn’t the only reason the talks fail: Anglo-French delays and limited mandates, Polish fear of Soviet occupation, Soviet bargaining, and the German option all play a part. [C-027]

That same Monday, 14 August, Ribbentrop decides that the Russian suggestion that he visit Moscow is a good one. He’ll go on Tuesday, 15 August—Ferragosto. The French general has written that, before the agreement can be signed, the Russians want a guarantee that the Poles will let them enter if war breaks out. So the Poles have to be asked.

The French foreign minister, Georges Bonnet, tries to find the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz. And where do you think the Polish ambassador is on Ferragosto? At the seaside. They bring him back to Paris and tell him: allow the Russians through if there is a war against Germany. The ambassador says he’ll have to ask Warsaw, but in his view the answer will be no, because the Poles are even more afraid of the Russians than they are of the Germans.

Bonnet warns the Polish ambassador that the French understand Hitler has said he will dispose of Poland in three weeks. The ambassador’s reply is important if we want to understand the atmosphere, and the kind of regime Poland has. Bonnet later recalls Łukasiewicz answering: “On the contrary, it is the Polish army that will invade Germany from the very first days.” [Q-026]

Wednesday, 16 August. Ribbentrop writes to Moscow: “I am coming.” This is the first time they put it in writing that he will make the journey to sign a treaty with the Soviets. Meanwhile, in Berlin, the Germans are beginning to worry about one thing. Those of you who were here last night will forgive me: history does sometimes repeat itself. The Germans are asking themselves: what will Britain do?

On the evening of 15 August, the British ambassador, Sir Nevile Henderson, visits State Secretary Ernst von Weizsäcker at the German Foreign Ministry. The German argument goes like this: surely Britain isn’t going to support Poland? Look how mad these Poles are, with all their provocations. The German minority in Poland is being oppressed; it’s all the fault of those unreasonable Poles. Surely the British aren’t prepared to help a bunch of madmen?

Henderson answers: “I told Baron von Weizsäcker that the last was a very dangerous theory and sounded like Herr von Ribbentrop who had never been able to understand the British mentality. If the Poles were compelled by any act of Germany to resort to arms to defend themselves there was not a shadow of doubt that we would give them our full armed support. We had made that abundantly clear and Germany would be making a tragic mistake if she imagined the contrary.” [Q-027] [C-028]

Thursday, 17 August. Incidents multiply along the German-Polish border: shootings, people crossing the frontier. The German press launches a campaign—remember Czechoslovakia the year before?—about “Polish terror against the German minority”: arrests, murders. In Moscow, Ribbentrop’s reply arrives: he is willing to come and sign an agreement.

Friday, 18 August. As you know, Mussolini and Ciano are terrified because Italy isn’t ready for war. But the official line dictated to the Italian press is, of course, quite different. Italy has signed the Pact of Steel. The Italian newspapers have precise instructions: in this crisis, the Germans are right.

So let me read you the front-page display from the afternoon edition of the *Corriere della Sera* on 18 August:

“POLAND AT THE DECISIVE TURNING POINT OF ITS HISTORY / The tragic responsibility of the democracies / which unleashed and sustained Polish arrogance / Unshakable firmness of the Axis against the warmongering cloaked in hypocrisy by the encirclers” [Q-028]

Democracy, of course, is a dirty word in Fascist Italy. The “encirclers” are none other than the “demo-pluto-Jewish-ocracies”: France, Britain, and the Soviet Union, which supposedly have the peoples under siege. Well, you know; we know this story in Italy.

That same Friday, 18 August, the Polish government has to decide whether to enter an alliance with the Soviet Union and allow Soviet troops through its territory. The Polish government says no: “We have no military agreements with the Soviet Union and do not wish to have any.”

There is also a famous remark that later becomes proverbial, attributed—though not preserved in any secure contemporary transcript—to Poland’s commander-in-chief, Marshal Edward Śmigły-Rydz: “With the Germans we may lose our freedom, with the Russians we shall lose our soul.” [Q-029] The remark is generally dated to around 19–20 August, rather than to the government’s decision on the eighteenth. And so there is no agreement. [C-027]

## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, 19 August, word reaches Moscow that the Poles don't want an agreement with Russia, and the negotiations grind to a halt. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: at this point, let Ribbentrop come. How about the 26th or 27th?

Sunday, 20 August: a telegram arrives from Hitler to Stalin. Remember, this is the first time in history that a Western head of government has written personally to the head of the Soviet government. Until then, the Soviet Union had been treated like a plague-stricken country. People spoke to it only indirectly, under the table. And the first telegram Stalin receives from a Western ruler comes from Hitler. On 20 August, Hitler tells him: “Hitler: 'I accept the draft of the non-aggression pact that your Foreign Minister, Herr Molotov, delivered but consider it urgently necessary to clarify the questions connected with it as soon as possible. [...] I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23.' Stalin: 'I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23.'” [Q-030]

Monday, 21 August. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for some way to prevent war. Then it occurs to them: why not do what they did the year before? Hold another nice conference in Munich, an international conference where everyone can come to an agreement. So they call Berlin, ask for Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and put forward this splendid idea. According to Ciano's diary, Ribbentrop is nowhere to be found. Finally, that afternoon, he calls back and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That very day, 21 August, Stalin receives Hitler's telegram at three in the afternoon and replies at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let's stop here for a moment. This is a crucial decision, one that does a great deal to make what follows possible. It is completely unexpected, and it comes as a shock. We need to be very clear about this because—and here I'll give you my opinion; some people will disagree—for years we've been brainwashed with the idea that all totalitarian regimes were alike, that Stalin's Soviet Union and Hitler's Germany were the same thing. If you believe that, their agreement seems less strange. But for people at the time, the reality is an enormous shock. Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union look like the two most bitterly opposed, most hostile countries in existence. And they are. This agreement is proof of the extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and coldness of both the Nazis and Stalin and his circle. The world is stunned.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are too hesitant, that they don't really want an agreement, so he'll make one with the Nazis instead, just to spite them. Because he needs security. Let me add one small thing, something few people know, and perhaps many of you don't: a small piece of the context of those days. Stalin sees that things are going very badly—as Ciano says, “we are heading for a fight”—and decides he absolutely needs security, even if that means making a pact with the devil. They won't keep the pact, but for now they need it. Soviet distrust and the need to avoid immediate isolation were important motives, alongside the neutrality and territorial advantages offered by Germany. [C-029]

So what is this piece of context that no one knows at the time, and that few people remember even today? At that very moment, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war with Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets, and it borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war involving entire army corps is raging there. Barbero gives a total of 70,000 dead and wounded, though casualty totals are disputed. The two governments agree to keep it from the world, and the world hears nothing about it—still less about some border between Mongolia and Manchuria. But this is a full-blown war. The Japanese advance from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. And the Soviets, incidentally, win decisively. On the 21st, while the battle is still raging, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany because he has to secure at least one frontier. This fighting influenced his calculation, although the evidence does not reduce his decision to that single consideration. [C-030]

That same day, the news becomes public. Mussolini and Ciano, like the rest of the world, are stunned by the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. Stunned, but impressed too: these Germans are formidable. Ciano writes in his diary: “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke. The European situation has been overturned. Can France and England, which based their entire anti-Axis policy on alliance with the Soviets, still count on the unconditional support of the extremist masses? And will the encirclement system built through the small states still hold now that its Moscow cornerstone has collapsed? In any case, we should not rush our decisions: wait, and if possible remain ready to take our own share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.” [Q-031]

That final thought tells you everything about the language and the moral level of the regime.

On Tuesday, 22 August, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. He says this is the right moment, just as it was in 1914. But here's the interesting part: he isn't talking about whether the armies are ready. He has something else in mind. In this famous speech to his generals on 22 August, Hitler says: “My own personality and that of Mussolini. Essentially it depends on me, my existence, because of my political activities. Furthermore the fact that probably no one will ever again have the confidence of the whole German people as I do. There will probably never again be a man in the future with more authority than I have. My existence is therefore a factor of great value. But I can be eliminated at any time by a criminal or an idiot. The second personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive. If something happens to him, Italy's loyalty to the alliance will no longer be certain. [...] The Duce is the man with the strongest nerves in Italy.” [Q-032] Hitler's political culture is steeped in political terrorism and assassination. To him, being killed is simply something that might happen. So the war has to begin while he's still alive, and while Mussolini is still there. Hitler still trusts his old friend and is fooling himself about Mussolini at the very moment Mussolini has already decided to pull out. [C-031]

Wednesday, 23 August. The agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union is announced in the world press. The *Corriere della Sera* proclaims: “THE GERMAN-SOVIET AGREEMENT / The Axis's great political success / has broken the murky manoeuvre of the democratic encirclers / A completely new situation, rich in highly important developments - Von Ribbentrop has left by air for Moscow - The enormous worldwide response - Astonishment and dismay in the anti-totalitarian countries - Poland will have to seek its salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany” [Q-033]. “Anti-totalitarian,” of course, is an insult. *We* are totalitarian—Mussolini invented the whole idea of a totalitarian country—while those feeble French and the rest are “anti-totalitarian.”

Poland is starting to give way, starting to think that perhaps an agreement with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible. But it's too late. On 23 August, Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the non-aggression pact, which is announced publicly. He also signs the secret protocol, which of course is not made public. In it, Germany and the Soviet Union establish spheres in Polish territory, essentially providing for the partition of Poland while leaving the final settlement open. [C-032]

## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

<!-- transcript: U-00881–U-00942; omissions: none -->

That day, Hitler receives the British ambassador, because his one remaining concern is to keep Britain out. He tells him about the atrocities the Poles are supposedly committing against the German minority in Poland. Now, here's a small detail I could have left out, like so many others. But even tonight, I haven't had the courage to cut too many details. I'll have to hurry, because I'm taking far too long. In any case, this is a morbid conversation between Hitler and the British ambassador. Henderson's report says: “He then violently attacked the Poles, talked of 100,000 German refugees from Poland, excesses against Germans, closing of German institutions and Polish systematic persecution of German nationals generally. [...] He had mentioned the castration of Germans. I happened to be aware of one case. The German in question was a sex-maniac, who had been treated as he deserved. Herr Hitler's retort was that there had not been one case but six.” [Q-034]

And on they go. Hitler also tells him that Poland is the last issue. Henderson reports Hitler's words like this: “Among various points mentioned by Herr Hitler were: [...] that he was by nature an artist not a politician, and that once the Polish question was settled he would end his life as an artist and not as a war-monger; [...] that once the Polish question was settled he himself would settle down.” [Q-035] The ambassador reports all this to London.

Friday, 25 August. Hitler orders the attack for the next day. Then a letter arrives from Mussolini, and for the first time Mussolini tells him the truth: Italy isn't ready for war. But he still doesn't have the courage to tell him the whole truth. Mussolini writes: “If Germany attacks Poland and Poland's allies counterattack Germany, I put before you the advisability of my not taking the initiative in military operations, given the present condition of Italian military preparations, which has repeatedly and promptly been made known to you, Führer, and to von Ribbentrop. Our intervention can nevertheless be immediate if Germany immediately gives us the war materiel and raw materials needed to withstand the blow that the French and British will direct mainly against us. In our meetings war had been envisaged for after 1942, and by then I would have been ready on land, at sea, and in the air, according to the agreed plans.” [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He postpones the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what raw materials Italy needs. Mussolini's letter was one immediate factor in the postponement, alongside the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance and Hitler's continuing attempt to detach Britain from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, 26 August. Mussolini and the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces meet at Palazzo Venezia to draw up the list of raw materials they will ask Germany for. Ciano has already primed the generals: when you make this list, no criminal optimism. In other words, don't hold back. They draw up the list, and I'll read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—Italy's entire production for two years, just to give you an idea—two million tonnes of steel, Italy's production for a year; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; and then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. The nominal requirement was for sustaining a twelve-month war. The list went down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to Attolico, the Italian ambassador in Berlin, and he takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks at the list and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” Panic. No one has told Attolico anything, and he has no instructions. But he understands the situation and improvises: “Immediately.” It was calculated that 17,000 trains would be needed to send all that material to Italy—a contemporary estimate by Ciano, not an independently audited conversion. [C-034]

Ribbentrop goes to Hitler, and at last they understand. The penny finally drops: Italy isn't going to take part. Italy will stay out. Very well, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini: “I have received the information concerning your final attitude. I respect the motives and influences determining your resolution. Perhaps it will nevertheless be for the best. Of course, in my opinion, this will be true only if the world does not receive any inkling of the intended attitude of Italy, at least not till actual fighting starts. [...] You and your people could help me most in this hard struggle by supporting me with Italian manpower, manpower for industrial as well as agricultural purposes.” [Q-038]

And here, quietly, another long story begins to unfold. It shows you how circumstances change. The Germans suddenly realize they can't count on Italy for military help, but they can count on Italian workers. Organized Italian labor migration to Germany had already begun in 1938; this request expanded an existing flow that would become increasingly coercive, distinct from the mass forced labor of Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]

## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, 27 August, Germany introduces rationing: food cards. Monday, 28 August. Britain and France still think they may be able to persuade the Poles to negotiate, and they put pressure on the Polish government: go and ask the Germans exactly what they want, and then let's talk about it.

Tuesday, 29 August. In Italy, private cars are banned from the roads, and austerity measures come into force in restaurants. Menus are allowed just one main dish: meat or fish, but only one. The British ambassador goes to see Hitler and find out whether, even at this last moment, negotiations are genuinely still possible. The surviving documents read: “Henderson: 'Interview this evening was of a stormy character and Herr Hitler far less reasonable than yesterday. [...] He kept saying that he wanted British friendship more than anything in the world, but he could not sacrifice Germany's vital interests therefor.' Henderson's accompanying report: 'German Government, note adds, counts on arrival of such plenipotentiary to-morrow, Wednesday, 30th August. [...] I remarked that this phrase sounded like an ultimatum.' Hitler's written reply: 'The German Government, accordingly, in these circumstances agree to accept the British Government's offer of their good offices in securing the despatch to Berlin of a Polish Emissary with full powers. They count on the arrival of this Emissary on Wednesday, the 30th August, 1939.'” [Q-039]

Wednesday, 30 August. In Italy, the world cycling championships in Varese are suspended. The sale of coffee to private individuals is banned—you can't even have a cup in a café—and all coffee is reserved for the armed forces. In France, rail traffic is suspended, and the animals at Vincennes Zoo are evacuated for safety. In every capital, people start building air-raid shelters and handing out gas masks, because everyone is convinced the war will begin with devastating air raids on cities using poison gas. The broad preparations for aerial and gas attack are well established, though some individual details in this montage have not been independently verified. [C-036]

That same day, Wednesday, 30 August, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax—the man who, two years earlier, had told Hitler that his demands were fundamentally understandable—writes to the British ambassador in Berlin. The exchange reads: Halifax: ‘We shall give careful consideration to German Government's reply, but it is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.’ Henderson: ‘I had made similar observation to Herr Hitler yesterday evening, his reply being that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in one and a half hours.’ [Q-040]

At this point, the British are desperate to avoid war, and they start putting enormous pressure on the Polish government. Don't provoke the Germans. Stop the Polish newspapers publishing articles against Germany. End the anti-German campaign. Send a plenipotentiary to Berlin at once and negotiate. The British ambassador goes back to Ribbentrop and tells him they're doing everything they can, urging the Polish government to make peace and reach an agreement. Ribbentrop replies that British advice has so far had “damned little effect.” The ambassador answers—and reports his reply to London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

And now another comedy begins, a kind of German black comedy. Ribbentrop says they had drawn up terms for an agreement with Poland. The British ambassador reports: “Herr von Ribbentrop's reply was to produce a lengthy document which he read out in German aloud at top speed. Imagining that he would eventually hand it to me I did not attempt to follow too closely the sixteen or more articles which it contained. [...] When I asked Herr von Ribbentrop for text of these proposals in accordance with undertaking in the German reply of yesterday, he asserted that it was now too late as Polish representative had not arrived in Berlin by midnight. [...] In the most violent terms Herr von Ribbentrop said that he would never ask the Ambassador to visit him. He hinted that if Polish Ambassador asked him for interview it might be different. [...] We parted on that note, but I must tell you that Herr von Ribbentrop's whole demeanour during an unpleasant interview was aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] The arbitrary deadline, rapid oral reading, and refusal to hand over the text obstructed genuine negotiation. [C-037]

## 14. 31 August–1 September: The Attack Order, Gleiwitz, and Diplomatic Lag

<!-- transcript: U-01015–U-01095; omissions: none -->

On 31 August, the British tell the Poles: send a plenipotentiary to Berlin, or at least send your ambassador to knock on the door and ask for the negotiating terms. The Poles say they won't send the ambassador. It would be too humiliating. At 12:40 p.m. on 31 August, Hitler signs the order to attack the next morning. At one in the afternoon, the Poles change their minds. Without telling the British, they instruct Józef Lipski, the Polish ambassador in Berlin, to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms. Lipski goes. Ribbentrop's account records that he asks Lipski whether he has authority to negotiate. Lipski says he doesn't. Ribbentrop then says he had expected Lipski to be authorized to negotiate and will report the démarche to Hitler. [Q-043] The decision to attack had already been made before this belated Polish approach. [C-038]

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. What makes it curious, above all, is this: after everything I've told you, no one today would imagine the Germans still needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. But they want one, and the German security services under Heydrich have already prepared it. Reinhard Heydrich, a senior SS figure and head of the security services, would be killed four years later by Czechoslovak agents in Prague.

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. The curious thing about Gleiwitz, above all, is this: after everything I've told you, no one today would believe the Germans still needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. But they do want a pretext, and the German secret services under Heydrich's command have already prepared it. Heydrich—you know, Reinhard Heydrich, an SS big shot and head of the security services, who would be killed four years later by Czechoslovak partisans in Prague—has had a commando waiting for some time, for two weeks, at Gleiwitz, a German radio station near the Polish border. The best reconstruction has five or six attackers in civilian clothes at Gleiwitz, not twelve men in Polish uniforms. [N-033] On the morning of 31 August, the moment arrives. Heydrich sends the commando the code phrase: “Grandmother died.” [Q-044]

The commando swings into action on German territory. They seize the Gleiwitz radio station, fire shots, and broadcast a rambling statement in Polish—as we used to say in the days of the Red Brigades. They leave a body behind, though the victim's precise identity and clothing are less certain than the traditional account suggests. The incident is then presented as a Polish attack on German territory. The Germans claim the right to invade Poland, and the invasion begins a few hours later, at dawn on 1 September. [C-039]

Meanwhile, during the night, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax still hopes genuine negotiations with Poland can begin. He is worried because the Poles don't want to send their ambassador. What he doesn't know is that the Polish ambassador had already gone the previous evening and been rebuffed. On the night of 1 September, Halifax sends instructions to the ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw—separate messages that Barbero presents together—and what follows is perhaps the most inane telegram ever sent by a British government. The exchange reads: “Halifax: 'I do not see why the Polish Government should feel difficulty about authorising Polish Ambassador to accept a document from the German Government, and I earnestly hope that they may be able to modify their instructions to him in this respect. [...] On the other hand, a refusal by them to receive proposals would be gravely misunderstood by outside opinion. [...] I should have thought that the Polish Ambassador could surely be instructed to receive and transmit a document [...]. If negotiations are initiated, His Majesty's Government will at all times be ready, if desired, to lend any assistance in their power to achieve a just settlement. [...] Please speak to M. Beck immediately in the above sense.' Kennard: 'Your telegram of 1st September was decyphered at 4 a. m. to-day. M. Lipski had already called on the German Foreign Minister at 6:30 p. m. yesterday. In view of this fact, which was followed by German invasion of Poland at dawn to-day, it was clearly useless for me to take the action suggested.'” [Q-045]

Halifax's instruction doesn't include the latest outcome. Events have already overtaken it. [C-040]

## 15. Delayed Declarations and Fascist Propaganda

<!-- transcript: U-01096–U-01117; omissions: none -->

Another two days pass before Britain and France declare war on Germany. They are still clinging to the mad, naïve, but sincere hope that somehow this catastrophe can be avoided. In the meantime, there are ultimatums and further diplomatic exchanges. Britain's ultimatum expires at eleven in the morning on 3 September, and France's at five that afternoon. [C-041]

Meanwhile, in Italy, Mussolini is calm. Hitler has accepted that Italy will stay out, so they wait to see whether, as Ciano puts it, they too can claim a share of the spoils. The Fascist government calls its position non-belligerency, not neutrality, and the calculation is conditional rather than a settled plan for annexation. Ciano had recorded the idea on 13 and 15 August: “13 August: 'The Duce's reactions are varied. At first he agrees with me. Then he says that honor obliges him to march with Germany. Finally he states that he wants his share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.' 15 August: 'For in his view it is still possible—though difficult—that the democracies will back down. In that case it would not suit us to have clashed with Germany, since we too must take our share of the spoils. We must therefore find a solution that allows us: (a) if the democracies attack, to disengage ourselves “honorably” from the Germans; (b) if the democracies swallow it, to seize the opportunity to settle accounts with Belgrade once and for all.'” [Q-046] [C-042]

Naturally, the Italian press receives its instructions. It doesn't report any of this. It has to say something quite different. So I'll close with the *Corriere della Sera* of 1 September: “THE DECISIVE HOUR STRIKES / Hitler's proposals for Danzig and the Corridor—loyal, reasonable, and eminently feasible—foolishly allowed to lapse by Warsaw and London / England and company nailed to their tremendous responsibilities / The publication of this page of history confirms Germany's conscious wisdom and moderation in the face of the blind intransigence of the other parties to the conflict” [Q-047]. These are the newspapers our fathers were reading in this happy country of ours in 1939.

Thank you.
