## 5. Munich, peace at any price, and appeasement’s failure

<!-- transcript: U-00257–U-00358; omissions: none -->

Finally, we reach September. The situation is now so serious that the British and French governments decide they have to force the Czechoslovaks to make concessions to Hitler. They need to come up with something. How about a conference? Today we’d call it bringing everyone to the negotiating table. Back then, they haven’t invented the “table” yet—or the “summit.” Let’s hold a conference, they say.

How do they persuade Hitler to negotiate? Ask Mussolini, since the two men are friends. The British and French approach Mussolini and tell him, in essence—I’m summarizing here—to sound Hitler out. See if he’ll agree to bring everyone together and work out a deal that meets Germany’s demands on Czechoslovakia. Hitler agrees.

On September 29 and 30, 1938, we come to an episode that will go down as one of the low points of European politics, though at least two of the four people involved are acting in good faith: the Munich Conference. Four leaders meet in Munich, in Bavaria: Hitler, Mussolini, the British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, and the French prime minister, Daladier. [C-012]

At least Chamberlain and Daladier are acting in good faith. It’s hard to doubt that. They’re haunted by the same nightmare as the entire generation that lived through the First World War. Whenever a crisis erupts, the politicians of this era—the generation that witnessed that slaughter—have only one thought: anything but another war. Never again, no matter the price. Or, if you prefer: “our policy was the policy of peace at any price.” [Q-012] [C-013]

What does “any price” mean? Well, if Czechoslovakia has to give Germany a piece of its territory, so be it. That’s better than war. At the Munich Conference, Britain, France, Italy, and Germany meet to decide which part of Czechoslovakia Germany will get. The Czechoslovaks wait outside the door to find out what the others have decided. And the decision is this: the entire German-populated region, the Sudetenland, goes to Germany.

Hitler takes note. Not only do they stay out of my way; they actually help me take a piece of Czechoslovakia.

And here—I don’t know. It’s hard to talk about, because part of you wants to say what people said for fifty years: what a tragedy, to have politicians of that caliber. And yet these are first-rate politicians. They simply don’t understand what’s happening, and they’re acting in good faith.

Chamberlain returns to London, and people have mocked this homecoming from Munich ever since. As he comes down the airplane steps, he’s already waving the piece of paper with the agreements on it. Picture the scene: the photographers, the crowd, everyone gathered around. Chamberlain waves the paper and addresses them.

Here’s Chamberlain’s speech to the crowd that day, as he steps off the plane from Munich: “The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is, in my view, only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace. [...] We regard the agreement signed last night and the Anglo-German naval agreement as symbolic of the desire of our two peoples never to go to war with one another again. [...] My good friends, this is the second time in our history that there has come back from Germany to Downing Street peace with honour. I believe it is peace for our time. [...] And now I recommend you to go home and sleep quietly in your beds.” Remember, the Second World War is only eleven months away. That’s what Chamberlain tells the London crowd when he returns from Munich. [Q-013]

The Second World War is eleven months away. Keep that in mind. Chamberlain says he met again that morning with the German chancellor, Herr Hitler, and here is the treaty with both their signatures. They regard the agreement signed the night before as a symbol of their peoples’ desire never to go to war with each other again. My good friends, the British prime minister has come home from Germany with peace and honor. He believes it is peace for their time. Go home and sleep soundly. That’s his message to the London crowd when he returns from Munich.

Chamberlain’s policy has a specific name. It later becomes an ugly word in British politics—we’ll hear it again in the next episode, when we talk about Mrs. Thatcher: *appeasement*. Pacification. Peace at any price.

And today, *appeasement* really is shocking. They don’t understand. No, they simply don’t understand. The tragic irony is that they’re willing to do anything to keep war from breaking out. And the result is that they cause it to break out. [C-014]

Mind you, Hitler is good at this. He’s extraordinarily skilled at running people in circles and deceiving them. At Munich, Hitler tells Chamberlain—and listen to this; Chamberlain himself reports it, apparently. Chamberlain says: I spoke with Hitler, and Hitler told me with great sincerity: “In the first place he repeated to me with great earnestness what he had said already at Berchtesgaden, namely, that this was the last of his territorial ambitions in Europe and that he had no wish to include in the Reich people of other races than Germans.” [Q-014]

Hitler said it, and he sounds so sincere. So go ahead: sleep peacefully.
