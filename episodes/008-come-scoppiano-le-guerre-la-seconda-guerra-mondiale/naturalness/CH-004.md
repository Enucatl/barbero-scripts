## 4. Sudeten crisis and the exclusion of the Soviet Union

<!-- transcript: U-00212–U-00256; omissions: none -->

Hitler summons the leader of the Czechoslovak Nazis. Because Czechoslovakia, too, has a Nazi party among the German minority in the Sudetenland. That's what they're called—I can never remember why—but the Germans of Czechoslovakia are known as the Sudeten Germans.

The Germans in Czechoslovakia have a Nazi party that takes its orders from Hitler, and in March 1938 Hitler gives the signal: start the street violence, the agitation, the terrorism. Czechoslovakia begins to descend into chaos. [C-009]

The German press launches an extremely aggressive campaign. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are being oppressed; they're a persecuted minority, a downtrodden minority. The criminal Czechoslovak police arrest them, rape them, torture them. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are suffering; this is intolerable. [Q-010]

Acting on instructions from their government, the British ambassadors in Berlin and Prague say: well, after all, the Germans aren't entirely wrong; you can understand their position. They keep saying that Germany's claims are, in the end, justified, and that the Czechoslovak government must try to meet the Germans halfway. We certainly don't want to unleash another war, do we? So the Czechoslovak government had better make concessions. [C-010]

At that moment, as the Nazis in Czechoslovakia begin playing the same game they played in Austria, only one country takes a public stand and says that all the powers should come together to guarantee Czechoslovakia. That country is the Soviet Union.

And of course nobody listens, because the Soviet Union is the Soviet Union—the Bolshevik, communist country—and it has no place in the game. During those weeks, the British ambassador in Moscow sends a report to London that concludes, and I quote: “The Soviet Union must […] for the time being, be counted out of European politics in so far as the exercise of a decisive influence one way or the other is concerned.” [Q-011] Russia can be treated as though it doesn't exist. [C-011]

Notice again how hard they find it to grasp that the world is changing. They go on talking about “European politics” as though politics were still always made by Britain and France. No: the United States isn't there, Japan isn't there, the Soviet Union isn't there—nothing. It's still European politics as usual, and Russia doesn't count.

This goes on for months. Czechoslovakia sinks into chaos, the German press pounds away, pounds away, pounds away at the suffering of the Sudeten Germans, and Britain and France keep pressing the government in Prague: come on, make some concessions.
