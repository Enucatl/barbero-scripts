## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Ten days later, on March 26, Hitler receives the Polish government’s formal rejection of Germany’s demand to bring the Free City of Danzig into the Reich. Germany has already been pressuring Poland over Danzig for months. At the end of April 1939, Hitler speaks to the Reichstag. Three months earlier, standing in that very same place, he had called German-Polish friendship one of the reassuring features of European political life. Now, three months later, he publicly makes the case for Germany’s broader territorial demands and addresses Poland’s access to the sea: “This is, as I have emphasized before, perhaps the most difficult of all Germany’s problems. [...] I saw the peaceful resolution of this question as a further contribution to a lasting relaxation of tensions in Europe. [...] The Polish government rejected this offer of mine. [...] I sincerely regretted this attitude of the Polish government, which I could not understand.” [Q-019] [C-019]

From that point on, tensions rise in Danzig and along its border with Poland. There are clashes, shootings, incidents every day. Nazi militias form inside Danzig, with a Polish customs post just outside. But this time, the British and French governments are starting to think that maybe they should do something. But what?

Britain and France propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain goes even further and gives Poland an official guarantee. Even though the treaty hasn’t been signed yet, Britain promises all the support it can provide if an action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland decides it has to resist. France takes the same position. Looking back now, you might say: well, it’s about time. What else could they do? [C-020]

At the time, though, selling this to the public is extremely difficult. Danzig? We’re supposed to go to war over Danzig? The French newspaper *L’Œuvre* runs a famous headline: “Morire per Danzica?”—“Die for Danzig?” No. The slogan doesn’t speak for everyone in Britain and France, but it becomes influential. Still, now that the French and British governments have discovered Hitler might not be trustworthy, they press ahead. And in April and May 1939, they do something else unheard-of. [Q-020] [C-020]

They start looking for some kind of agreement with the Soviet Union. The negotiations go on for months as they try to work out a military-defense pact in case war breaks out. It’s a sad, depressing story, because you can’t help thinking: if only they had reached that agreement. But no one seems to want it badly enough. They’re all afraid of one another, and there’s mistrust and obstruction on every side. Still, the telegrams and replies reveal a striking pattern. Again and again, Britain and France make a proposal, and the Russians answer the next day. Then weeks go by—week after week—before the British and French finally take the next step. The Russians answer the next day, and the negotiations keep dragging on. [C-021]

Meanwhile, Stalin is taking notes. They’re asking me to negotiate, but do they mean it? Or are they trying to pull me in, only to leave me alone with Germany when the decisive moment comes? Stalin decides the risk is too great. He’s negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French don’t seem very committed.

Stalin lets the Germans know that even though their countries are enemies—one is communist, the other is, well...—they do have business interests in common. Maybe it would be a good idea to start negotiating a trade agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The Germans are interested. And so, over the summer, Stalin quietly works both sides, negotiating with Britain and France at one table while exploring an agreement with Germany at another. The German track starts with uneven economic contacts, develops into a political channel later, and accelerates sharply in August. Stalin waits to see what happens. [C-021]
