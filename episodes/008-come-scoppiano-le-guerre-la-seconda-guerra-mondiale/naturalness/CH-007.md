## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Ten days later, on 26 March, Hitler receives the Polish government’s formal rejection of Germany’s demand to incorporate the Free City of Danzig into the Reich—although German pressure over Danzig has actually been building for months. At the end of April 1939, Hitler addresses the Reichstag. Three months earlier, in the same place, he has called German-Polish friendship one of the reassuring features of European political life. Now, three months later, he publicly presents his case like this: “This is, as I have emphasized before, perhaps the most difficult of all Germany’s problems. [...] I saw the peaceful resolution of this question as a further contribution to a lasting relaxation of tensions in Europe. [...] The Polish government rejected this offer of mine. [...] I sincerely regretted this attitude of the Polish government, which I could not understand.” The opening sentence refers to the broader territorial settlement and Poland’s access to the sea, not just to Danzig; this is Hitler’s public justification for Germany’s demands, not a neutral account of them. [Q-019] [C-019]

From this point on, the conflict intensifies in Danzig and along the border between Danzig and Poland: incidents, shootings. Nazi militias form in Danzig; just outside is the Polish customs post. Incidents keep happening. But this time, the British and French governments are beginning to think that perhaps they ought to do something. What?

Britain and France propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain even goes so far as to issue an official guarantee. No treaty has yet been signed, but Britain—with France taking the same position—will give Poland all the support in its power if an action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland considers it vital to resist. Looking back today, of course, you say: about time. What else could they do? [C-020]

At the time, though, this is very hard to sell to the public. Danzig? Are we really supposed to go to war over Danzig? A famous headline appears in the French newspaper *L’Œuvre*: “Morire per Danzica?”—“Die for Danzig?” No. It doesn’t represent all British and French opinion, but the slogan becomes influential. Even so, the French and British governments—undaunted, now that they’ve discovered Hitler may not be trustworthy—do something else unprecedented in April and May 1939. [Q-020] [C-020]

They begin exploring whether some kind of agreement can be reached with the Soviet Union. Negotiations begin and drag on for months, as they try to work out whether a military defense agreement can be concluded in the event of war. The story of these negotiations is sad and depressing, because you can’t help thinking: if only they had reached that agreement. Instead, it seems that no one wants it quite enough; they’re all afraid of one another. One apparently objective pattern emerges from the telegrams and replies: whenever Britain and France make a proposal to the Russians, the Russians answer the next day. Then weeks go by—weeks and weeks—before the British and French finally take the next step. The Russians answer the next day, and the negotiations drag on. This is an illustrative pattern, not an exact rule for every single exchange, and there is mistrust and obstruction on all sides. [C-021]

Meanwhile, Stalin takes note. They’re asking me to negotiate—but do they mean it? Or are they trying to draw me in, only to leave me facing Germany alone when the decisive moment comes? Stalin takes note and decides the risk is too great. He is negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French seem decidedly lukewarm.

Stalin lets the Germans know that, although the two countries are enemies—one communist, the other, well...—they do have commercial interests. So perhaps it would be a good idea to open negotiations for a trade agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The Germans are willing. And over the summer, without anyone knowing, a situation develops in which Stalin is negotiating with Britain and France at one table and with Germany at another, waiting to see what happens. More precisely, economic contacts revive unevenly and only later develop into a political channel, before accelerating dramatically in August. [C-021]
