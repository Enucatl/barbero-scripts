## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

<!-- transcript: U-00001–U-00103; omissions: none -->

Well, good evening. We have quite a journey ahead of us tonight, and it is, how shall I put it, a pretty grim one. You may say last night was no picnic either, but the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story. The only comic relief comes from Italy's part in it. Whatever you may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and the rest of them, they provide the only comedy in those otherwise very dramatic weeks, as you'll see.

So how does the Second World War break out? People have written that it really begins in 1919, with the signing of the Treaty of Versailles. The treaty that ends the First World War does so by creating endless grievances and frustrations. You'll remember that even Italy, which had after all gained Trento and Trieste and swallowed up South Tyrol, is not satisfied. Quite the opposite: Italians speak of the “mutilated victory”: “Our victory, you will not be mutilated. No one can break your knees or clip your wings. Where are you running? Where are you rising?” [Q-001] Even victorious Italy then comes close to sinking into civil war and falls into dictatorship, all because of a war that ended badly. Imagine how the defeated countries feel. [C-001]

Germany, having lost, emerges from the First World War and the Treaty of Versailles frustrated, convinced that it has suffered an injustice. What injustice? For one thing, Germany has been forced to give territory to a new country. Versailles creates many new states, including Poland. Germany gives Poland—or rather, is forced to give Poland—a large stretch of land inhabited by Germans. And on top of that, the treaty creates situations that are impossible to manage.

There is one city that means nothing to anyone today: Gdańsk, in Poland. We've hardly heard its name since the days of Lech Wałęsa, but back then people talk about it constantly. Danzig is a major port, an important German city, right on the border between the new Poland and Germany. Both countries claim it. The victors at Versailles decide: all right, let's make it a Free City, independent, neither German nor Polish. Danzig is inhabited entirely by Germans who want to belong to Germany, but they aren't allowed to.

Straight away, a public campaign begins in Germany, rather like the one in prewar Italy over Trento, Trieste, irredentism, and the separated brothers. Germans campaign on the slogan: Danzig, Danzig is German, Danzig is German and they didn't give it to us. It's a disgrace. One day we'll have to reopen the question. [Q-002]

Then there are all the other problems created by the end of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The empire is dissolved and many new states appear: Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia. And they bring plenty of problems with them. Austria is small and entirely German-speaking. Most Austrians would have liked to say: all right, now things have turned out this way, let's join Germany; we Austrians are Germans too. But the victors won't allow it. Austria has to remain independent.

Then there's Czechoslovakia, the state of the Czechs and Slovaks. But under the Austrian Empire, a great many Germans had lived in those regions—that is, Austrian citizens who spoke German. Now they find themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, but they don't feel Czechoslovak. So Versailles leaves behind this whole collection of problems, problems that are understandable in themselves. To a large extent, they're the problems of minorities living outside their own country.

Tonight I'll have to spend a while on the deeper causes of the Second World War. I would have liked to say: right, it's 10 August 1939, and this is what happens. We'll get there, but first we need to talk a little about those deeper causes, or none of what follows will make sense. And those causes are precisely the ones I've just described: a frustrated Germany with claims against Poland over Danzig; against Austria, because the Austrians are all Germans and it's hard to see why they shouldn't belong to Germany; and against Czechoslovakia, because of the Germans who live there and feel discriminated against.

In 1933 the Nazis win the elections in Germany and Hitler comes to power. Hitler takes power with a program containing many fine promises, but the point Germans perhaps feel most strongly is this: we must revise the Treaty of Versailles; we must revise the Europe that came out of Versailles. It's an unjust Europe, an iniquitous treaty. Germany has suffered, and that's not right. We must reopen the issue and change the borders. [C-002]

At heart, the story of how the Second World War breaks out is the story of two illusions. The first is the illusion of the great democratic powers, Britain and France: that Hitler can be given some satisfaction because, on certain points, he is right after all. They imagine they can say, very well, let's negotiate, let's reach an agreement—Danzig here, and so on—without causing any great upheaval. An illusion, as we'll see.

The other illusion is Hitler's: that he can take everything back, and a little more besides, and turn Germany once again into Europe's great power without anyone seriously standing in his way. The democracies' illusion—the illusion of the Western democracies, France and Britain. [C-003]
