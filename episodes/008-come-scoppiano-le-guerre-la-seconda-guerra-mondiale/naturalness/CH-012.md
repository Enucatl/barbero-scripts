## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

<!-- transcript: U-00881–U-00942; omissions: none -->

That day, Hitler receives the British ambassador, because his one remaining concern is to keep Britain out. He tells him about the atrocities the Poles are supposedly committing against the German minority in Poland. Now, here's a small detail I could have left out, like so many others. But even tonight, I haven't had the courage to cut too many details. I'll have to hurry, because I'm taking far too long. In any case, this is a morbid conversation between Hitler and the British ambassador. Henderson's report says: “He then violently attacked the Poles, talked of 100,000 German refugees from Poland, excesses against Germans, closing of German institutions and Polish systematic persecution of German nationals generally. [...] He had mentioned the castration of Germans. I happened to be aware of one case. The German in question was a sex-maniac, who had been treated as he deserved. Herr Hitler's retort was that there had not been one case but six.” [Q-034]

And on they go. Hitler also tells him that Poland is the last issue. Henderson reports Hitler's words like this: “Among various points mentioned by Herr Hitler were: [...] that he was by nature an artist not a politician, and that once the Polish question was settled he would end his life as an artist and not as a war-monger; [...] that once the Polish question was settled he himself would settle down.” [Q-035] The ambassador reports all this to London.

Friday, 25 August. Hitler orders the attack for the next day. Then a letter arrives from Mussolini, and for the first time Mussolini tells him the truth: Italy isn't ready for war. But he still doesn't have the courage to tell him the whole truth. Mussolini writes: “If Germany attacks Poland and Poland's allies counterattack Germany, I put before you the advisability of my not taking the initiative in military operations, given the present condition of Italian military preparations, which has repeatedly and promptly been made known to you, Führer, and to von Ribbentrop. Our intervention can nevertheless be immediate if Germany immediately gives us the war materiel and raw materials needed to withstand the blow that the French and British will direct mainly against us. In our meetings war had been envisaged for after 1942, and by then I would have been ready on land, at sea, and in the air, according to the agreed plans.” [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He postpones the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what raw materials Italy needs. Mussolini's letter was one immediate factor in the postponement, alongside the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance and Hitler's continuing attempt to detach Britain from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, 26 August. Mussolini and the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces meet at Palazzo Venezia to draw up the list of raw materials they will ask Germany for. Ciano has already primed the generals: when you make this list, no criminal optimism. In other words, don't hold back. They draw up the list, and I'll read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—Italy's entire production for two years, just to give you an idea—two million tonnes of steel, Italy's production for a year; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; and then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rosin, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. The nominal requirement was for sustaining a twelve-month war. The list went down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to Attolico, the Italian ambassador in Berlin, and he takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks at the list and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” Panic. No one has told Attolico anything, and he has no instructions. But he understands the situation and improvises: “Immediately.” It was calculated that 17,000 trains would be needed to send all that material to Italy—a contemporary estimate by Ciano, not an independently audited conversion. [C-034]

Ribbentrop goes to Hitler, and at last they understand. The penny finally drops: Italy isn't going to take part. Italy will stay out. Very well, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini: “I have received the information concerning your final attitude. I respect the motives and influences determining your resolution. Perhaps it will nevertheless be for the best. Of course, in my opinion, this will be true only if the world does not receive any inkling of the intended attitude of Italy, at least not till actual fighting starts. [...] You and your people could help me most in this hard struggle by supporting me with Italian manpower, manpower for industrial as well as agricultural purposes.” [Q-038]

And here, quietly, another long story begins to unfold. It shows you how circumstances change. The Germans suddenly realize they can't count on Italy for military help, but they can count on Italian workers. Organized Italian labor migration to Germany had already begun in 1938; this request expanded an existing flow that would become increasingly coercive, distinct from the mass forced labor of Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]
