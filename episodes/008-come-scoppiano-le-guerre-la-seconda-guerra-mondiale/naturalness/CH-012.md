## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

<!-- transcript: U-00881–U-00942; omissions: none -->

That day, Hitler meets with the British ambassador. His one remaining concern is keeping Britain out of the war. He tells the ambassador about the atrocities the Poles are supposedly committing against the German minority in Poland. Now, this next detail is one I could have left out, along with many others. But once again, I couldn't quite bring myself to cut too much. I'll have to speed up; I'm taking too long. In any case, the conversation between Hitler and the British ambassador gets downright morbid. Henderson's report says: “He then violently attacked the Poles, talked of 100,000 German refugees from Poland, excesses against Germans, closing of German institutions and Polish systematic persecution of German nationals generally. [...] He had mentioned the castration of Germans. I happened to be aware of one case. The German in question was a sex-maniac, who had been treated as he deserved. Herr Hitler's retort was that there had not been one case but six.” [Q-034]

And on they go. Hitler also tells him that Poland is the last issue. Henderson reports Hitler's words this way: “Among various points mentioned by Herr Hitler were: [...] that he was by nature an artist not a politician, and that once the Polish question was settled he would end his life as an artist and not as a war-monger; [...] that once the Polish question was settled he himself would settle down.” [Q-035] And the ambassador sends that back to London.

Friday, August 25. Hitler orders the attack for the next day. Then a letter arrives from Mussolini, who finally tells him the truth: Italy isn't ready for war. But Mussolini still doesn't have the nerve to tell him the whole truth. He writes: “If Germany attacks Poland and Poland's allies counterattack Germany, I put before you the advisability of my not taking the initiative in military operations, given the present condition of Italian military preparations, which has repeatedly and promptly been made known to you, Führer, and to von Ribbentrop. Our intervention can nevertheless be immediate if Germany immediately gives us the war materiel and raw materials needed to withstand the blow that the French and British will direct mainly against us. In our meetings war had been envisaged for after 1942, and by then I would have been ready on land, at sea, and in the air, according to the agreed plans.” [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He puts off the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what raw materials Italy needs. Mussolini's letter helps trigger the delay. So does the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance, along with Hitler's continued effort to pry Britain away from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, August 26. Mussolini meets at Palazzo Venezia with the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces. They're there to draw up a list of the raw materials Italy will ask Germany for. Ciano has already coached the generals: no criminal optimism when you make this list. In other words, don't hold back. So they make the list, and I'll read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—to give you some idea, that's two years' worth of Italian production; two million tonnes of steel, a full year of Italian production; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rosin, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. On paper, this is what Italy needs to fight for twelve months. The list goes down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to Attolico, the Italian ambassador in Berlin, and he takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks it over and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” Now Attolico is in trouble. No one has told him what to say, and he has no instructions. But he understands what they're trying to do, so he improvises: “Immediately.” Ciano estimates that it would take 17,000 trains to ship all of it to Italy. That's his contemporary estimate, not a modern calculation. [C-034]

Ribbentrop takes the list to Hitler, and at last they get it. The penny drops: Italy isn't coming in. Italy is staying out. Fine, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini: “I have received the information concerning your final attitude. I respect the motives and influences determining your resolution. Perhaps it will nevertheless be for the best. Of course, in my opinion, this will be true only if the world does not receive any inkling of the intended attitude of Italy, at least not till actual fighting starts. [...] You and your people could help me most in this hard struggle by supporting me with Italian manpower, manpower for industrial as well as agricultural purposes.” [Q-038]

And just like that, almost in the background, a much longer story begins to unfold. It shows you how quickly things change. The Germans suddenly realize they can't count on military help from Italy, but they can count on Italian workers. Organized labor migration from Italy to Germany had already begun in 1938. This request expands that flow, which will become increasingly coercive. It is separate from the mass forced labor imposed on Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]
