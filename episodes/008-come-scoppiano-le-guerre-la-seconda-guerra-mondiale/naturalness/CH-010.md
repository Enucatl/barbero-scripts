## 10. 12–18 August: rival negotiations and Polish refusal

<!-- transcript: U-00596–U-00727; omissions: none -->

The day after Ciano’s meeting with Ribbentrop is Saturday, 12 August. It’s the Ferragosto holiday season; this war, like the First World War, breaks out in the middle of summer. People don’t take as many holidays as they do now, but they do take some, and at Ferragosto they go away. On Saturday, 12 August, for example, Milan empties out: half a million people take the train to the seaside.

That same day, after months of talk, the Anglo-French delegation finally arrives in Moscow to negotiate a treaty. The Soviets are deeply disappointed. The delegation consists of a French general, Joseph Doumenc, and a British admiral, Reginald Drax—neither of them at the very highest level. The Russians ask, “Do you have full powers to sign a treaty?”

Let me stop for a moment. This question of full powers is extremely important if we want to understand diplomatic exchanges in this period. Of course, the telephone, the telegraph, and the airplane all exist. Even so, everyone considers it essential to have someone there in person who can sign. So the Soviets ask: “You’ve come to negotiate—excellent. This is urgent. Do you have full powers?” Drax can negotiate, but he can’t sign. Doumenc has Daladier’s authority to conclude a military convention, although he doesn’t carry the usual plenipotentiary credentials and remains dependent on Paris. In practical terms, the answer is: “You tell us, and we’ll report back.” The Soviets are deeply disappointed. [C-025]

That same day, the Soviet government sends an invitation: it would be a good idea for Ribbentrop to come to Moscow, because they have things to discuss. Sunday, 13 August: the height of summer, the holiday season, the Venice Film Festival; Maria Bellonci wins the Viareggio Prize for *Lucrezia Borgia*.

That same day, Hitler speaks with Mussolini. It is exactly as Ciano has said: Germany is going to make war on Poland, and will attack at the first incident. And that isn’t all. Hitler explains that it is now mid-August. His generals calculate that it will take a month and a half—six weeks at most—to defeat Poland. By mid-October, autumn will arrive, and Poland’s roads and airfields will be unusable because of the mud. So the campaign has to be over by mid-October. They need a month and a half, which means they have to attack by the end of August. This is Hitler’s operational estimate and the reasoning behind his timetable. [C-026]

Mussolini and the others in Italy are appalled. In his diary entry for 13 August, Ciano writes: “I return to Rome disgusted with Germany, its leaders, and their way of acting. They have deceived us and lied to us. And today they are about to drag us into an adventure that we did not want and that could imperil the regime and the country.” [Q-025]

Monday, 14 August: the holiday atmosphere continues, with the Salzburg Festival under way. Hitler, who is up in the mountains, goes to the festival to see Mozart’s *The Abduction from the Seraglio*.

In Moscow, the Russians meet the Anglo-French delegation and put their cards on the table. Do they want an agreement to defend Poland? Very well. But the Soviets want a guarantee that the agreement will be taken seriously: if war comes, the Poles must allow Soviet troops onto Polish territory. Are the Soviets acting in good faith or bad faith? Are they already thinking about swallowing Poland, or do they sincerely believe that, if a war has to be fought, it has to be fought seriously? We don’t know. In any case, that is their demand. And what do the British admiral and the French general say? “That isn’t within our authority. We have to report back. We’ll report to Paris and London, and then we’ll see.” The transit issue is the immediate impasse, but it isn’t the only reason the talks fail: Anglo-French delays and limited mandates, Polish fear of Soviet occupation, Soviet bargaining, and the German option all play a part. [C-027]

That same Monday, 14 August, Ribbentrop decides that the Russian suggestion that he visit Moscow is a good one. He’ll go on Tuesday, 15 August—Ferragosto. The French general has written that, before the agreement can be signed, the Russians want a guarantee that the Poles will let them enter if war breaks out. So the Poles have to be asked.

The French foreign minister, Georges Bonnet, tries to find the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz. And where do you think the Polish ambassador is on Ferragosto? At the seaside. They bring him back to Paris and tell him: allow the Russians through if there is a war against Germany. The ambassador says he’ll have to ask Warsaw, but in his view the answer will be no, because the Poles are even more afraid of the Russians than they are of the Germans.

Bonnet warns the Polish ambassador that the French understand Hitler has said he will dispose of Poland in three weeks. The ambassador’s reply is important if we want to understand the atmosphere, and the kind of regime Poland has. Bonnet later recalls Łukasiewicz answering: “On the contrary, it is the Polish army that will invade Germany from the very first days.” [Q-026]

Wednesday, 16 August. Ribbentrop writes to Moscow: “I am coming.” This is the first time they put it in writing that he will make the journey to sign a treaty with the Soviets. Meanwhile, in Berlin, the Germans are beginning to worry about one thing. Those of you who were here last night will forgive me: history does sometimes repeat itself. The Germans are asking themselves: what will Britain do?

On the evening of 15 August, the British ambassador, Sir Nevile Henderson, visits State Secretary Ernst von Weizsäcker at the German Foreign Ministry. The German argument goes like this: surely Britain isn’t going to support Poland? Look how mad these Poles are, with all their provocations. The German minority in Poland is being oppressed; it’s all the fault of those unreasonable Poles. Surely the British aren’t prepared to help a bunch of madmen?

Henderson answers: “I told Baron von Weizsäcker that the last was a very dangerous theory and sounded like Herr von Ribbentrop who had never been able to understand the British mentality. If the Poles were compelled by any act of Germany to resort to arms to defend themselves there was not a shadow of doubt that we would give them our full armed support. We had made that abundantly clear and Germany would be making a tragic mistake if she imagined the contrary.” [Q-027] [C-028]

Thursday, 17 August. Incidents multiply along the German-Polish border: shootings, people crossing the frontier. The German press launches a campaign—remember Czechoslovakia the year before?—about “Polish terror against the German minority”: arrests, murders. In Moscow, Ribbentrop’s reply arrives: he is willing to come and sign an agreement.

Friday, 18 August. As you know, Mussolini and Ciano are terrified because Italy isn’t ready for war. But the official line dictated to the Italian press is, of course, quite different. Italy has signed the Pact of Steel. The Italian newspapers have precise instructions: in this crisis, the Germans are right.

So let me read you the front-page display from the afternoon edition of the *Corriere della Sera* on 18 August:

“POLAND AT THE DECISIVE TURNING POINT OF ITS HISTORY / The tragic responsibility of the democracies / which unleashed and sustained Polish arrogance / Unshakable firmness of the Axis against the warmongering cloaked in hypocrisy by the encirclers” [Q-028]

Democracy, of course, is a dirty word in Fascist Italy. The “encirclers” are none other than the “demo-pluto-Jewish-ocracies”: France, Britain, and the Soviet Union, which supposedly have the peoples under siege. Well, you know; we know this story in Italy.

That same Friday, 18 August, the Polish government has to decide whether to enter an alliance with the Soviet Union and allow Soviet troops through its territory. The Polish government says no: “We have no military agreements with the Soviet Union and do not wish to have any.”

There is also a famous remark that later becomes proverbial, attributed—though not preserved in any secure contemporary transcript—to Poland’s commander-in-chief, Marshal Edward Śmigły-Rydz: “With the Germans we may lose our freedom, with the Russians we shall lose our soul.” [Q-029] The remark is generally dated to around 19–20 August, rather than to the government’s decision on the eighteenth. And so there is no agreement. [C-027]
