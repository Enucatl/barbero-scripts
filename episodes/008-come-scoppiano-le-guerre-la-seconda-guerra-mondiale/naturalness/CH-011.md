## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, August 19, word reaches Moscow that the Poles don't want a deal with Russia, so the negotiations grind to a halt. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: all right, send Ribbentrop. How about the 26th or 27th?

Sunday, August 20: Hitler sends Stalin a telegram. Keep in mind, this is the first time in history that a Western head of government has written personally to the head of the Soviet government. Until now, the Soviet Union has been treated like a country with the plague. People deal with it only indirectly, under the table. And the first Western ruler to send Stalin a telegram is Hitler. On August 20 he tells him: “Hitler: 'I accept the draft of the non-aggression pact that your Foreign Minister, Herr Molotov, delivered but consider it urgently necessary to clarify the questions connected with it as soon as possible. [...] I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23.' Stalin: 'I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23.'” [Q-030]

Monday, August 21. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for some way to prevent the war. Then they think: why not do what we did last year? Hold another nice conference in Munich, get everyone around a table, and work something out. So they call Berlin, ask for Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and pitch this wonderful idea. According to Ciano's diary, Ribbentrop is nowhere to be found. He finally calls back that afternoon and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That same day, August 21, Stalin gets Hitler's telegram at three in the afternoon and answers at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let's stop here for a moment. This is a crucial decision, one that does a great deal to make everything that follows possible. And it comes as a complete shock. We need to be very clear about that because—and this is my opinion; some of you may disagree—for years we've been told that all totalitarian regimes were alike, that Stalin's Soviet Union and Hitler's Germany were essentially the same. That makes this agreement seem less surprising. But to people at the time, it is an enormous shock. Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union look like the two most bitterly opposed countries on earth. And they are. The pact shows the extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and cold-bloodedness of both the Nazis and Stalin and his inner circle. The world is stunned.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are dragging their feet and don't really want a deal. Fine, then he'll make one with the Nazis, just to spite them. He needs security. And let me add one detail that few people know, perhaps including many of you. It helps explain the situation in those days. Things are going very badly—as Ciano puts it, “we are heading for a fight”—and Stalin decides he needs security at any cost, even if that means making a pact with the devil. They won't honor the pact forever, but they need it right now. The Soviets distrust the West and need to avoid being isolated. Germany is also offering neutrality and territorial gains. [C-029]

So what's this piece of context that no one knows at the time and few people remember even today? At that very moment, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war against Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets, and it borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war is raging there, with entire army corps involved. Some estimates put the total number of dead and wounded at 70,000, though the figures are disputed. The two governments agree to keep the war quiet, so the rest of the world knows nothing about it. Who, after all, is paying attention to the border between Mongolia and Manchuria? But this is a real war. The Japanese move from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. And the Soviets, by the way, win decisively. On the 21st, with the battle still raging, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany. He has to secure at least one border. The fighting shapes his calculation, though it isn't the only reason for his decision. [C-030]

The news becomes public that same day. Mussolini and Ciano are as stunned as everyone else by the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. Stunned, yes, but also impressed: these Germans are good. Ciano writes in his diary: “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke. The European situation has been overturned. Can France and England, which based their entire anti-Axis policy on alliance with the Soviets, still count on the unconditional support of the extremist masses? And will the encirclement system built through the small states still hold now that its Moscow cornerstone has collapsed? In any case, we should not rush our decisions: wait, and if possible remain ready to take our own share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.” [Q-031]

That final thought tells you everything about the regime's language and its moral standards.

On Tuesday, August 22, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. He says the time is right, just as it was in 1914. But here's the interesting part: he isn't talking about whether the armies are ready. He has something else in mind. In this famous speech to his generals on August 22, Hitler says: “My own personality and that of Mussolini. Essentially it depends on me, my existence, because of my political activities. Furthermore the fact that probably no one will ever again have the confidence of the whole German people as I do. There will probably never again be a man in the future with more authority than I have. My existence is therefore a factor of great value. But I can be eliminated at any time by a criminal or an idiot. The second personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive. If something happens to him, Italy's loyalty to the alliance will no longer be certain. [...] The Duce is the man with the strongest nerves in Italy.” [Q-032] Hitler's whole political world revolves around terrorism and assassination. To him, getting killed is simply something that can happen. So he has to start the war while he's still alive—and while Mussolini is still around. Hitler still trusts his old friend. He is fooling himself about Mussolini at the very moment Mussolini has already decided to back out. [C-031]

Wednesday, August 23. Newspapers around the world announce the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. The *Corriere della Sera* proclaims: “THE GERMAN-SOVIET AGREEMENT / The Axis's great political success / has broken the murky manoeuvre of the democratic encirclers / A completely new situation, rich in highly important developments - Von Ribbentrop has left by air for Moscow - The enormous worldwide response - Astonishment and dismay in the anti-totalitarian countries - Poland will have to seek its salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany” [Q-033]. “Anti-totalitarian,” of course, is an insult. *We* are totalitarian—Mussolini invented the whole idea of the totalitarian state—while those spineless French and the rest are “anti-totalitarian.”

Poland is starting to give in and wonder whether a deal with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible. But it's too late. On August 23, Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the non-aggression pact, which is announced publicly. He also signs a secret protocol, which of course is not made public. In it, Germany and the Soviet Union divide Polish territory into spheres, effectively setting up the partition of Poland while leaving the final borders unsettled. [C-032]
