## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, 19 August, word reaches Moscow that the Poles don't want an agreement with Russia, and the negotiations grind to a halt. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: at this point, let Ribbentrop come. How about the 26th or 27th?

Sunday, 20 August: a telegram arrives from Hitler to Stalin. Remember, this is the first time in history that a Western head of government has written personally to the head of the Soviet government. Until then, the Soviet Union had been treated like a plague-stricken country. People spoke to it only indirectly, under the table. And the first telegram Stalin receives from a Western ruler comes from Hitler. On 20 August, Hitler tells him: “Hitler: 'I accept the draft of the non-aggression pact that your Foreign Minister, Herr Molotov, delivered but consider it urgently necessary to clarify the questions connected with it as soon as possible. [...] I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23.' Stalin: 'I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23.'” [Q-030]

Monday, 21 August. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for some way to prevent war. Then it occurs to them: why not do what they did the year before? Hold another nice conference in Munich, an international conference where everyone can come to an agreement. So they call Berlin, ask for Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and put forward this splendid idea. According to Ciano's diary, Ribbentrop is nowhere to be found. Finally, that afternoon, he calls back and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That very day, 21 August, Stalin receives Hitler's telegram at three in the afternoon and replies at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let's stop here for a moment. This is a crucial decision, one that does a great deal to make what follows possible. It is completely unexpected, and it comes as a shock. We need to be very clear about this because—and here I'll give you my opinion; some people will disagree—for years we've been brainwashed with the idea that all totalitarian regimes were alike, that Stalin's Soviet Union and Hitler's Germany were the same thing. If you believe that, their agreement seems less strange. But for people at the time, the reality is an enormous shock. Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union look like the two most bitterly opposed, most hostile countries in existence. And they are. This agreement is proof of the extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and coldness of both the Nazis and Stalin and his circle. The world is stunned.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are too hesitant, that they don't really want an agreement, so he'll make one with the Nazis instead, just to spite them. Because he needs security. Let me add one small thing, something few people know, and perhaps many of you don't: a small piece of the context of those days. Stalin sees that things are going very badly—as Ciano says, “we are heading for a fight”—and decides he absolutely needs security, even if that means making a pact with the devil. They won't keep the pact, but for now they need it. Soviet distrust and the need to avoid immediate isolation were important motives, alongside the neutrality and territorial advantages offered by Germany. [C-029]

So what is this piece of context that no one knows at the time, and that few people remember even today? At that very moment, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war with Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets, and it borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war involving entire army corps is raging there. Barbero gives a total of 70,000 dead and wounded, though casualty totals are disputed. The two governments agree to keep it from the world, and the world hears nothing about it—still less about some border between Mongolia and Manchuria. But this is a full-blown war. The Japanese advance from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. And the Soviets, incidentally, win decisively. On the 21st, while the battle is still raging, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany because he has to secure at least one frontier. This fighting influenced his calculation, although the evidence does not reduce his decision to that single consideration. [C-030]

That same day, the news becomes public. Mussolini and Ciano, like the rest of the world, are stunned by the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. Stunned, but impressed too: these Germans are formidable. Ciano writes in his diary: “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke. The European situation has been overturned. Can France and England, which based their entire anti-Axis policy on alliance with the Soviets, still count on the unconditional support of the extremist masses? And will the encirclement system built through the small states still hold now that its Moscow cornerstone has collapsed? In any case, we should not rush our decisions: wait, and if possible remain ready to take our own share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.” [Q-031]

That final thought tells you everything about the language and the moral level of the regime.

On Tuesday, 22 August, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. He says this is the right moment, just as it was in 1914. But here's the interesting part: he isn't talking about whether the armies are ready. He has something else in mind. In this famous speech to his generals on 22 August, Hitler says: “My own personality and that of Mussolini. Essentially it depends on me, my existence, because of my political activities. Furthermore the fact that probably no one will ever again have the confidence of the whole German people as I do. There will probably never again be a man in the future with more authority than I have. My existence is therefore a factor of great value. But I can be eliminated at any time by a criminal or an idiot. The second personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive. If something happens to him, Italy's loyalty to the alliance will no longer be certain. [...] The Duce is the man with the strongest nerves in Italy.” [Q-032] Hitler's political culture is steeped in political terrorism and assassination. To him, being killed is simply something that might happen. So the war has to begin while he's still alive, and while Mussolini is still there. Hitler still trusts his old friend and is fooling himself about Mussolini at the very moment Mussolini has already decided to pull out. [C-031]

Wednesday, 23 August. The agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union is announced in the world press. The *Corriere della Sera* proclaims: “THE GERMAN-SOVIET AGREEMENT / The Axis's great political success / has broken the murky manoeuvre of the democratic encirclers / A completely new situation, rich in highly important developments - Von Ribbentrop has left by air for Moscow - The enormous worldwide response - Astonishment and dismay in the anti-totalitarian countries - Poland will have to seek its salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany” [Q-033]. “Anti-totalitarian,” of course, is an insult. *We* are totalitarian—Mussolini invented the whole idea of a totalitarian country—while those feeble French and the rest are “anti-totalitarian.”

Poland is starting to give way, starting to think that perhaps an agreement with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible. But it's too late. On 23 August, Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the non-aggression pact, which is announced publicly. He also signs the secret protocol, which of course is not made public. In it, Germany and the Soviet Union establish spheres in Polish territory, essentially providing for the partition of Poland while leaving the final settlement open. [C-032]
