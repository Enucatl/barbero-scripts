## 14. 31 August–1 September: The Attack Order, Gleiwitz, and Diplomatic Lag

<!-- transcript: U-01015–U-01095; omissions: none -->

On 31 August, the British tell the Poles: send a plenipotentiary to Berlin, or at least send your ambassador to knock on the door and ask for the negotiating terms. The Poles say they won't send the ambassador. It would be too humiliating. At 12:40 p.m. on 31 August, Hitler signs the order to attack the next morning. At one in the afternoon, the Poles change their minds. Without telling the British, they instruct Józef Lipski, the Polish ambassador in Berlin, to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms. Lipski goes. Ribbentrop's account records that he asks Lipski whether he has authority to negotiate. Lipski says he doesn't. Ribbentrop then says he had expected Lipski to be authorized to negotiate and will report the démarche to Hitler. [Q-043] The decision to attack had already been made before this belated Polish approach. [C-038]

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. What makes it curious, above all, is this: after everything I've told you, no one today would imagine the Germans still needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. But they want one, and the German security services under Heydrich have already prepared it. Reinhard Heydrich, a senior SS figure and head of the security services, would be killed four years later by Czechoslovak agents in Prague.

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. The curious thing about Gleiwitz, above all, is this: after everything I've told you, no one today would believe the Germans still needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. But they do want a pretext, and the German secret services under Heydrich's command have already prepared it. Heydrich—you know, Reinhard Heydrich, an SS big shot and head of the security services, who would be killed four years later by Czechoslovak partisans in Prague—has had a commando waiting for some time, for two weeks, at Gleiwitz, a German radio station near the Polish border. The best reconstruction has five or six attackers in civilian clothes at Gleiwitz, not twelve men in Polish uniforms. [N-033] On the morning of 31 August, the moment arrives. Heydrich sends the commando the code phrase: “Grandmother died.” [Q-044]

The commando swings into action on German territory. They seize the Gleiwitz radio station, fire shots, and broadcast a rambling statement in Polish—as we used to say in the days of the Red Brigades. They leave a body behind, though the victim's precise identity and clothing are less certain than the traditional account suggests. The incident is then presented as a Polish attack on German territory. The Germans claim the right to invade Poland, and the invasion begins a few hours later, at dawn on 1 September. [C-039]

Meanwhile, during the night, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax still hopes genuine negotiations with Poland can begin. He is worried because the Poles don't want to send their ambassador. What he doesn't know is that the Polish ambassador had already gone the previous evening and been rebuffed. On the night of 1 September, Halifax sends instructions to the ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw—separate messages that Barbero presents together—and what follows is perhaps the most inane telegram ever sent by a British government. The exchange reads: “Halifax: 'I do not see why the Polish Government should feel difficulty about authorising Polish Ambassador to accept a document from the German Government, and I earnestly hope that they may be able to modify their instructions to him in this respect. [...] On the other hand, a refusal by them to receive proposals would be gravely misunderstood by outside opinion. [...] I should have thought that the Polish Ambassador could surely be instructed to receive and transmit a document [...]. If negotiations are initiated, His Majesty's Government will at all times be ready, if desired, to lend any assistance in their power to achieve a just settlement. [...] Please speak to M. Beck immediately in the above sense.' Kennard: 'Your telegram of 1st September was decyphered at 4 a. m. to-day. M. Lipski had already called on the German Foreign Minister at 6:30 p. m. yesterday. In view of this fact, which was followed by German invasion of Poland at dawn to-day, it was clearly useless for me to take the action suggested.'” [Q-045]

Halifax's instruction doesn't include the latest outcome. Events have already overtaken it. [C-040]
