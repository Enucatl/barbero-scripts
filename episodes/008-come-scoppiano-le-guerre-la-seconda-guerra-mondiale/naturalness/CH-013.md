## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, 27 August, Germany introduces rationing: food cards. Monday, 28 August. Britain and France still think they may be able to persuade the Poles to negotiate, and they put pressure on the Polish government: go and ask the Germans exactly what they want, and then let's talk about it.

Tuesday, 29 August. In Italy, private cars are banned from the roads, and austerity measures come into force in restaurants. Menus are allowed just one main dish: meat or fish, but only one. The British ambassador goes to see Hitler and find out whether, even at this last moment, negotiations are genuinely still possible. The surviving documents read: “Henderson: 'Interview this evening was of a stormy character and Herr Hitler far less reasonable than yesterday. [...] He kept saying that he wanted British friendship more than anything in the world, but he could not sacrifice Germany's vital interests therefor.' Henderson's accompanying report: 'German Government, note adds, counts on arrival of such plenipotentiary to-morrow, Wednesday, 30th August. [...] I remarked that this phrase sounded like an ultimatum.' Hitler's written reply: 'The German Government, accordingly, in these circumstances agree to accept the British Government's offer of their good offices in securing the despatch to Berlin of a Polish Emissary with full powers. They count on the arrival of this Emissary on Wednesday, the 30th August, 1939.'” [Q-039]

Wednesday, 30 August. In Italy, the world cycling championships in Varese are suspended. The sale of coffee to private individuals is banned—you can't even have a cup in a café—and all coffee is reserved for the armed forces. In France, rail traffic is suspended, and the animals at Vincennes Zoo are evacuated for safety. In every capital, people start building air-raid shelters and handing out gas masks, because everyone is convinced the war will begin with devastating air raids on cities using poison gas. The broad preparations for aerial and gas attack are well established, though some individual details in this montage have not been independently verified. [C-036]

That same day, Wednesday, 30 August, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax—the man who, two years earlier, had told Hitler that his demands were fundamentally understandable—writes to the British ambassador in Berlin. The exchange reads: Halifax: ‘We shall give careful consideration to German Government's reply, but it is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.’ Henderson: ‘I had made similar observation to Herr Hitler yesterday evening, his reply being that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in one and a half hours.’ [Q-040]

At this point, the British are desperate to avoid war, and they start putting enormous pressure on the Polish government. Don't provoke the Germans. Stop the Polish newspapers publishing articles against Germany. End the anti-German campaign. Send a plenipotentiary to Berlin at once and negotiate. The British ambassador goes back to Ribbentrop and tells him they're doing everything they can, urging the Polish government to make peace and reach an agreement. Ribbentrop replies that British advice has so far had “damned little effect.” The ambassador answers—and reports his reply to London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

And now another comedy begins, a kind of German black comedy. Ribbentrop says they had drawn up terms for an agreement with Poland. The British ambassador reports: “Herr von Ribbentrop's reply was to produce a lengthy document which he read out in German aloud at top speed. Imagining that he would eventually hand it to me I did not attempt to follow too closely the sixteen or more articles which it contained. [...] When I asked Herr von Ribbentrop for text of these proposals in accordance with undertaking in the German reply of yesterday, he asserted that it was now too late as Polish representative had not arrived in Berlin by midnight. [...] In the most violent terms Herr von Ribbentrop said that he would never ask the Ambassador to visit him. He hinted that if Polish Ambassador asked him for interview it might be different. [...] We parted on that note, but I must tell you that Herr von Ribbentrop's whole demeanour during an unpleasant interview was aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] The arbitrary deadline, rapid oral reading, and refusal to hand over the text obstructed genuine negotiation.
