## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, August 27, Germany introduces rationing: food cards. Monday, August 28. Britain and France still think they may be able to persuade the Poles to negotiate. They lean on the Polish government: go ask the Germans exactly what they want, and then let's talk about it.

Tuesday, August 29. Italy bans private cars from the roads and imposes austerity measures on restaurants. A menu can have only one main course: meat or fish, but not both. The British ambassador goes to see Hitler and find out whether, even at this late hour, real negotiations are still possible. Here is how the documents record the exchange: “Henderson: 'Interview this evening was of a stormy character and Herr Hitler far less reasonable than yesterday. [...] He kept saying that he wanted British friendship more than anything in the world, but he could not sacrifice Germany's vital interests therefor.' Henderson's accompanying report: 'German Government, note adds, counts on arrival of such plenipotentiary to-morrow, Wednesday, 30th August. [...] I remarked that this phrase sounded like an ultimatum.' Hitler's written reply: 'The German Government, accordingly, in these circumstances agree to accept the British Government's offer of their good offices in securing the despatch to Berlin of a Polish Emissary with full powers. They count on the arrival of this Emissary on Wednesday, the 30th August, 1939.'” [Q-039]

Wednesday, August 30. Italy suspends the world cycling championships in Varese. Coffee can no longer be sold to private citizens. You can't even have a cup at a café; all of it is reserved for the armed forces. France suspends rail traffic, and the Vincennes Zoo is evacuated to save the animals. Across the capitals of Europe, people start building air-raid shelters and handing out gas masks. Everyone is convinced the war will begin with massive air raids dropping poison gas on cities. Preparations for air raids and gas attacks are widespread, with the exact measures varying from place to place. [C-036]

That same day, Wednesday, August 30, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax writes to the British ambassador in Berlin. This is the same Lord Halifax who, two years earlier, had told Hitler that his demands were basically understandable. Here is the exchange: Halifax: ‘We shall give careful consideration to German Government's reply, but it is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.’ Henderson: ‘I had made similar observation to Herr Hitler yesterday evening, his reply being that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in one and a half hours.’ [Q-040]

Now the British are desperate to prevent war, and they put intense pressure on the Polish government. Don't provoke the Germans. Stop the Polish newspapers from running articles against Germany. End the anti-German campaign. Send someone with full authority to Berlin right away and negotiate. The British ambassador goes back to Ribbentrop and tells him they're doing everything they can. They're urging the Polish government to make peace, to reach a deal. Ribbentrop answers that British advice has had “damned little effect” so far. The ambassador responds—and later tells London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

And now another farce begins, a kind of German black comedy. Ribbentrop says they have drawn up terms for an agreement with Poland. The British ambassador reports: “Herr von Ribbentrop's reply was to produce a lengthy document which he read out in German aloud at top speed. Imagining that he would eventually hand it to me I did not attempt to follow too closely the sixteen or more articles which it contained. [...] When I asked Herr von Ribbentrop for text of these proposals in accordance with undertaking in the German reply of yesterday, he asserted that it was now too late as Polish representative had not arrived in Berlin by midnight. [...] In the most violent terms Herr von Ribbentrop said that he would never ask the Ambassador to visit him. He hinted that if Polish Ambassador asked him for interview it might be different. [...] We parted on that note, but I must tell you that Herr von Ribbentrop's whole demeanour during an unpleasant interview was aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] The deadline is arbitrary. Ribbentrop races through the terms out loud, then refuses to hand over the text. Everything about it is designed to prevent real negotiations. [C-037]
