## 2. Halifax, anti-Bolshevism, and Hitler’s reading of the climate

<!-- transcript: U-00104–U-00181; omissions: none -->

Then comes a moment when their self-deception becomes impossible to miss. In the end, I decided to begin tonight’s story on this date: November 19, 1937. The Second World War is less than two years away. We know that. They don’t.

On November 19, 1937, the British foreign secretary, Lord Halifax, goes to Berlin and meets with Hitler. There, Halifax expresses sympathy for Germany’s claims. By this point, a generation in Britain is saying: yes, we went too far at Versailles. We imposed unfair terms on Germany. You can understand why the Germans want them revised.

Halifax tells Hitler that Britain understands Germany’s claims against Poland, Austria, and Czechoslovakia. Changes may be possible in time, he says, as long as they happen peacefully and don’t cause major upheaval. In his own words: “I said that there were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today, but we were concerned to avoid such treatment of them as would be likely to cause trouble. If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.” [Q-003]

Lord Halifax tells Hitler something else too: Germany is Europe’s bulwark against Bolshevism. [Q-004]

To understand how people think in those years, we can’t forget one other factor. To the east of Europe stands an unspoken presence, one everyone pretends doesn’t exist and mentions as little as possible: the Soviet Union. By 1937, Stalin is in power there. We know he has put world revolution away in a drawer, but they don’t.

To Western politicians, the Soviet Union is still the country not just of communists, but of Bolsheviks—extremely dangerous revolutionaries. It’s a country to keep at arm’s length. So the Germany of Herr Hitler, as British diplomats call him in their reports—they always call him that, Herr Hitler—is useful, all things considered. It is fiercely anti-communist, and it has thrown German communists into concentration camps. [C-004]

That’s true in Britain, and it’s true in France. Unlike Britain, France has a strong Communist Party and a strong Socialist Party, and the left-wing Popular Front is in government. We’ve forgotten it today, but at the time the Popular Front inspires—forget Hollande—enormous hopes and enormous fears in France. The country also has a ruling class of industrialists, generals, and clergy. Faced with a communist government, they say: well, the Nazis are better. Much better. Better Hitler than Stalin. “Nothing about the conduct of this fraction of the French bourgeoisie can be understood unless one hears it murmuring under its breath: ‘Hitler rather than Blum.’” [Q-005]

So Hitler senses that the climate is favorable. Certain things are now possible. He just has to wait for the opportunity. Historians have debated whether Hitler planned everything from the start, whether he already had detailed plans that led all the way to invading Poland. You know Woody Allen? “I can't listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” [Q-006] But the invasion of Poland is only the final act. Had Hitler already decided everything from the beginning? Perhaps not.

In the 1960s, the English historian A. J. P. Taylor publishes a famous book. His argument is that, criminal though Hitler is, his foreign-policy goals are fairly conventional. Everyone wants their country to be great and powerful. Hitler does too, and he waits for the right opportunities, sniffing the air. The wind is blowing his way. “There was here no concrete plan, no directive for German policy in 1937 and 1938. Or if there were a directive, it was to wait upon events. [...] Hitler did not make plans—for world conquest or for anything else. He assumed that others would provide opportunities, and that he would seize them. [...] His foreign policy was a different matter. He aimed to make Germany the dominant Power in Europe and maybe, more remotely, in the world. Other Powers have pursued similar aims, and still do.” [Q-007]

But Hitler doesn’t just wait for opportunities. He creates them too. Taylor doesn’t know much about that; documents that surface later make it clear. Hitler creates his opportunities. It starts with Austria. Austria is a German country. [C-005]
