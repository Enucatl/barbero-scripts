## 8. Italy’s alliance without the means for war

<!-- transcript: U-00522–U-00560; omissions: none -->

And then there’s Italy. Italy and Nazi Germany are the best of friends. On May 22, 1939, they sign the Pact of Steel, and the press is ecstatic. Hitler sends Victor Emmanuel III a telegram: “In this historic hour I wish to express to Your Majesty my profound satisfaction that our two peoples are bound to one another by an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.” [Q-021]

But Mussolini is worried. Ribbentrop has repeatedly assured the Italians that Germany doesn’t want a war anytime soon. Those assurances are only verbal—there’s nothing about that in the treaty—and Mussolini is starting to have doubts. [C-023]

And the first thing we need to understand—this isn’t always made clear, even though it’s well documented—is why Mussolini has those doubts. At this point, Italy is desperately short of resources. It has fought the Ethiopian War and intervened in the Spanish Civil War on Franco’s side. Everything has gone well. The Ethiopian War was a triumph, and Spain went pretty well too. But wars cost staggering amounts of money. The wars in Ethiopia and Spain have blown a hole in Italy’s budget and drained its reserves of military equipment and weapons. Italy is running short of everything, especially modern weapons. [C-022]

And over the past few years, every country has seen which way the wind is blowing. They’re all testing new weapons and putting a new generation of fighter planes into production—planes like the Spitfire—along with new tanks. Weapons that were perfectly adequate just two or three years ago are quickly becoming obsolete. Italy doesn’t have the industrial capacity or the resources to modernize fast enough. Mussolini knows this, even if he sometimes prefers to forget it. But in the summer of 1939, he knows it. And he has one great fear: that the Germans will decide to start a war. [C-022]

In early August, Ciano writes in his diary. You know Galeazzo Ciano: the foreign minister, Mussolini’s son-in-law. Five years later, he’ll be shot after the Verona trial, but that’s another story. So in early August, Ciano, our foreign minister, makes a note in his diary.
