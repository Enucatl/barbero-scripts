## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

<!-- transcript: U-00359–U-00443; omissions: none -->

But Chamberlain and Daladier aren’t the only ones who fall for it. Mussolini does too. And so does Poland. Remember, Poland will become the victim once the war breaks out. But when Czechoslovakia is carved up under the Munich settlement, Poland takes advantage of the situation and grabs a small piece for itself. Poland is a militaristic country run by colonels, a kind of half-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and intensely anti-communist. At this point, the Poles are convinced they can be friends with Hitler because they’re all anti-communists. Who could be more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles could—even more so. So in late 1938 and early 1939, the Poles believe they’re Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

And Hitler? On January 30, 1939, with seven months to go before the world war breaks out, he tells the Reichstag, the German parliament: “Even during the troubled months of the past year, German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life.” [Q-015]

At the end of January 1939, Polish foreign minister Józef Beck meets Ribbentrop in Warsaw—not Hitler in Berlin, as I just made it sound. According to Ribbentrop: “M. Beck made no secret of the fact that Poland had aspirations directed toward the Soviet Ukraine and a connection with the Black Sea; but at the same time he called attention to the supposed dangers to Poland that in the Polish view would arise from a treaty with Germany directed against the Soviet Union.” [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, while all these lovely conversations are going on in January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia is falling apart. It has lost the entire German-speaking border region, most of its industry, and its border defenses. Now Slovakia wants independence. You know the story: it eventually gets it, and today Slovakia is an independent country. Czechoslovakia is collapsing, and Hungary is about to invade. Hitler decides it’s time to get rid of the problem. [C-016]

On March 14, 1939, Hitler orders the Czechoslovak president, Emil Hácha, to come meet with him in Berlin. Hácha obeys. Shortly after one in the morning on March 15, Hitler tells him that German troops will move in at six and crush any resistance. It’s Göring, not Hitler, who threatens to bomb Prague. Hácha collapses and is given medical treatment and injections, though he isn’t diagnosed with a heart attack, and the doctors don’t deliberately keep him awake. He signs a declaration. It isn’t literally a request for German intervention. It reads: “The President of the Czecho-Slovak State declared that, in order to serve this end and to reach a final pacification, he confidently placed the fate of the Czech people and of their country in the hands of the Fuehrer of the German Reich.” [Q-017] German forces occupy Bohemia and Moravia on March 15, after Slovakia declares independence under German pressure on March 14. [N-014] [C-017]

On March 15, the Germans invade Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, no one has stepped in. [C-017]

And you’ll say: well, it’s about time. Chamberlain is starting to think that maybe Hitler can’t be trusted after all. Two days later, on March 17, 1939, Chamberlain gives a speech in Birmingham. [N-015] I’ll translate it for you. He’s clearly saying things he would much rather not say. You can hear it in the way he builds his sentences. Chamberlain says:

“Does not the question inevitably arise in our minds, if it is so easy to discover good reasons for ignoring assurances so solemnly and so repeatedly given, what reliance can be placed upon any other assurances that come from the same source? [...] Is this the end of an old adventure, or is it the beginning of a new? Is this the last attack upon a small State, or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” [Q-018]

Chamberlain is clearly hoping someone will tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt is starting to creep in. And the proof comes right away. The Germans invade Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on March 15. [C-018]
