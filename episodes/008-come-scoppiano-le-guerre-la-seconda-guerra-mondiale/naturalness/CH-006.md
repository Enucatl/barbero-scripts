## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

<!-- transcript: U-00359–U-00443; omissions: none -->

But Chamberlain and Daladier aren’t the only ones taken in—Mussolini believes it too. And so does Poland. Poland, remember, will itself become a victim when war breaks out. Yet when Czechoslovakia is partitioned under the Munich settlement, Poland takes advantage of the situation and seizes a small piece of Czechoslovakia for itself. Poland is a militaristic country, governed by colonels, a kind of semi-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and rabidly anti-communist. At this point, the Poles are convinced they can be friends with Hitler because they’re all anti-communists. After all, who is more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles are—even more so. And so, in the last months of 1938 and the first months of 1939, the Poles believe they’re Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

And Hitler? On 30 January 1939, with seven months to go before the outbreak of the world war, he tells the Reichstag, the German parliament: “Even during the troubled months of the past year, German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life.” [Q-015]

At the end of January 1939, the Polish foreign minister, Józef Beck, meets Ribbentrop in Warsaw—not Hitler in Berlin, as I’ve just made it sound. In Ribbentrop’s account: “M. Beck made no secret of the fact that Poland had aspirations directed toward the Soviet Ukraine and a connection with the Black Sea; but at the same time he called attention to the supposed dangers to Poland that in the Polish view would arise from a treaty with Germany directed against the Soviet Union.” [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, as these pleasant conversations continue through January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia is falling apart. It has lost the entire German-populated belt, most of its industry, and its border fortifications. Slovakia now wants independence. It’s an old story, as you know: that does eventually happen, and today Slovakia is an independent country, having separated. Czechoslovakia is collapsing and is on the point of being invaded by Hungary. At a certain point, Hitler decides to settle the matter. [C-016]

On 14 March 1939, Hitler orders the Czechoslovak president, Emil Hácha, to come and see him in Berlin. Hácha obeys. After one in the morning on 15 March, Hitler tells him that German troops will enter at six, and that any resistance will be crushed. It is Göring, not Hitler, who threatens Prague with an air attack. Hácha collapses and is given medical attention and injections; the primary records consulted don’t establish that he had a diagnosed heart attack or that doctors deliberately kept him awake. He signs a declaration—not a literal request for German intervention—which reads: “The President of the Czecho-Slovak State declared that, in order to serve this end and to reach a final pacification, he confidently placed the fate of the Czech people and of their country in the hands of the Fuehrer of the German Reich.” [Q-017] German forces occupy Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March, after Slovakia declares independence under German pressure on 14 March. [N-014] [C-017]

On 15 March, the Germans invade Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, no one has intervened. [C-017]

But surely, you’ll say, by now Chamberlain is beginning to wonder whether Hitler can be trusted after all. Two days later, on 17 March 1939, Chamberlain speaks in Birmingham. [N-015] I’ll translate the speech for you. He is clearly saying things he would much rather not have to say; you can hear it in the syntax. Chamberlain says:

“Does not the question inevitably arise in our minds, if it is so easy to discover good reasons for ignoring assurances so solemnly and so repeatedly given, what reliance can be placed upon any other assurances that come from the same source? [...] Is this the end of an old adventure, or is it the beginning of a new? Is this the last attack upon a small State, or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” [Q-018]

Chamberlain is plainly hoping that someone will tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt is beginning to creep in. And the proof comes immediately. The Germans have invaded Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on 15 March. [C-018]
