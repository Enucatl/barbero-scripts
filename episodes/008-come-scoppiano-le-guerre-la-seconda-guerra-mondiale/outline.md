# Structural outline

## Overview

Barbero explains the outbreak of the Second World War as the product of a “double illusion”: Britain and France believed that legitimate German grievances could be satisfied without a general upheaval, while Hitler believed he could overturn Versailles, expand German power, and destroy Poland without serious Western intervention. The lecture first establishes the post-1919 grievances and the anti-Bolshevik assumptions that enabled appeasement, then follows Hitler’s escalating successes in Austria and Czechoslovakia. It turns to the failed Anglo-French-Soviet talks, the Nazi-Soviet pact, Italy’s inability to honor the Pact of Steel, and a close daily chronology of the final August 1939 crisis. The ending juxtaposes the manufactured Gleiwitz pretext and invasion with British diplomatic lag and Fascist press propaganda.

## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

- **Utterances:** U-00001–U-00103
- **Original audio:** 00:01–06:10
- **Argument:** Versailles ended one war while producing frustrations and minority problems that gave revisionism broad appeal. The road to war is framed as reciprocal miscalculation: Western democracies hoped to accommodate bounded German claims, and Hitler expected no determined resistance.
- **Steps/examples:** Italy’s “vittoria mutilata”; Danzig and the Polish corridor; prohibited Austrian union; Sudeten Germans; Hitler’s 1933 rise and revisionist program.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Gdańsk and Lech Wałęsa; comparison with Italian irredentism.
- **Quotation targets:** Q-001 (“vittoria mutilata,” U-00016–U-00024); Q-002 German revisionist slogans about Danzig and Versailles (U-00043–U-00085).
- **Claim targets:** C-001 Versailles created destabilizing grievances (U-00008–U-00073); C-002 Nazi electoral victory and Hitler’s revisionist program (U-00074–U-00085); C-003 the “double illusion” as causal thesis (U-00086–U-00103).
- **Treatment:** preserve; clarify the distinction between understandable grievances and Nazi expansion.
