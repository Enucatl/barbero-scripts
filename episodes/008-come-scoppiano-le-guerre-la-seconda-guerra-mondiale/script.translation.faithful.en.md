# How Wars Break Out: The Second World War — assembled faithful translation

## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

<!-- transcript: U-00001–U-00103; omissions: none -->

Well, good evening. Tonight we have a journey ahead of us which is, how shall I put it, rather tough. You will say that last night was no joke either, but tonight the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story. The only cheerful notes are provided by Italy’s participation. Whatever one may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and so on, they provided the only note of comedy in those fairly dramatic weeks, as you will see.

You remember that even Italy—even victorious Italy, which gets Trento and Trieste and swallows up South Tyrol—isn’t satisfied. In Italy people begin speaking of the *mutilated victory*. The phrase grows into a nationalist political myth. D’Annunzio’s original line, published in October 1918 before the armistice and before the Paris settlement, is addressed to Victory itself: “the so-called ‘mutilated victory’” [Q-001] [C-001]

Germany, which had lost, emerged from the First World War and from the Treaty of Versailles frustrated, with the feeling that it had suffered an injustice. What injustice? To begin with, Germany had been forced to cede territories to a new country. The Treaty of Versailles created many new states, among them Poland. Germany ceded to Poland—was forced to cede to Poland—a large tract of territory inhabited by Germans. And, what is more, situations were created that were impossible to manage.

There was one city that today no longer matters to anyone: it is Gdańsk in Poland. We have hardly heard it mentioned since the days of Lech Wałęsa, but at the time it was discussed constantly. Danzig was a great port, an important German city, located on the border between the new Poland and Germany. Both claimed it. The victors at Versailles decided: all right, let us make it a Free City, independent, neither German nor Polish. The city of Danzig was inhabited entirely by Germans who wanted to be part of Germany, but they were not allowed to.

And in Germany a movement of public opinion began immediately, somewhat comparable to what had happened in Italy before the war: Trento, Trieste, irredentism, the separated brothers. In Germany people campaigned: Danzig, Danzig is German, Danzig is German and they did not give it to us. It is a disgrace; one day we shall have to go back to the question. [Q-002]

But then there were other problems born from the end of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The Austro-Hungarian Empire was dissolved and many new states came into being: Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia. And with them came many problems. Austria was small and inhabited entirely by Germans. Most of them would have liked to say: all right, since things have ended as they have, let us become part of Germany; we Austrians are Germans too. But the victors would not allow it. Austria had to remain independent.

Then there was Czechoslovakia, the state of the Czechs and the Slovaks. But in those regions, in the days of the Austrian Empire, there had lived a great many Germans—that is, Austrian citizens who spoke German. They now found themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, but they did not feel Czechoslovak. So there was this whole collection of problems created by Versailles, problems which in themselves were also understandable. To a large extent they were problems of minorities living abroad.

Tonight I shall have to speak for a while about the remote causes of the Second World War. I would have liked to say to you: very well, it is 10 August 1939, and this happens. We shall do that, but first we have to speak a little about these remote causes, because otherwise we shall understand nothing. And so the remote causes are precisely those I have described: a Germany that emerged frustrated, with a series of claims against Poland over Danzig; against Austria, because the Austrians were all Germans and it was not clear why they should not be part of Germany; and against Czechoslovakia, because of the Germans living there who felt discriminated against.

In 1933, in Germany, the Nazis won the elections and Hitler came to power. Hitler came to power with a program that included many fine things, but the point the Germans perhaps felt most strongly was this: we must revise the Treaty of Versailles; we must revise the Europe that emerged from Versailles. It is an unjust Europe, an iniquitous treaty. Germany has suffered, and that is not right. We must return to the matter and alter the borders. [C-002]

The story of how the Second World War broke out is, at bottom, the story of a double illusion. One was the illusion of the great democratic powers, Britain and France: the illusion that Hitler could be given some satisfaction, because on certain matters Hitler was, after all, right. It was the illusion that one could say, very well, let us negotiate, let us reach an agreement—Danzig there, and so on—without causing any great upheaval. An illusion, as we shall see.

The other illusion was Hitler’s: that it was possible to take everything back, and something more besides, and once again transform Germany into the great power of Europe without anyone seriously opposing him. The illusion of the democracies, the illusion of the Western democracies—France and Britain. [C-003]

## 2. Halifax, anti-Bolshevism, and Hitler’s reading of the climate

<!-- transcript: U-00104–U-00181; omissions: none -->

There is a moment when the fact that they are deluding themselves becomes absolutely clear. So, our story tonight—in the end I decided to begin it on this day: 19 November 1937. Less than two years remain before the outbreak of the Second World War. We know that; they did not.

On 19 November 1937 the British foreign secretary, Lord Halifax, goes to Berlin, meets Hitler, and in Berlin the British minister expresses sympathy for the German claims. Because by then there is a generation in Britain saying: yes, after all, at Versailles we went too far; we imposed unjust things on Germany. After all, one can understand the Germans asking for revisions.

On that day Lord Halifax, then Lord President of the Council, visits Hitler at the Berghof near Berchtesgaden. Halifax tells him that Britain does not insist on preserving every detail of the Versailles settlement: “There were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today ... If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.” [Q-003]

Lord Halifax also says something else to Hitler: Germany is Europe’s bulwark against Bolshevism. [Q-004]

Because we must not forget, if we are to understand how they reasoned in those years, that there is this other factor as well. In Europe, in the East, there is, there is an unmentioned presence which everyone pretends does not exist and speaks of as little as possible: the Soviet Union. There is the Soviet Union, where by 1937 Stalin is in power. We know that Stalin has put world revolution away in a drawer, but they do not know it.

For them, the Soviet Union—for them, for Western politicians—is still the country not even of the communists, but of the Bolsheviks, of revolutionaries who are extremely dangerous. It is a country to be kept at a distance. Therefore the Germany of Herr Hitler, as British diplomats call it in their reports—they always call him that, Herr Hitler—is, all things considered, with its ferocious anti-communism and all those concentration camps into which it has thrown the German communists, well, all things considered, a useful country. [C-004]

In France there is a strong Communist Party, a strong Socialist Party, and the Popular Front. We have forgotten how many hopes—and how many terrors—the Popular Front arouses. Among part of the French bourgeoisie, industrialists, officers, clergy, the sentiment is: “Hitler rather than Blum.” [Q-005]

People have argued endlessly over whether he had planned every step from the beginning, all the way to Poland. You know the line from Woody Allen’s character in *Manhattan Murder Mystery*: “I can’t listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” But Poland is the last act of this story, not the first. [Q-006]

In the 1960s A. J. P. Taylor causes a scandal by arguing that Hitler waits upon events, expects others to provide opportunities, and seizes them; in foreign policy he seeks German predominance, as other great powers had sought predominance. But Hitler does not merely wait for opportunities. He creates them. [Q-007]

Waiting for opportunities—and also creating the opportunities, however. Taylor did not know much about that; documents which emerged later show it: Hitler created the opportunities. It begins with Austria. Austria is a German country. [C-005]

## 3. Anschluss: engineered crisis and non-response

<!-- transcript: U-00182–U-00211; omissions: none -->

And so in Austria there is a Nazi party which wants annexation to Germany and takes orders from Hitler. At a certain point Hitler gives the Nazis in Austria the go-ahead: create agitation, attacks, terrorism, disorder, political murders. [C-006]

Finally, at a certain point, it is March 1938. Four months have passed since Lord Halifax’s visit to Berlin, when he told Hitler: well, after all, your reasons can be understood. Four months pass. In Vienna, the Austrian Nazi Party stages a coup and invites German troops to enter Austria to guarantee security. On 12 March 1938 German troops enter Austria. Yes, the Anschluss, as it was called: the annexation.

Yes, it is said that the German army at that date was so unprepared for war that seventy percent of the German military vehicles entering Austria broke down before they reached Vienna. [C-007] Yet abroad nobody reacts.

There is genuine enthusiasm in Austria, and then there is a plebiscite under occupation, coercive propaganda, and exclusions. The official result is 99.73 percent for union. Cardinal Innitzer and the other Austrian bishops declare: “We are also convinced that the work of the National Socialist movement has warded off the danger of utterly destructive, godless Bolshevism ... On the day of the plebiscite it is for us bishops a self-evident national duty, as Germans, to declare ourselves for the German Reich.” [Q-008] [C-008]

Britain and France protest, but do not act. *The Times* writes that Austria “was destined sooner or later to find herself in close association with the German Reich,” comparing it to the old union of Scotland and England. Hitler takes note: Austria annexed, no intervention. [Q-009]

Hitler takes note: Austria annexed, and nobody has reacted. A few days pass, but really only a few days. Still in that March of 1938—

## 4. Sudeten crisis and the exclusion of the Soviet Union

<!-- transcript: U-00212–U-00256; omissions: none -->

Hitler summons the leader of the Czechoslovak Nazis. Because in Czechoslovakia too there is a Nazi party among the German minority of the Sudetenland. That is what they are called—I can never remember why—but the Germans of Czechoslovakia are called the Sudeten Germans.

Among the Germans of Czechoslovakia there is a Nazi party which takes orders from Hitler, and in March 1938 Hitler gives the signal: begin the street violence, the agitation, the terrorism. Czechoslovakia begins to sink into chaos. [C-009]

The German press begins an extremely heavy campaign. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are oppressed; they are a discriminated-against minority, an oppressed minority. The criminal Czechoslovak police arrest them, rape them, torture them. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are suffering; this cannot be endured. [Q-010]

The British ambassadors in Berlin and in Prague, on instructions from their government, say: well, after all, the Germans are not entirely in the wrong; one can understand them. They repeat that the German claims are, after all, well founded, and that it is the Czechoslovak government which must try to meet the Germans partway in what they are asking. We certainly do not want to unleash another war, do we? And therefore the Czechoslovak government would do better to make concessions. [C-010]

There is only one country which, at that moment, when the Nazis in Czechoslovakia begin the same game they had played in Austria—there is only one country which then takes a public position and says that all the powers ought to reach an agreement to guarantee Czechoslovakia. It is the Soviet Union.

One government publicly proposes that the powers act together to guarantee Czechoslovakia: the Soviet Union. Litvinov calls for “immediate practical measures.” The proposal depends on French action and military access, and it is discounted. Britain’s ambassador in Moscow concludes that Russia can effectively be treated “as outside European politics.” [Q-011] [C-011]

Notice once again how difficult it is for them to take account of the way the world is changing. They continue to speak of “European politics” as though politics were still always made by Britain and France. No: the United States is not there, Japan is not there, the Soviet Union is not there—nothing. It is still always European politics, and Russia does not count.

This goes on for months, with Czechoslovakia in chaos and the German press pounding away, pounding away, pounding away at the sufferings of the Sudeten Germans, while Britain and France press the government in Prague, saying: but make some concessions.

## 5. Munich, peace at any price, and appeasement’s failure

<!-- transcript: U-00257–U-00358; omissions: none -->

At last we come to September. The situation has become so serious that the British and French governments decide that the Czechoslovaks must be forced to make concessions to Hitler. Something has to be invented. They ought to invent a conference—today we would call it a negotiating table. At the time they had not yet invented the “table,” nor had they invented the “summit.” They ought to hold a conference, they say.

How can they persuade Hitler to accept a negotiating table? Ask Mussolini, since the two of them are friends. The British and French approach Mussolini and say to him, in substance—I am summarizing—sound Hitler out and see whether he is willing to arrange for all of us to meet and reach an agreement, in order to accommodate the German demands on Czechoslovakia. Hitler agrees.

On 29 and 30 September 1938 comes the episode that was later always deplored as, how shall I put it, one of the low points of European politics, although at least two of its four protagonists were acting in good faith: the Munich Conference. In Munich, Bavaria, there meet Hitler, Mussolini, the British prime minister Chamberlain—Neville Chamberlain—and the French prime minister, Daladier. [C-012]

Chamberlain and Daladier sincerely want to avoid another slaughter. They belong to the generation haunted by 1914–18. Everything but another war. Never again, at almost any price. Their motives also include unreadiness, alliance weakness, empire, public opinion, and the hope of buying time—but the nightmare is real. [Q-012] [C-013]

What does “any price” mean? Well, if Czechoslovakia has to surrender a piece of territory to Germany, so be it; that is better. The Munich Conference consists of Britain, France, Italy, and Germany meeting to decide which piece of Czechoslovakia will be ceded to Germany. The Czechoslovaks are outside the door waiting to learn what has been decided. And the Munich Conference decides: the whole area populated by Germans, the Sudetenland, is ceded to Germany.

Hitler takes note. Not only have they failed to obstruct me; they have actually helped me take a piece of Czechoslovakia.

And here, how shall I put it—precisely, as I said—I do not know. It is difficult to talk about it, because on the one hand one feels like saying what was said for fifty years: what a tragedy, politicians at that level. And yet, and yet, they were, how shall I put it, first-rate politicians who failed to understand what was happening, and they were acting in good faith.

Chamberlain returns to London. He was then mocked, right down to the present day, for this return to London from Munich. Chamberlain returns to London. Already as he comes down the aircraft steps he is waving the sheet of paper containing the agreements. You understand? There are the photographers, the crowd, everything around him. Chamberlain waves the paper and speaks to the crowd.

Chamberlain returns to London, a return for which he will be mocked for decades. As he comes down the aircraft steps, with photographers and a crowd around him, he waves the agreement. At Heston aerodrome he says: “The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is in my view only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace.” [Q-013]

We are eleven months before the outbreak of the Second World War; let us remember that. Chamberlain says that that morning he had had another meeting with the German chancellor, Herr Hitler, and here was the treaty bearing Hitler’s signature and his own. The two of them regarded the agreement signed the previous night as a symbol of the desire of their two peoples never again to go to war against one another. My dear friends: the British prime minister had returned from Germany bringing peace with honor. He believed it was peace for their time. Go home and have a good, peaceful sleep. That was Chamberlain’s speech to the London crowd on his return from Munich.

Chamberlain’s policy had a precise name, one which later became, in the vocabulary of British politics—we shall see it again tomorrow evening when we talk about Mrs Thatcher—an ugly word: *appeasement*. Pacification. Peace, whatever the cost.

And *appeasement* is indeed shocking today. They did not realize. No, they did not realize. The tragic irony is that they were prepared to do anything in order not to let war break out. The result was that they made it break out. [C-014]

Hitler is exceptionally skilled at misleading his interlocutors. Before Munich he tells Chamberlain, and on 26 September proclaims publicly: “It is the last territorial demand which I have to make in Europe.” He has said it. He sounds sincere. So: sleep soundly. [Q-014]

Hitler had said it. He was so sincere. Therefore: sleep peacefully.

## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

<!-- transcript: U-00359–U-00443; omissions: none -->

But Chamberlain and Daladier were not the only ones to be taken in—besides Mussolini, who believed it too. Even Poland believed it. Poland, let us remember, would later be the victim when war broke out. Yet when Czechoslovakia was partitioned under the Munich settlement, Poland took advantage of it and seized a small piece of Czechoslovakia as well. Poland was a militaristic country, governed by colonels, a semi-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and anti-communist in the extreme. At that moment the Poles were convinced that friendship with Hitler was possible, since they were all anti-communists. Who was more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles are—even more so. Thus, in the final months of 1938 and the opening months of 1939, the Poles believed they were Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

On 30 January 1939, seven months before the world war begins, Hitler tells the Reichstag that friendship between Germany and Poland is “one of the reassuring factors in the political life of Europe.” He also says: “The Polish state respects the national conditions in the Free City of Danzig, and Germany respects Polish rights.” [Q-015]

In the January talks Ribbentrop tempts Foreign Minister Józef Beck with Ukrainian compensation and access toward the Black Sea. The prospect is an eastward expansion into Soviet Ukraine, where there are Polish minorities. Beck listens, resists alignment, and still believes Poland can manage its dangerous neighbor. [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, as these fine conversations went on through January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia was falling apart. It had lost the entire belt inhabited by Germans, the bulk of its industry, and its border fortifications. Slovakia now wanted independence. It is an old story, as you know: that later happened, and today Slovakia is independent, having separated. Czechoslovakia was collapsing and was about to be invaded by Hungary. At a certain point Hitler decided to dispose of the problem. [C-016]

On the night of 14–15 March 1939 President Emil Hácha is summoned to Berlin. Hitler tells him German troops will enter at six in the morning and resistance will be crushed. Göring threatens to bomb Prague. Hácha collapses and receives medical treatment. At last, under coercion, he signs a declaration placing the fate of the Czech people and country “trustingly in the hands of the Führer.” [Q-017] [C-017]

On 15 March the Germans invaded Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervened. Hitler took note: once again, no one had intervened. [C-017]

But, you will say, it was about time: Chamberlain began to think that perhaps Hitler could not be trusted after all. Two days later, on 17 March 1939, Chamberlain gave a public address in Birmingham—not in the House of Commons. I will translate his speech for you. He is plainly saying things he would very much rather not say; you can hear it in the syntax. Chamberlain says:

You may say: it was about time. Chamberlain begins to think that perhaps Hitler cannot be trusted after all. On 17 March in Birmingham—not in Parliament—he speaks as though the words are things he does not want to say: if solemn and repeated guarantees can so easily be ignored, what reliance can be placed on other guarantees from the same source? “Is this the last attack upon a small State or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” The doubt has begun. Prague is a turning point in British policy, though rearmament and doubts had already begun. [Q-018]

Chamberlain was evidently hoping someone would tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt was beginning to arise. The proof came at once. The Germans had invaded Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on 15 March. [C-018]

## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Three months after calling German-Polish friendship a reassuring foundation of European politics, Hitler speaks again in the same place. On 28 April he calls Danzig perhaps Germany’s most painful problem. He says he thought a peaceful solution would help remove tension from Europe, but the Polish government had refused his offer; he deeply regrets this “incomprehensible” attitude. He tells the Reichstag that he had offered Poland “a solution which, in view of the intimate relations between Germany and Poland, solved the questions under discussion in the most favourable manner for Poland.” He renounces the 1934 declaration. [Q-019] [C-019]

From that moment on, conflict intensified in Danzig and along the frontier between Danzig and Poland: incidents and shootings. Nazi militias formed in Danzig; outside stood the Polish customs post. There were incidents continually. But this time the British and French governments were beginning to think that perhaps something ought to be done. What?

Britain and France proposed negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain even went so far as to give an official guarantee: although no treaty had yet been signed, Britain—with France taking the same position—would give Poland all the support in its power if action clearly threatened Polish independence and Poland considered it vital to resist. Bear in mind that today, with hindsight, one says: it was about time. What else could they do? [C-020]

With hindsight one says: it was about time. What else could they do? At the time, however, this is extremely difficult to sell to public opinion in Britain and France. War for Danzig? On 4 May the French politician Marcel Déat publishes the famous headline: “To die for Danzig?” His conclusion is sharper: fight beside our Polish friends for common defense, yes—“But to die for Danzig, no!” The phrase travels everywhere and becomes part of the crisis. [Q-020] [C-020]

They began exploring whether some kind of agreement could be reached with the Soviet Union. Negotiations began and dragged on for many months, to see whether a military-defense agreement could be concluded in the event of war. The story of these negotiations is sad and depressing, because one thinks: if only they had made that agreement. Instead, it appears that no one truly wanted it enough; they were all afraid of one another. One apparently objective feature emerges from the telegrams and replies: whenever Britain and France made a proposal to the Russians, the Russians would answer the next day. Then weeks would pass—weeks and weeks—before the British and French finally took the next step. The Russians would answer the next day, and so the negotiations dragged on. This is an illustrative pattern rather than an exact rule for every exchange, and mistrust and obstruction existed on all sides. [C-021]

Meanwhile Stalin took note. They are asking me to negotiate—but do they mean it, or do they want to draw me in and then, at the decisive moment, leave me to deal with Germany alone? Stalin took note and decided that the risk was too great. He was negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French seemed very lukewarm.

Stalin let the Germans know that although their countries were enemies—one communist, the other, well...—they had commercial interests, and perhaps opening negotiations for a commercial agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union might be a good idea. The Germans were willing. And so a situation developed over the summer in which Stalin, without anyone knowing, was negotiating with Britain and France at one table and with Germany at another, waiting to see what would happen. More precisely, economic contacts revived unevenly and only developed into a political channel later, accelerating decisively in August. [C-021]

## 8. Italy’s alliance without the means for war

<!-- transcript: U-00522–U-00560; omissions: none -->

Italy is Nazi Germany’s closest friend. On 22 May 1939 the two countries sign the Pact of Steel. The Fascist press erupts with eternal, unbreakable friendship: the two peoples are joined by “an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.” [Q-021]

But in reality Mussolini was worried. He was worried because Ribbentrop had repeatedly assured the Italians orally that Germany did not want war for the time being—though the treaty itself contained no such guarantee—and Mussolini was beginning to feel less certain. [C-023]

The first thing we must understand clearly—something that is not always stated so plainly, although it is well known and documented—is why Mussolini was so uncertain. Italy at that moment was severely short of resources. Italy had fought the Ethiopian War and had intervened in the Spanish Civil War on Franco’s side. Everything had gone well: the Ethiopian War was a triumph, and things had gone fairly well in Spain too. But wars cost terrifying sums. The Ethiopian and Spanish wars had torn a chasm in the Italian budget and depleted matériel and armament reserves. There was severe scarcity, especially for modern weapons. [C-022]

Now, as it happened, in those last few years every country had understood which way the wind was blowing and was experimenting with new weapons. Everywhere, new-concept fighters such as the Spitfire and new tanks were entering production. Armaments that had been adequate only two or three years earlier were rapidly becoming obsolete. Italy lacked the industrial capacity and resources to modernize quickly enough. Mussolini knew it, even if every so often he preferred to forget. But in the summer of 1939 he knew, and he had one concern: that the Germans might take it into their heads to start a war. [C-022]

In early August Ciano wrote in his diary—you know Galeazzo Ciano, the foreign minister, Mussolini’s son-in-law, who would be shot five years later after the Verona trial, but that is another story. In early August Ciano, our foreign minister, made a note in his diary.

## 9. Ciano learns Germany wants war

<!-- transcript: U-00561–U-00595; omissions: none -->

Galeazzo Ciano is Mussolini’s son-in-law and foreign minister. Five years later he will be shot after the Verona trial, but that is another story. In early August he writes in his diary that the time has come to see how matters stand: the stakes are too high. He flies to Germany. [Q-022]

On 11 August Ciano meets his German counterpart, Ribbentrop, at Fuschl near Salzburg. Ciano has been sent to explain that war at that moment would be madness and must absolutely be avoided. He asks what all this agitation over Poland is about: “What do you want? Danzig?” Ribbentrop answers: “We no longer want Danzig. We want war.” [Q-023]

They were at a reception, all standing there with champagne glasses in hand. Ciano was burning to tell his staff what Ribbentrop had just told him, but they were still at the reception. Ciano later wrote all this down. At last he passed close to one of his aides and whispered in his ear, “We’re headed for a fight.”

Ciano spoke further with Ribbentrop. The message was that war with Poland had been decided, and Poland would be crushed. And Britain? France? Ribbentrop believed they would not intervene. And then there was the wager, as in 1914, for those of you who were here last night. The wager was that they would not intervene. Except this time—you know how history repeats itself, first as tragedy and then as farce?—they really made a bet.

The wager returns, as in 1914. History repeats itself first as tragedy and then as farce, and this time they actually bet. Ribbentrop says: if I win, you owe me a fine Italian Renaissance painting. If you win, I will give you a collection of antique German weapons. But I will win; they will not intervene. We will make war, and it will not become a world war, because Britain and France will not dare intervene. [Q-024] [C-024]

The gala dinner ended, then the reception, the toasts, everything. Ciano returned to the hotel with his aides, burning to tell them everything. Once there, he began to wonder whether the Germans might have planted microphones in the room. So they locked themselves in the bathroom, and there Ciano told his aides that the Germans had now decided on war. And here the countdown begins: 12 August.

## 10. 12–18 August: rival negotiations and Polish refusal

<!-- transcript: U-00596–U-00727; omissions: none -->

The day after Ciano’s interview with Ribbentrop was Saturday, 12 August. It was the Ferragosto holiday season; this war, like the First World War, broke out in the middle of summer. People did not yet take as many holidays as they do now, but they did take some, and at Ferragosto people went away. On Saturday, 12 August, for example, Milan emptied: half a million people took the train to the seaside.

That same day the Anglo-French delegation finally arrived in Moscow after months of talk, sent to negotiate a treaty. The Soviets were deeply disappointed. The delegation consisted of a French general, Joseph Doumenc, and a British admiral, Reginald Drax—figures below the highest rank. The Russians asked, “Do you have full powers to sign a treaty?”

Let me pause for a second. This matter of full powers is extremely important for understanding diplomatic exchanges in this period. There was, of course, the telephone, the telegraph, and the airplane; nevertheless, everyone considered it essential to have someone on the spot who could sign. The Soviets asked: “You have come to negotiate—excellent. This is urgent. Do you have full powers?” Drax could negotiate but not sign. Doumenc had authority from Daladier to conclude a military convention, although he did not carry conventional plenipotentiary credentials and remained dependent on Paris. In effect, the answer was: “You tell us, and we will report back.” The Soviets were deeply disappointed. [C-025]

That same day the Soviet government sent an invitation: it would be a good thing if Ribbentrop came to Moscow, because they had matters to discuss. Sunday, 13 August: high summer, holidays, the Venice Film Festival; Maria Bellonci won the Viareggio Prize for *Lucrezia Borgia*.

That same day Hitler spoke with Mussolini. It was exactly as Ciano had said: Germany would make war on Poland and attack at the first incident. Nor was that all. Hitler explained that it was mid-August. His generals calculated that defeating Poland would take a month and a half, six weeks at most. By mid-October autumn would arrive, and Poland’s roads and airfields would become unusable in the mud. Therefore the campaign had to be over by mid-October. They needed a month and a half, so they would attack by the end of August. This was Hitler’s operational estimate and planning rationale. [C-026]

On 12 August Hitler explains the timetable to Ciano. Poland, he says, must be dealt with quickly. Operations should end by mid-October, before autumn weather cripples roads, airfields, and mechanized forces; the campaign may take six weeks. Hence the intended opening before the end of August. Ciano writes on 13 August: “They have deceived us and lied to us.” [Q-025]

Monday, 14 August: a holiday atmosphere, with the Salzburg Festival under way. Hitler, who was in the mountains, attended Mozart’s *The Abduction from the Seraglio* at the festival.

In Moscow the Russians met the Anglo-French delegation and put their cards on the table. Did they want an agreement to defend Poland? Very well, but the Soviets wanted a guarantee that it would be taken seriously: if war came, the Poles must allow Soviet troops onto Polish territory. Were the Soviets acting in good faith or bad faith? Were they already thinking of gobbling Poland up, or did they sincerely believe that if war had to be fought, it had to be fought seriously? We do not know. In any case, this was their demand. What did the British admiral and French general answer? “That is not within our authority. We must report back. We will report to Paris and London, and then we shall see.” Transit was the immediate impasse, but not the only cause of the talks’ failure: Anglo-French delays and mandates, Polish fear of Soviet occupation, Soviet bargaining, and the German option all mattered. [C-027]

That same Monday, 14 August, Ribbentrop decided that the Russian idea of his going to Moscow was a good one. He would go on Tuesday, 15 August—Ferragosto. The French general had written that, to sign the agreement, the Russians wanted a guarantee that the Poles would let them enter in the event of war. The Poles had to be asked.

French foreign minister Georges Bonnet sought the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz. Where do you suppose the Polish ambassador was on Ferragosto? At the seaside. They brought him back to Paris and told him: allow the Russians passage in the event of war against Germany. The ambassador said that he would have to ask Warsaw, but in his opinion the answer would be no, because the Poles feared the Russians even more than the Germans.

Bonnet summons the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz, back from the seaside. The ambassador’s answer is predictably hostile: Poland fears Soviet entry perhaps more than German attack. He boasts: “It is Poland which, from the first days, will carry the war into Germany.” [Q-026]

Wednesday, 16 August. Ribbentrop wrote to Moscow: “I am coming.” It was the first time they put in writing that he would make the journey to sign a treaty with the Soviets. In Berlin, meanwhile, the Germans were beginning to worry about one thing. Those of you who were here last night will forgive me: history sometimes repeats itself. The Germans were asking themselves, what would Britain do?

On the evening of 15 August, British ambassador Sir Nevile Henderson visited State Secretary Ernst von Weizsäcker at the German Foreign Ministry. The Germans’ argument was: surely Britain would not support Poland? Look how mad these Poles were, with their provocations. The German minority in Poland was oppressed; it was all the fault of the unreasonable Poles. Surely the British would not be willing to help madmen?

On 15 August the German Foreign Ministry tests Britain. Ambassador Nevile Henderson answers with brutal clarity: it is “quite untrue” that Britain will not fight for Poland; if Germany uses force, Britain will fulfill its obligations. [Q-027] [C-028]

Thursday, 17 August. Incidents multiplied along the German-Polish frontier: shootings and border crossings. The German press unleashed a campaign—remember Czechoslovakia the year before?—about “Polish terror against the German minority”: arrests, murders. In Moscow, Ribbentrop’s reply arrived: he was willing to come and sign an agreement.

Friday, 18 August. As you know, Mussolini and Ciano were terrified because Italy was not ready for war, but the official line dictated to the Italian press was, obviously, quite different. Italy had signed the Pact of Steel. The Italian press had precise orders: in this crisis, the Germans were right.

So I will read you the front-page display from the afternoon edition of the *Corriere della Sera* of 18 August:

Yet the Fascist press tells another story. On 18 August *Corriere della Sera* announces that Polish provocation is worsening the crisis and that Danzig demands reunion with Germany. [Q-028]

Democracy, of course, was a dirty word in Fascist Italy. The “encirclers” were precisely the “demo-pluto-Jewish-ocracies”: France, Britain, and the Soviet Union, which supposedly besieged the peoples. Well, you know; we know this story in Italy.

That same Friday, 18 August, the Polish government had to decide whether to enter an alliance with the Soviet Union while granting passage to Soviet troops. The Polish government said no: “We have no military agreements with the Soviet Union and do not wish to have any.”

And Poland still refuses Soviet passage. Marshal Śmigły-Rydz captures the fear: “With the Germans we risk losing our freedom; with the Russians we lose our soul.” [Q-029] [C-027]

## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, 19 August, word reaches Moscow that the Poles do not want an agreement with Russia, and the negotiations therefore stall. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: at this point, let Ribbentrop come. Shall we meet on the 26th or 27th?

On Sunday, 20 August, Hitler sends Stalin a personal telegram. It is the first time a Western head of government has written personally to the Soviet leader; until then the Soviet Union had been treated like a country of plague victims, addressed indirectly and under the table. Hitler says he is pleased Stalin has agreed to receive Ribbentrop, but asks why they should wait until the 26th or 27th. Make it the 22nd or 23rd. [Q-030]

Monday, 21 August. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for a way to prevent war. It occurs to them to do what they did the previous year: a fine conference in Munich, an international conference where everyone can reach an agreement. So they telephone Berlin, ask to speak to Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and propose this splendid idea. From Ciano’s diary: Ribbentrop cannot be found. Finally, that afternoon, Ribbentrop replies and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That very day, 21 August, Stalin receives Hitler’s telegram at three in the afternoon and replies at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let us pause over this. It is a crucial decision, one that contributed greatly to making what happened possible. It was absolutely unexpected and shocking. We must be very clear about this because—and here I will give you my opinion; some will disagree—for many years we were brainwashed with the claim that all totalitarian regimes were the same, that Stalin’s Soviet Union and Hitler’s Germany were the same thing. That makes their agreement seem less strange. But the reality is that, to people at the time, it was an enormous shock, because Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union appeared to be the two most opposed and hostile countries in existence. And they were. The agreement is proof of extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and coldness on the part of both the Nazis and Stalin and his circle. The world was stunned by it.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are too hesitant and do not want an agreement, so he will make one with the Nazis, to spite them. Because he needs security. I will add one small thing, something few people know and perhaps many of you do not know: a small piece of the context of those days. When Stalin decides that things are going very badly—as Ciano says, “we are heading for a fight”—he decides that he absolutely needs security, even at the price of making a pact with the devil. They will not keep the pact, but for the moment they need it. Soviet distrust and the need to avoid immediate isolation were important motives, alongside the neutrality and territorial advantages offered by Germany. [C-029]

What is the piece of context that no one knew at the time, and that few remember even today? In those same days, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war with Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets and borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war involving entire army corps is under way there. Barbero gives a total of 70,000 dead and wounded, though casualty totals are disputed. The two governments agree not to reveal it to the world, and the world knows nothing about it, still less about the border between Mongolia and Manchuria. It is a full-blown war: the Japanese advance from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. The Soviets, incidentally, win decisively. On the 21st, with the battle under way, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany because he must secure at least one frontier. This fighting influenced his calculation, although the evidence does not reduce his decision to that single consideration. [C-030]

Ciano understands what Germany has achieved. “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke,” he writes. “The European situation is upset.” [Q-031]

That last conclusion is emblematic of the language and moral level of the regime.

On Tuesday, 22 August, Hitler summons his generals and gives the final orders for war. The moment is right, as in 1914, but he is not speaking about the armies’ readiness. War must come as soon as possible, while he is alive: Germany will never again have a leader with his power and following, and a criminal or an idiot could kill him at any moment. “Essentially all depends on me, on my existence, because of my political talents.” [Q-032] [C-031]

On Wednesday, 23 August, the agreement is announced in the world press. *Corriere della Sera* calls it “the grand political success of the Axis,” says the dark maneuver of the democratic encirclers has been broken, and reports astonishment and dismay in the “anti-totalitarian countries.” “Anti-totalitarian” is a bad word in Fascist usage: Italy is totalitarian; Mussolini invented the conception. The soft French and the others are anti-totalitarian. [Q-033]

Poland is beginning to give way and to think that perhaps an agreement with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible, but it is too late. On 23 August Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the publicly announced non-aggression pact. He also signs the secret protocol, which is of course not made public, in which Germany and the Soviet Union establish spheres in Polish territory, providing for Poland’s partition in substance while leaving the final settlement open. [C-032]

## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

<!-- transcript: U-00881–U-00942; omissions: none -->

Hitler receives the British ambassador that day because his remaining concern is to keep Britain out. Their conversation about alleged Polish atrocities against Germans becomes morbid. Hitler says he has reports of many Germans being castrated by Poles. Henderson happens to know one case: the German concerned was a sexual maniac who had been treated as he deserved. Hitler answers that he knows six cases, not one. [Q-034]

Hitler also says Poland is the last problem. “I am an artist, not a warrior.” Once the Polish question is settled, he says, he will retire from politics and devote himself to art. Henderson reports the conversation to London. [Q-035]

Friday, 25 August. Hitler orders the attack for the following day. Then he receives a letter in which Mussolini tells him the truth for the first time: Italy is not ready for war. But Mussolini does not have the courage to tell the whole truth. He writes that Italy cannot fight now without large supplies of raw materials from Germany. If Germany attacks Poland and the conflict remains local, Italy will give political and economic support; if it provokes general war, Italy can intervene only if Germany immediately supplies the matériel Italy lacks. [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He postpones the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what supplies of raw materials he needs. Mussolini’s letter was one immediate factor in the postponement, alongside the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance and Hitler’s continuing attempt to detach Britain from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, 26 August. Mussolini and the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces meet at Palazzo Venezia to draw up the list of raw materials to request from Germany. Ciano has primed the generals: in making this list, no criminal optimism. In other words, come down hard. They prepare the list, and I will read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—Italy’s production for two years, to give you an idea—two million tonnes of steel, Italy’s production for one year; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; and then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rosin, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. The nominal requirement was for sustaining a twelve-month war. The list went down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to the Italian ambassador in Berlin, Attolico, who takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks at the list and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” A crisis: they have told Attolico nothing, and he has no instructions. But he understands the mood and improvises: “Immediately.” It was calculated that 17,000 trains would be needed to send all that material to Italy—a contemporary estimate by Ciano, not an independently audited conversion. [C-034]

Hitler adds one small request, indicative of the future: Italy could help by sending workers to Germany. Quietly, a long story begins. The Germans have opened their eyes: they cannot count on Italian military help, but they can count on workers. Organized recruitment has existed since 1938; now the flow expands, eventually carrying roughly half a million Italian civilians to Germany by 1943, before the mass coercion of Italian military internees. [Q-038]

And here, quietly, an extended story develops further. It shows how circumstances change: the Germans suddenly realize that they cannot count on Italy for military assistance, but they can count on workers. Organized Italian labor migration to Germany had already begun in 1938; this request expanded an existing flow that would become increasingly coercive, distinct from the mass forced labor of Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]

## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, 27 August, rationing is introduced in Germany: food cards. Monday, 28 August. Britain and France still think that perhaps the Poles can be persuaded to negotiate, and they press the Polish government: go and ask the Germans exactly what they want, and let us discuss it.

Henderson reports that Hitler was not pleasant that evening; the interview was stormy. Hitler kept saying that he desired British friendship more than anything in the world but could not sacrifice Germany’s vital interests. Still, if Britain guaranteed that the Poles were truly willing to negotiate, they could send an emissary with full powers to Berlin the next day, and negotiation could continue. [Q-039]

Wednesday, 30 August. In Italy the world cycling championships in Varese are suspended. The sale of coffee to private individuals is banned—even drinking a cup at a café is prohibited—and all coffee is reserved for the armed forces. Rail traffic is suspended in France; the Vincennes Zoo is evacuated to save the animals. In all the capitals, they begin building air-raid shelters and distributing gas masks to the population, because everyone is convinced that war will begin with tremendous aerial bombardments of cities using poison gas. The broad preparations for aerial and gas attack are well established, though some individual details in this montage have not been independently verified. [C-036]

On Wednesday, 30 August, Halifax—the same man who two years earlier had told Hitler that Germany’s claims were understandable—writes to Henderson: “It is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.” Henderson replies that he had made the same observation to Hitler the previous evening. Hitler answered that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in an hour and a half. [Q-040]

At this point the British, desperate to avoid war, begin putting heavy pressure on the Polish government. Do not provoke the Germans. Stop Polish newspapers from publishing articles against Germany; end the anti-German campaign. Send a plenipotentiary to Berlin immediately to negotiate. The British ambassador returns to Ribbentrop and tells him they are doing everything possible, advising the Polish government to make peace and reach an agreement. Ribbentrop replies that British advice has so far had “damned little effect.” The ambassador answers—and reports it to London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

Could he not give them to the Polish ambassador in Berlin? Never; Germany will never summon him. And if the ambassador himself asks to be received? In that case, Ribbentrop says, perhaps they could see. Henderson reports that the conversation was extremely unpleasant and that throughout the evening Ribbentrop had been “aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] [C-037]

## 14. 31 August–1 September: The Attack Order, Gleiwitz, and Diplomatic Lag

<!-- transcript: U-01015–U-01095; omissions: none -->

On 31 August, the British tell the Poles: send a plenipotentiary to Berlin, or at least send your ambassador to knock on the door and ask for the negotiating terms. The Poles reply that they will not send the ambassador; it would be too humiliating. At 12:40 p.m. on 31 August, Hitler signs the order to attack the following morning. At one in the afternoon, the Poles have second thoughts. Without telling the British, they instruct the Polish ambassador in Berlin, Józef Lipski, to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms. Lipski goes. Ribbentrop’s account of the exchange records that he asked whether Lipski had authority to negotiate; Lipski replied that he did not. Ribbentrop then said he had thought Lipski would be authorized to negotiate and would report the démarche to Hitler. [Q-043] The attack decision had already preceded this belated Polish approach. [C-038]

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. The Gleiwitz incident is a curious thing, above all for this reason: after everything I have told you, today one would not believe that the Germans needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. Instead, they want a pretext, and the German secret services under Heydrich’s command have already prepared it. Heydrich—you know, Reinhard Heydrich, an SS big shot, head of the security services, who would be killed four years later by Czechoslovak partisans in Prague—has had a commando ready for some time, for two weeks, at Gleiwitz, a German radio station near the Polish border. Twelve men from the German secret services are waiting there with Polish uniforms and documents, ready to go into action when the moment comes. On the morning of 31 August the moment comes. Heydrich sends the commando the code phrase: “Grandmother died.” [Q-044]

The commando goes into action on German territory, seizes the Gleiwitz radio station, fires shots, and broadcasts a rambling statement in Polish—as we used to say in the days of the Red Brigades. A corpse is left behind, though the victim’s precise identity and clothing are less secure than the traditional account suggests. The episode is then presented as Polish aggression on German territory. The Germans claim the right to invade Poland and begin a few hours later, at dawn on 1 September. [C-039]

Halifax cables the British ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw. He cannot see why the Polish government is making difficulties: its ambassador should accept the German document, and refusal would be gravely misunderstood by public opinion. His Majesty’s Government is ready to do everything to assist mediation. He tells the ambassador in Warsaw to insist that the Polish ambassador go to Ribbentrop immediately to save the peace. [Q-045]

Halifax’s instruction lacked the latest outcome and was overtaken by events. [C-040]

## 15. Delayed Declarations and Fascist Propaganda

<!-- transcript: U-01096–U-01117; omissions: none -->

Two more days pass before Britain and France declare war on Germany, in the mad and naïve but sincere hope that this catastrophe can still be avoided. The interval included ultimatums and continued diplomatic exchanges: Britain’s ultimatum expired at eleven in the morning on 3 September, and France’s at five that afternoon. [C-041]

Italy chooses “non-belligerency.” Mussolini and Ciano had already calculated on 13–15 August that if the democracies fought, Italy must disengage honorably; if they backed down, Italy should seek “our share of the spoils,” specifically in Croatia and Dalmatia and ultimately against Belgrade. [Q-046] [C-042]

And beneath it: “The publication of this page of history confirms Germany’s conscious wisdom and moderation in the face of the blind intransigence of the other parties to the conflict.” [Q-047]
