## 14. 31 August–1 September: The Attack Order, Gleiwitz, and Diplomatic Lag

<!-- transcript: U-01015–U-01095; omissions: none -->

On 31 August, the British tell the Poles: send a plenipotentiary to Berlin, or at least send your ambassador to knock on the door and ask for the negotiating terms. The Poles reply that they will not send the ambassador; it would be too humiliating. At 12:40 p.m. on 31 August, Hitler signs the order to attack the following morning. At one in the afternoon, the Poles have second thoughts. Without telling the British, they instruct the Polish ambassador in Berlin, Józef Lipski, to go to Ribbentrop and ask for the terms. Lipski goes. Ribbentrop’s account of the exchange records that he asks whether Lipski has authority to negotiate; Lipski replies that he does not. Ribbentrop then says he had thought Lipski would be authorized to negotiate and would report the démarche to Hitler. [Q-043] The attack decision had already preceded this belated Polish approach. [C-038]

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. It is curious above all for this reason: after everything I have told you, a person today would not believe that the Germans needed a pretext for declaring war on Poland. Yet they want one, and the German security services under Heydrich had already prepared it. Reinhard Heydrich, a leading SS figure and head of the security services, will be killed four years later by Czechoslovak agents in Prague.

That evening comes the famous Gleiwitz incident. The Gleiwitz incident is a curious thing, above all for this reason: after everything I have told you, today one would not believe that the Germans needed a pretext to declare war on Poland. Instead, they want a pretext, and the German secret services under Heydrich’s command have already prepared it. Heydrich—you know, Reinhard Heydrich, an SS big shot, head of the security services, who will be killed four years later by Czechoslovak partisans in Prague—has had a commando ready for some time, for two weeks, at Gleiwitz, a German radio station near the Polish border. The best reconstruction has five or six attackers in civilian clothes at Gleiwitz, not twelve men in Polish uniforms. [N-033] On the morning of 31 August the moment comes. Heydrich sends the commando the code phrase: “Grandmother died.” [Q-044]

The commando goes into action on German territory, seizes the Gleiwitz radio station, fires shots, and broadcasts a rambling statement in Polish—as we used to say in the days of the Red Brigades. A corpse is left behind, though the victim’s precise identity and clothing are less secure than the traditional account suggests. The episode is then presented as Polish aggression on German territory. The Germans claim the right to invade Poland and begin a few hours later, at dawn on 1 September. [C-039]

Meanwhile, during the night, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax still hopes that genuine negotiations with Poland can begin and worries that the Poles do not want to send their ambassador. He does not know that the Polish ambassador had already gone the previous evening and been rebuffed. On the night of 1 September Halifax sends instructions to the ambassadors in Berlin and Warsaw—separate messages that Barbero presents together—and what follows is perhaps the most inane telegram ever sent by a British government. The exchange reads: “Halifax: 'I do not see why the Polish Government should feel difficulty about authorising Polish Ambassador to accept a document from the German Government, and I earnestly hope that they may be able to modify their instructions to him in this respect. [...] On the other hand, a refusal by them to receive proposals would be gravely misunderstood by outside opinion. [...] I should have thought that the Polish Ambassador could surely be instructed to receive and transmit a document [...]. If negotiations are initiated, His Majesty's Government will at all times be ready, if desired, to lend any assistance in their power to achieve a just settlement. [...] Please speak to M. Beck immediately in the above sense.' Kennard: 'Your telegram of 1st September was decyphered at 4 a. m. to-day. M. Lipski had already called on the German Foreign Minister at 6:30 p. m. yesterday. In view of this fact, which was followed by German invasion of Poland at dawn to-day, it was clearly useless for me to take the action suggested.'” [Q-045]

Halifax’s instruction lacks the latest outcome and is overtaken by events. [C-040]
