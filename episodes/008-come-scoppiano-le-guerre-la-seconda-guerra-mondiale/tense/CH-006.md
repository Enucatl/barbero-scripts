## 6. Poland’s confidence and the destruction of Czechoslovakia

<!-- transcript: U-00359–U-00443; omissions: none -->

But Chamberlain and Daladier are not the only ones to be taken in—besides Mussolini, who believes it too. Even Poland believes it. Poland, let us remember, will later be the victim when war breaks out. Yet when Czechoslovakia is partitioned under the Munich settlement, Poland takes advantage of it and seizes a small piece of Czechoslovakia as well. Poland is a militaristic country, governed by colonels, a semi-democracy: authoritarian, fiercely nationalist, and anti-communist in the extreme. At that moment the Poles are convinced that friendship with Hitler is possible, since they are all anti-communists. Who is more anti-communist than Hitler? We Poles are—even more so. Thus, in the final months of 1938 and the opening months of 1939, the Poles believe they are Hitler’s friends. [C-015]

And Hitler? On 30 January 1939, with seven months left before the outbreak of the world war, Hitler tells the Reichstag, the German parliament: “Even during the troubled months of the past year, German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life.” [Q-015]

At the end of January 1939, Polish foreign minister Józef Beck meets Ribbentrop in Warsaw—not Hitler in Berlin, as I have just made it sound. In Ribbentrop’s account: “M. Beck made no secret of the fact that Poland had aspirations directed toward the Soviet Ukraine and a connection with the Black Sea; but at the same time he called attention to the supposed dangers to Poland that in the Polish view would arise from a treaty with Germany directed against the Soviet Union.” [Q-016] [C-015]

Meanwhile, as these fine conversations go on through January, February, and March 1939, Czechoslovakia is falling apart. It has lost the entire belt inhabited by Germans, the bulk of its industry, and its border fortifications. Slovakia now wants independence. It is an old story, as you know: that will later happen, and today Slovakia is independent, having separated. Czechoslovakia is collapsing and is about to be invaded by Hungary. At a certain point Hitler decides to dispose of the problem. [C-016]

On 14 March 1939, Hitler orders the Czechoslovak president, Emil Hácha, to come and speak with him in Berlin. Hácha obeys. After one in the morning on 15 March, Hitler informs him that German troops will enter at six and that any resistance will be crushed. Göring, not Hitler, threatens Prague with air attack. Hácha collapses and receives medical attention and injections; the consulted primary records do not establish a diagnosed heart attack or that doctors deliberately kept him awake. He signs a declaration—not a literal request for German intervention—whose text reads: “The President of the Czecho-Slovak State declared that, in order to serve this end and to reach a final pacification, he confidently placed the fate of the Czech people and of their country in the hands of the Fuehrer of the German Reich.” [Q-017] German forces occupy Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March, after Slovakia had declared independence under German pressure on 14 March. [N-014] [C-017]

On 15 March the Germans invade Czechoslovakia. No one in the world intervenes. Hitler takes note: once again, no one has intervened. [C-017]

But, you will say, it is about time: Chamberlain begins to think that perhaps Hitler cannot be trusted after all. Two days later, on 17 March 1939, Chamberlain speaks in Birmingham. [N-015] I will translate his speech for you. He is plainly saying things he would very much rather not say; you can hear it in the syntax. Chamberlain says:

“Does not the question inevitably arise in our minds, if it is so easy to discover good reasons for ignoring assurances so solemnly and so repeatedly given, what reliance can be placed upon any other assurances that come from the same source? [...] Is this the end of an old adventure, or is it the beginning of a new? Is this the last attack upon a small State, or is it to be followed by others? Is this, in fact, a step in the direction of an attempt to dominate the world by force?” [Q-018]

Chamberlain is evidently hoping someone will tell him, “No, don’t worry.” No. But the doubt—the doubt is beginning to arise. The proof comes at once. The Germans had invaded Czechoslovakia—yes, Czechoslovakia—on 15 March. [C-018]
