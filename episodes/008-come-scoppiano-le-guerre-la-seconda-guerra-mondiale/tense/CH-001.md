## 1. Versailles, German grievances, and the double illusion

<!-- transcript: U-00001–U-00103; omissions: none -->

Well, good evening. Tonight we have a journey ahead of us which is, how shall I put it, rather tough. You will say that last night was no joke either, but tonight the outbreak of the Second World War is not a cheerful story. The only cheerful notes are provided by Italy’s participation. Whatever one may think of Mussolini, Galeazzo Ciano, and so on, they provide the only note of comedy in those fairly dramatic weeks, as you will see.

So, how does the Second World War break out? It has been written that in reality the Second World War breaks out in 1919, when the Treaty of Versailles is signed. The Treaty of Versailles, which brings the First World War to an end, brings it to an end by creating an endless number of grievances and frustrations. You will remember that even Italy, which has nevertheless obtained Trento and Trieste and has swallowed South Tyrol, is not satisfied. On the contrary, in Italy people speak of the “mutilated victory”: “Our victory, you will not be mutilated. No one can break your knees or clip your wings. Where are you running? Where are you rising?” [Q-001] Even victorious Italy then sinks almost into civil war and into dictatorship, as a consequence of that war which has ended badly. Imagine the countries that have lost. [C-001]

Germany, which has lost, emerges from the First World War and from the Treaty of Versailles frustrated, with the feeling that it has suffered an injustice. What injustice? To begin with, Germany has been forced to cede territories to a new country. The Treaty of Versailles creates many new states, among them Poland. Germany cedes to Poland—is forced to cede to Poland—a large tract of territory inhabited by Germans. And, what is more, situations are created that are impossible to manage.

There is one city that today no longer matters to anyone: it is Gdańsk in Poland. We have hardly heard it mentioned since the days of Lech Wałęsa, but at the time it is discussed constantly. Danzig is a great port, an important German city, located on the border between the new Poland and Germany. Both claim it. The victors at Versailles decide: all right, let us make it a Free City, independent, neither German nor Polish. The city of Danzig is inhabited entirely by Germans who want to be part of Germany, but they are not allowed to.

And in Germany a movement of public opinion begins immediately, somewhat comparable to what had happened in Italy before the war: Trento, Trieste, irredentism, the separated brothers. In Germany people campaign: Danzig, Danzig is German, Danzig is German and they did not give it to us. It is a disgrace; one day we shall have to go back to the question. [Q-002]

But then there are other problems born from the end of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The Austro-Hungarian Empire is dissolved and many new states come into being: Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia. And with them come many problems. Austria is small and inhabited entirely by Germans. Most of them would like to say: all right, since things have ended as they have, let us become part of Germany; we Austrians are Germans too. But the victors do not allow it. Austria has to remain independent.

Then there is Czechoslovakia, the state of the Czechs and the Slovaks. But in those regions, in the days of the Austrian Empire, there had lived a great many Germans—that is, Austrian citizens who spoke German. They now find themselves citizens of Czechoslovakia, but they do not feel Czechoslovak. So there is this whole collection of problems created by Versailles, problems which in themselves are also understandable. To a large extent they are problems of minorities living abroad.

Tonight I shall have to speak for a while about the remote causes of the Second World War. I would have liked to say to you: very well, it is 10 August 1939, and this happens. We shall do that, but first we have to speak a little about these remote causes, because otherwise we shall understand nothing. And so the remote causes are precisely those I have described: a Germany that emerges frustrated, with a series of claims against Poland over Danzig; against Austria, because the Austrians are all Germans and it is not clear why they should not be part of Germany; and against Czechoslovakia, because of the Germans living there who feel discriminated against.

In 1933, in Germany, the Nazis win the elections and Hitler comes to power. Hitler comes to power with a program that includes many fine things, but the point the Germans perhaps feel most strongly is this: we must revise the Treaty of Versailles; we must revise the Europe that emerged from Versailles. It is an unjust Europe, an iniquitous treaty. Germany has suffered, and that is not right. We must return to the matter and alter the borders. [C-002]

The story of how the Second World War breaks out is, at bottom, the story of a double illusion. One is the illusion of the great democratic powers, Britain and France: the illusion that Hitler can be given some satisfaction, because on certain matters Hitler is, after all, right. It is the illusion that one can say, very well, let us negotiate, let us reach an agreement—Danzig there, and so on—without causing any great upheaval. An illusion, as we shall see.

The other illusion is Hitler’s: that it is possible to take everything back, and something more besides, and once again transform Germany into the great power of Europe without anyone seriously opposing him. The illusion of the democracies, the illusion of the Western democracies—France and Britain. [C-003]
