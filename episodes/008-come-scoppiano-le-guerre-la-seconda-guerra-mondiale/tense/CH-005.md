## 5. Munich, peace at any price, and appeasement’s failure

<!-- transcript: U-00257–U-00358; omissions: none -->

At last we come to September. The situation has become so serious that the British and French governments decide that the Czechoslovaks must be forced to make concessions to Hitler. Something has to be invented. They ought to invent a conference—today we would call it a negotiating table. At the time they had not yet invented the “table,” nor had they invented the “summit.” They ought to hold a conference, they say.

How can they persuade Hitler to accept a negotiating table? Ask Mussolini, since the two of them are friends. The British and French approach Mussolini and say to him, in substance—I am summarizing—sound Hitler out and see whether he is willing to arrange for all of us to meet and reach an agreement, in order to accommodate the German demands on Czechoslovakia. Hitler agrees.

On 29 and 30 September 1938 comes the episode that will later always be deplored as, how shall I put it, one of the low points of European politics, although at least two of its four protagonists are acting in good faith: the Munich Conference. In Munich, Bavaria, there meet Hitler, Mussolini, the British prime minister Chamberlain—Neville Chamberlain—and the French prime minister, Daladier. [C-012]

At least Chamberlain and Daladier are acting in good faith. It is difficult to have any doubt about that. They have a nightmare, the same nightmare as the entire generation which saw the First World War. Like the whole generation which saw the slaughter of the First World War, the politicians of those years have just one thing in mind whenever a crisis arises: anything, but not another war. Never again war, at any price. Or, if you prefer: “our policy was the policy of peace at any price.” [Q-012] [C-013]

What does “any price” mean? Well, if Czechoslovakia has to surrender a piece of territory to Germany, so be it; that is better. The Munich Conference consists of Britain, France, Italy, and Germany meeting to decide which piece of Czechoslovakia will be ceded to Germany. The Czechoslovaks are outside the door waiting to learn what has been decided. And the Munich Conference decides: the whole area populated by Germans, the Sudetenland, is ceded to Germany.

Hitler takes note. Not only have they failed to obstruct me; they have actually helped me take a piece of Czechoslovakia.

And here, how shall I put it—precisely, as I said—I do not know. It is difficult to talk about it, because on the one hand one feels like saying what was said for fifty years: what a tragedy, politicians at that level. And yet, and yet, they are, how shall I put it, first-rate politicians who fail to understand what is happening, and they are acting in good faith.

Chamberlain returns to London. He has been mocked from then right down to the present day for this return to London from Munich. Chamberlain returns to London. Already as he comes down the aircraft steps he is waving the sheet of paper containing the agreements. You understand? There are the photographers, the crowd, everything around him. Chamberlain waves the paper and speaks to the crowd.

I shall translate for you directly Chamberlain’s speech to the crowd on that first day, as he came down from the aircraft returning from Munich: “The settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem, which has now been achieved, is, in my view, only the prelude to a larger settlement in which all Europe may find peace. [...] We regard the agreement signed last night and the Anglo-German naval agreement as symbolic of the desire of our two peoples never to go to war with one another again. [...] My good friends, this is the second time in our history that there has come back from Germany to Downing Street peace with honour. I believe it is peace for our time. [...] And now I recommend you to go home and sleep quietly in your beds.” We are eleven months before the outbreak of the Second World War, remember. This is Chamberlain’s speech to the London crowd on his return from Munich. [Q-013]

We are eleven months before the outbreak of the Second World War; let us remember that. Chamberlain says that that morning he had had another meeting with the German chancellor, Herr Hitler, and here was the treaty bearing Hitler’s signature and his own. The two of them regarded the agreement signed the previous night as a symbol of the desire of their two peoples never again to go to war against one another. My dear friends: the British prime minister had returned from Germany bringing peace with honor. He believed it was peace for their time. Go home and have a good, peaceful sleep. That is Chamberlain’s speech to the London crowd on his return from Munich.

Chamberlain’s policy has a precise name, one which later becomes, in the vocabulary of British politics—we shall see it again tomorrow evening when we talk about Mrs Thatcher—an ugly word: *appeasement*. Pacification. Peace, whatever the cost.

And *appeasement* is indeed shocking today. They do not realize. No, they do not realize. The tragic irony is that they are prepared to do anything in order not to let war break out. The result is that they make it break out. [C-014]

Mind you, Hitler is good at it. Hitler is extremely skillful at leading people around in circles and deceiving those he deals with. At Munich Hitler says to Chamberlain—and listen to this; Chamberlain himself reports it, it seems. He says: I spoke with Hitler. Speaking with great sincerity, Hitler told me: “In the first place he repeated to me with great earnestness what he had said already at Berchtesgaden, namely, that this was the last of his territorial ambitions in Europe and that he had no wish to include in the Reich people of other races than Germans.” [Q-014]

Hitler has said it. He is so sincere. Therefore: sleep peacefully.
