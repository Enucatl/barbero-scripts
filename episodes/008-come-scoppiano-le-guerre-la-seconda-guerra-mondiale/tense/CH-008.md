## 8. Italy’s alliance without the means for war

<!-- transcript: U-00522–U-00560; omissions: none -->

And Italy. Italy is the closest of friends with Nazi Germany. On 22 May 1939 they sign the Pact of Steel amid tremendous press enthusiasm. In Hitler’s telegram to Victor Emmanuel III, the two peoples are described as being “In this historic hour I wish to express to Your Majesty my profound satisfaction that our two peoples are bound to one another by an unbreakable friendship and community of destiny.” [Q-021]

But in reality Mussolini is worried. He is worried because Ribbentrop has repeatedly assured the Italians orally that Germany does not want war for the time being—though the treaty itself contains no such guarantee—and Mussolini is beginning to feel less certain. [C-023]

The first thing we must understand clearly—something that is not always stated so plainly, although it is well known and documented—is why Mussolini is so uncertain. Italy at that moment is severely short of resources. Italy has fought the Ethiopian War and has intervened in the Spanish Civil War on Franco’s side. Everything has gone well: the Ethiopian War was a triumph, and things went fairly well in Spain too. But wars cost terrifying sums. The Ethiopian and Spanish wars have torn a chasm in the Italian budget and depleted matériel and armament reserves. There is severe scarcity, especially for modern weapons. [C-022]

Now, as it happens, in those last few years every country has understood which way the wind is blowing and is experimenting with new weapons. Everywhere, new-concept fighters such as the Spitfire and new tanks are entering production. Armaments that had been adequate only two or three years earlier are rapidly becoming obsolete. Italy lacks the industrial capacity and resources to modernize quickly enough. Mussolini knows it, even if every so often he prefers to forget. But in the summer of 1939 he knows, and he has one concern: that the Germans might take it into their heads to start a war. [C-022]
