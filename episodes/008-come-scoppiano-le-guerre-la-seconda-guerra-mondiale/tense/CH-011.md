## 11. 19–23 August: The Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Mongolian Context

<!-- transcript: U-00728–U-00880; omissions: none -->

On Saturday, 19 August, word reaches Moscow that the Poles do not want an agreement with Russia, and the negotiations therefore stall. Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov writes to Berlin: at this point, let Ribbentrop come. Shall we meet on the 26th or 27th?

Sunday, 20 August: a telegram from Hitler to Stalin. Bear in mind that this is the first time in history that a Western head of government has written personally to the head of the Soviet government. Until then, the Soviet Union had been treated like a plague-stricken country: people spoke to it only indirectly, under the table. The first telegram from a Western ruler to Stalin is from Hitler. On 20 August he tells him: “Hitler: 'I accept the draft of the non-aggression pact that your Foreign Minister, Herr Molotov, delivered but consider it urgently necessary to clarify the questions connected with it as soon as possible. [...] I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23.' Stalin: 'I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23.'” [Q-030]

Monday, 21 August. Mussolini and Ciano rack their brains for a way to prevent war. It occurs to them to do what they did the previous year: a fine conference in Munich, an international conference where everyone can reach an agreement. So they telephone Berlin, ask to speak to Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop, and propose this splendid idea. From Ciano’s diary: Ribbentrop cannot be found. Finally, that afternoon, Ribbentrop replies and says, “I cannot discuss these matters now. I am waiting for an important message from Moscow.”

That very day, 21 August, Stalin receives Hitler’s telegram at three in the afternoon and replies at five: “I thank you for the letter. I hope that the German-Soviet non-aggression pact will mark a decided turn for the better in the political relations between our countries. [...] The Soviet Government has authorized me to inform you that it agrees to Herr von Ribbentrop’s arriving in Moscow on August 23.” [Q-030]

Let us pause over this. It is a crucial decision, one that contributes greatly to making what happens possible. It is absolutely unexpected and shocking. We must be very clear about this because—and here I will give you my opinion; some will disagree—for many years we were brainwashed with the claim that all totalitarian regimes were the same, that Stalin’s Soviet Union and Hitler’s Germany were the same thing. That makes their agreement seem less strange. But the reality is that, to people at the time, it is an enormous shock, because Nazi Germany and the communist Soviet Union appear to be the two most opposed and hostile countries in existence. And they are. The agreement is proof of extraordinary cynicism, cunning, and coldness on the part of both the Nazis and Stalin and his circle. The world is stunned by it.

Stalin has decided that the British and French are too hesitant and do not want an agreement, so he will make one with the Nazis, to spite them. Because he needs security. I will add one small thing, something few people know and perhaps many of you do not know: a small piece of the context of those days. When Stalin decides that things are going very badly—as Ciano says, “we are heading for a fight”—he decides that he absolutely needs security, even at the price of making a pact with the devil. They will not keep the pact, but for the moment they need it. Soviet distrust and the need to avoid immediate isolation are important motives, alongside the neutrality and territorial advantages offered by Germany. [C-029]

What is the piece of context that no one knows at the time, and that few remember even today? In those same days, the Soviet Union is fighting an undeclared war with Japan in Mongolia. Mongolia is a communist republic allied with the Soviets and borders Manchukuo, the part of China occupied by Japan. In the summer of 1939, a full-scale war involving entire army corps is under way there. Barbero gives a total of 70,000 dead and wounded, though casualty totals are disputed. The two governments agree not to reveal it to the world, and the world knows nothing about it, still less about the border between Mongolia and Manchuria. It is a full-blown war: the Japanese advance from China into Mongolia, the Mongolians ask the Soviets for help, and the Soviets come. The Soviets, incidentally, win decisively. On the 21st, with the battle under way, Stalin decides to make a pact with Germany because he must secure at least one frontier. This fighting influences his calculation, although the evidence does not reduce his decision to that single consideration. [C-030]

That same day the news becomes public. Mussolini and Ciano, like the whole world, are stunned by the agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union. They are stunned, but also impressed: these Germans are formidable. Ciano’s diary says: “There is no doubt that the Germans have pulled off a masterstroke. The European situation has been overturned. Can France and England, which based their entire anti-Axis policy on alliance with the Soviets, still count on the unconditional support of the extremist masses? And will the encirclement system built through the small states still hold now that its Moscow cornerstone has collapsed? In any case, we should not rush our decisions: wait, and if possible remain ready to take our own share of the spoils in Croatia and Dalmatia.” [Q-031]

That last conclusion is emblematic of the language and moral level of the regime.

On Tuesday, 22 August, Hitler summons the generals and gives the final orders for war. He says the moment is favorable, as in 1914. The interesting point is that he is not referring to the armies’ readiness. He has something else in mind. In this famous speech to his generals on 22 August, Hitler says: “My own personality and that of Mussolini. Essentially it depends on me, my existence, because of my political activities. Furthermore the fact that probably no one will ever again have the confidence of the whole German people as I do. There will probably never again be a man in the future with more authority than I have. My existence is therefore a factor of great value. But I can be eliminated at any time by a criminal or an idiot. The second personal factor is the Duce. His existence is also decisive. If something happens to him, Italy's loyalty to the alliance will no longer be certain. [...] The Duce is the man with the strongest nerves in Italy.” [Q-032] Hitler’s political culture is bound up with political terrorism and assassination: to him, being killed is a normal possibility. So the war must be launched while he is still alive, and while Mussolini is still there. Hitler still trusts his old friend and deludes himself about Mussolini at the very moment when Mussolini has already decided to pull out. [C-031]

Wednesday, 23 August. The agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union is announced in the world press. The *Corriere della Sera* proclaims: “THE GERMAN-SOVIET AGREEMENT / The Axis's great political success / has broken the murky manoeuvre of the democratic encirclers / A completely new situation, rich in highly important developments - Von Ribbentrop has left by air for Moscow - The enormous worldwide response - Astonishment and dismay in the anti-totalitarian countries - Poland will have to seek its salvation by negotiating rapidly with Germany” [Q-033]. “Anti-totalitarian” is naturally a term of abuse: *we* are totalitarian—Mussolini invented the concept of being a totalitarian country—whereas those feeble French and the others are “anti-totalitarian.”

Poland is beginning to give way and to think that perhaps an agreement with the Soviet Union—even with the devil—might be possible, but it is too late. On 23 August Ribbentrop flies to Moscow and signs the publicly announced non-aggression pact. He also signs the secret protocol, which is of course not made public, in which Germany and the Soviet Union establish spheres in Polish territory, providing for Poland’s partition in substance while leaving the final settlement open. [C-032]
