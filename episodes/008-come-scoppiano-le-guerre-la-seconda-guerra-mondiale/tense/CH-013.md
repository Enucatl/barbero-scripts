## 13. 27–30 August: Negotiations Staged to Fail

<!-- transcript: U-00943–U-01014; omissions: none -->

On Sunday, 27 August, rationing is introduced in Germany: food cards. Monday, 28 August. Britain and France still think that perhaps the Poles can be persuaded to negotiate, and they press the Polish government: go and ask the Germans exactly what they want, and let us discuss it.

Tuesday, 29 August. In Italy, private cars are banned from the roads and restaurants face austerity measures: menus may offer only one main dish, either meat or fish, but only one. The British ambassador goes to Hitler to see whether negotiations are genuinely still possible at the last moment. The surviving documents read: “Henderson: 'Interview this evening was of a stormy character and Herr Hitler far less reasonable than yesterday. [...] He kept saying that he wanted British friendship more than anything in the world, but he could not sacrifice Germany's vital interests therefor.' Henderson's accompanying report: 'German Government, note adds, counts on arrival of such plenipotentiary to-morrow, Wednesday, 30th August. [...] I remarked that this phrase sounded like an ultimatum.' Hitler's written reply: 'The German Government, accordingly, in these circumstances agree to accept the British Government's offer of their good offices in securing the despatch to Berlin of a Polish Emissary with full powers. They count on the arrival of this Emissary on Wednesday, the 30th August, 1939.'” [Q-039]

Wednesday, 30 August. In Italy the world cycling championships in Varese are suspended. The sale of coffee to private individuals is banned—even drinking a cup at a café is prohibited—and all coffee is reserved for the armed forces. Rail traffic is suspended in France; the Vincennes Zoo is evacuated to save the animals. In all the capitals, they begin building air-raid shelters and distributing gas masks to the population, because everyone is convinced that war will begin with tremendous aerial bombardments of cities using poison gas. The broad preparations for aerial and gas attack are well established, though some individual details in this montage have not been independently verified. [C-036]

That day, Wednesday, 30 August, British Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax—the same man who had told Hitler two years earlier that his demands were, fundamentally, understandable—writes to the British ambassador in Berlin. The exchange reads: Halifax: ‘We shall give careful consideration to German Government's reply, but it is, of course, unreasonable to expect that we can produce a Polish representative in Berlin to-day, and German Government must not expect this.’ Henderson: ‘I had made similar observation to Herr Hitler yesterday evening, his reply being that one could fly from Warsaw to Berlin in one and a half hours.’ [Q-040]

At this point the British, desperate to avoid war, begin putting heavy pressure on the Polish government. Do not provoke the Germans. Stop Polish newspapers from publishing articles against Germany; end the anti-German campaign. Send a plenipotentiary to Berlin immediately to negotiate. The British ambassador returns to Ribbentrop and tells him they are doing everything possible, advising the Polish government to make peace and reach an agreement. Ribbentrop replies that British advice has so far had “damned little effect.” The ambassador answers—and reports it to London—“I replied with restraint that I was surprised to hear such language from a Foreign Minister.” [Q-041]

At that point another comedy begins, a kind of German black comedy. Ribbentrop says they had prepared terms for an agreement with Poland. The British ambassador reports: “Herr von Ribbentrop's reply was to produce a lengthy document which he read out in German aloud at top speed. Imagining that he would eventually hand it to me I did not attempt to follow too closely the sixteen or more articles which it contained. [...] When I asked Herr von Ribbentrop for text of these proposals in accordance with undertaking in the German reply of yesterday, he asserted that it was now too late as Polish representative had not arrived in Berlin by midnight. [...] In the most violent terms Herr von Ribbentrop said that he would never ask the Ambassador to visit him. He hinted that if Polish Ambassador asked him for interview it might be different. [...] We parted on that note, but I must tell you that Herr von Ribbentrop's whole demeanour during an unpleasant interview was aping Herr Hitler at his worst.” [Q-042] The arbitrary deadline, rapid oral reading, and refusal to hand over the text obstruct genuine negotiation.
