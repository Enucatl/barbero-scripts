## 12. 23–26 August: Hitler’s Last Assurances and Italy’s “Molybdenum List”

<!-- transcript: U-00881–U-00942; omissions: none -->

That day Hitler receives the British ambassador, because his one remaining concern is to keep Britain out. He speaks to him about the atrocities the Poles are supposedly committing against the German minority in Poland. Here is a small detail I could have omitted, as I have omitted many others, but even tonight I have not had the courage to suppress too many details. I will have to hurry, because I am taking too long. Still, this is a morbid conversation between Hitler and the British ambassador. Henderson’s report says: “He then violently attacked the Poles, talked of 100,000 German refugees from Poland, excesses against Germans, closing of German institutions and Polish systematic persecution of German nationals generally. [...] He had mentioned the castration of Germans. I happened to be aware of one case. The German in question was a sex-maniac, who had been treated as he deserved. Herr Hitler's retort was that there had not been one case but six.” [Q-034]

And they go on like this. Hitler also tells him that Poland is the last issue. Henderson reports Hitler’s words this way: “Among various points mentioned by Herr Hitler were: [...] that he was by nature an artist not a politician, and that once the Polish question was settled he would end his life as an artist and not as a war-monger; [...] that once the Polish question was settled he himself would settle down.” [Q-035] The ambassador reports this to London.

Friday, 25 August. Hitler orders the attack for the following day. Then he receives a letter from Mussolini in which Mussolini tells him the truth for the first time: Italy is not ready to go to war. But he does not have the courage to tell him everything. Mussolini writes: “If Germany attacks Poland and Poland's allies counterattack Germany, I put before you the advisability of my not taking the initiative in military operations, given the present condition of Italian military preparations, which has repeatedly and promptly been made known to you, Führer, and to von Ribbentrop. Our intervention can nevertheless be immediate if Germany immediately gives us the war materiel and raw materials needed to withstand the blow that the French and British will direct mainly against us. In our meetings war had been envisaged for after 1942, and by then I would have been ready on land, at sea, and in the air, according to the agreed plans.” [Q-036]

Hitler is disappointed, but he falls for it. He postpones the attack on Poland for a few days and asks Mussolini exactly what supplies of raw materials he needs. Mussolini’s letter is one immediate factor in the postponement, alongside the newly signed Anglo-Polish alliance and Hitler’s continuing attempt to detach Britain from Poland. [C-033]

Saturday, 26 August. Mussolini and the chiefs of staff of the Italian armed forces meet at Palazzo Venezia to draw up the list of raw materials to request from Germany. Ciano has primed the generals: in making this list, no criminal optimism. In other words, come down hard. They prepare the list, and I will read it to you: six million tonnes of coal—Italy’s production for two years, to give you an idea—two million tonnes of steel, Italy’s production for one year; seven million tonnes of mineral oils; one million tonnes of timber; and then copper, sodium nitrate, potassium salts, rosin, rubber, toluene, turpentine, lead, tin, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, titanium, and twenty tonnes of zirconium. The nominal requirement is for sustaining a twelve-month war. The list will go down in history as the “molybdenum list.” [Q-037] [C-034]

They send it to the Italian ambassador in Berlin, Attolico, who takes it to Ribbentrop. Ribbentrop looks at the list and asks, “By when do you need all this material?” A crisis: they have told Attolico nothing, and he has no instructions. But he understands the mood and improvises: “Immediately.” It is calculated that 17,000 trains will be needed to send all that material to Italy—a contemporary estimate by Ciano, not an independently audited conversion. [C-034]

Ribbentrop goes to Hitler, and they understand at last. The penny finally drops: Italy will not take part; it will remain outside. Very well, so be it. Hitler writes to Mussolini: “I have received the information concerning your final attitude. I respect the motives and influences determining your resolution. Perhaps it will nevertheless be for the best. Of course, in my opinion, this will be true only if the world does not receive any inkling of the intended attitude of Italy, at least not till actual fighting starts. [...] You and your people could help me most in this hard struggle by supporting me with Italian manpower, manpower for industrial as well as agricultural purposes.” [Q-038]

And here, quietly, an extended story develops further. It shows how circumstances change: the Germans suddenly realize that they cannot count on Italy for military assistance, but they can count on workers. Organized Italian labor migration to Germany had already begun in 1938; this request expands an existing flow that will become increasingly coercive, distinct from the mass forced labor of Italian military internees after 1943. [C-035]
