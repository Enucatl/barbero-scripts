## 10. 12–18 August: rival negotiations and Polish refusal

<!-- transcript: U-00596–U-00727; omissions: none -->

The day after Ciano’s interview with Ribbentrop is Saturday, 12 August. It is the Ferragosto holiday season; this war, like the First World War, will break out in the middle of summer. People do not yet take as many holidays as they do now, but they do take some, and at Ferragosto people go away. On Saturday, 12 August, for example, Milan empties: half a million people take the train to the seaside.

That same day the Anglo-French delegation finally arrives in Moscow after months of talk, sent to negotiate a treaty. The Soviets are deeply disappointed. The delegation consists of a French general, Joseph Doumenc, and a British admiral, Reginald Drax—figures below the highest rank. The Russians ask, “Do you have full powers to sign a treaty?”

Let me pause for a second. This matter of full powers is extremely important for understanding diplomatic exchanges in this period. There is, of course, the telephone, the telegraph, and the airplane; nevertheless, everyone considers it essential to have someone on the spot who can sign. The Soviets ask: “You have come to negotiate—excellent. This is urgent. Do you have full powers?” Drax can negotiate but not sign. Doumenc has authority from Daladier to conclude a military convention, although he does not carry conventional plenipotentiary credentials and remains dependent on Paris. In effect, the answer is: “You tell us, and we will report back.” The Soviets are deeply disappointed. [C-025]

That same day the Soviet government sends an invitation: it would be a good thing if Ribbentrop came to Moscow, because they have matters to discuss. Sunday, 13 August: high summer, holidays, the Venice Film Festival; Maria Bellonci wins the Viareggio Prize for *Lucrezia Borgia*.

That same day Hitler speaks with Mussolini. It is exactly as Ciano has said: Germany will make war on Poland and attack at the first incident. Nor is that all. Hitler explains that it is mid-August. His generals calculate that defeating Poland will take a month and a half, six weeks at most. By mid-October autumn will arrive, and Poland’s roads and airfields will become unusable in the mud. Therefore the campaign has to be over by mid-October. They need a month and a half, so they will attack by the end of August. This is Hitler’s operational estimate and planning rationale. [C-026]

Mussolini and the others in Italy are appalled. In his diary entry of 13 August, Ciano writes: “I return to Rome disgusted with Germany, its leaders, and their way of acting. They have deceived us and lied to us. And today they are about to drag us into an adventure that we did not want and that could imperil the regime and the country.” [Q-025]

Monday, 14 August: a holiday atmosphere, with the Salzburg Festival under way. Hitler, who is in the mountains, attends Mozart’s *The Abduction from the Seraglio* at the festival.

In Moscow the Russians meet the Anglo-French delegation and put their cards on the table. Do they want an agreement to defend Poland? Very well, but the Soviets want a guarantee that it will be taken seriously: if war comes, the Poles must allow Soviet troops onto Polish territory. Are the Soviets acting in good faith or bad faith? Are they already thinking of gobbling Poland up, or do they sincerely believe that if war has to be fought, it has to be fought seriously? We do not know. In any case, this is their demand. What do the British admiral and French general answer? “That is not within our authority. We must report back. We will report to Paris and London, and then we shall see.” Transit is the immediate impasse, but not the only cause of the talks’ failure: Anglo-French delays and mandates, Polish fear of Soviet occupation, Soviet bargaining, and the German option all matter. [C-027]

That same Monday, 14 August, Ribbentrop decides that the Russian idea of his going to Moscow is a good one. He will go on Tuesday, 15 August—Ferragosto. The French general has written that, to sign the agreement, the Russians want a guarantee that the Poles will let them enter in the event of war. The Poles have to be asked.

French foreign minister Georges Bonnet seeks the Polish ambassador, Juliusz Łukasiewicz. Where do you suppose the Polish ambassador is on Ferragosto? At the seaside. They bring him back to Paris and tell him: allow the Russians passage in the event of war against Germany. The ambassador says that he will have to ask Warsaw, but in his opinion the answer will be no, because the Poles fear the Russians even more than the Germans.

Bonnet warns the Polish ambassador that the French understand Hitler to have declared that he will dispose of Poland in three weeks. The ambassador’s reply is important for understanding the atmosphere and the nature of the Polish regime. Bonnet later recalls Łukasiewicz answering: “On the contrary, it is the Polish army that will invade Germany from the very first days.” [Q-026]

Wednesday, 16 August. Ribbentrop writes to Moscow: “I am coming.” It is the first time they have put in writing that he will make the journey to sign a treaty with the Soviets. In Berlin, meanwhile, the Germans are beginning to worry about one thing. Those of you who were here last night will forgive me: history sometimes repeats itself. The Germans are asking themselves, what will Britain do?

On the evening of 15 August, British ambassador Sir Nevile Henderson visits State Secretary Ernst von Weizsäcker at the German Foreign Ministry. The Germans’ argument is: surely Britain would not support Poland? Look how mad these Poles are, with their provocations. The German minority in Poland is oppressed; it is all the fault of the unreasonable Poles. Surely the British would not be willing to help madmen?

Henderson answers: “I told Baron von Weizsäcker that the last was a very dangerous theory and sounded like Herr von Ribbentrop who had never been able to understand the British mentality. If the Poles were compelled by any act of Germany to resort to arms to defend themselves there was not a shadow of doubt that we would give them our full armed support. We had made that abundantly clear and Germany would be making a tragic mistake if she imagined the contrary.” [Q-027] [C-028]

Thursday, 17 August. Incidents multiply along the German-Polish frontier: shootings and border crossings. The German press unleashes a campaign—remember Czechoslovakia the year before?—about “Polish terror against the German minority”: arrests, murders. In Moscow, Ribbentrop’s reply arrives: he is willing to come and sign an agreement.

Friday, 18 August. As you know, Mussolini and Ciano are terrified because Italy is not ready for war, but the official line dictated to the Italian press is, obviously, quite different. Italy has signed the Pact of Steel. The Italian press has precise orders: in this crisis, the Germans are right.

So I will read you the front-page display from the afternoon edition of the *Corriere della Sera* of 18 August:

“POLAND AT THE DECISIVE TURNING POINT OF ITS HISTORY / The tragic responsibility of the democracies / which unleashed and sustained Polish arrogance / Unshakable firmness of the Axis against the warmongering cloaked in hypocrisy by the encirclers” [Q-028]

Democracy, of course, is a dirty word in Fascist Italy. The “encirclers” are precisely the “demo-pluto-Jewish-ocracies”: France, Britain, and the Soviet Union, which supposedly besiege the peoples. Well, you know; we know this story in Italy.

That same Friday, 18 August, the Polish government has to decide whether to enter an alliance with the Soviet Union while granting passage to Soviet troops. The Polish government says no: “We have no military agreements with the Soviet Union and do not wish to have any.”

There is also a famous comment which later becomes proverbial, attributed—though not in a secure contemporary transcript—to Poland’s commander-in-chief, Marshal Edward Śmigły-Rydz: “With the Germans we may lose our freedom, with the Russians we shall lose our soul.” [Q-029] The remark is generally placed around 19–20 August rather than in the government’s decision on the eighteenth. And so there is no agreement. [C-027]
