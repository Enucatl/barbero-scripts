## 4. Sudeten crisis and the exclusion of the Soviet Union

<!-- transcript: U-00212–U-00256; omissions: none -->

Hitler summons the leader of the Czechoslovak Nazis. Because in Czechoslovakia too there is a Nazi party among the German minority of the Sudetenland. That is what they are called—I can never remember why—but the Germans of Czechoslovakia are called the Sudeten Germans.

Among the Germans of Czechoslovakia there is a Nazi party which takes orders from Hitler, and in March 1938 Hitler gives the signal: begin the street violence, the agitation, the terrorism. Czechoslovakia begins to sink into chaos. [C-009]

The German press begins an extremely heavy campaign. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are oppressed; they are a discriminated-against minority, an oppressed minority. The criminal Czechoslovak police arrest them, rape them, torture them. The Germans in Czechoslovakia are suffering; this cannot be endured. [Q-010]

The British ambassadors in Berlin and in Prague, on instructions from their government, say: well, after all, the Germans are not entirely in the wrong; one can understand them. They repeat that the German claims are, after all, well founded, and that it is the Czechoslovak government which must try to meet the Germans partway in what they are asking. We certainly do not want to unleash another war, do we? And therefore the Czechoslovak government would do better to make concessions. [C-010]

There is only one country which, at that moment, when the Nazis in Czechoslovakia begin the same game they had played in Austria—there is only one country which then takes a public position and says that all the powers ought to reach an agreement to guarantee Czechoslovakia. It is the Soviet Union.

And obviously nobody listens to it, because the Soviet Union is the country—the Soviet Union is the Bolshevik, communist country, and it is out of the game. The British ambassador in Moscow sends a report to London in those weeks in which he concludes, and I quote: “The Soviet Union must […] for the time being, be counted out of European politics in so far as the exercise of a decisive influence one way or the other is concerned.” [Q-011] Russia can be treated as though it does not exist. [C-011]

Notice once again how difficult it is for them to take account of the way the world is changing. They continue to speak of “European politics” as though politics were still always made by Britain and France. No: the United States is not there, Japan is not there, the Soviet Union is not there—nothing. It is still always European politics, and Russia does not count.

This goes on for months, with Czechoslovakia in chaos and the German press pounding away, pounding away, pounding away at the sufferings of the Sudeten Germans, while Britain and France press the government in Prague, saying: but make some concessions.
