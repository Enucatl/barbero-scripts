## 2. Halifax, anti-Bolshevism, and Hitler’s reading of the climate

<!-- transcript: U-00104–U-00181; omissions: none -->

There is a moment when the fact that they are deluding themselves becomes absolutely clear. So, our story tonight—in the end I decided to begin it on this day: 19 November 1937. Less than two years remain before the outbreak of the Second World War. We know that; they do not.

On 19 November 1937 the British foreign secretary, Lord Halifax, goes to Berlin, meets Hitler, and in Berlin the British minister expresses sympathy for the German claims. Because by then there is a generation in Britain saying: yes, after all, at Versailles we went too far; we imposed unjust things on Germany. After all, one can understand the Germans asking for revisions.

Halifax says to Hitler that Britain understands their claims against Poland, Austria, and Czechoslovakia, and that in time changes are possible, provided they are made peacefully and without causing upheavals. His words are: “I said that there were no doubt other questions arising out of the Versailles settlement which seemed to us capable of causing trouble if they were mishandled, e.g. Danzig, Austria, Czechoslovakia. On all these matters we were not necessarily concerned to stand for the status quo as today, but we were concerned to avoid such treatment of them as would be likely to cause trouble. If reasonable settlements could be reached with the free assent and goodwill of those primarily concerned we certainly had no desire to block.” [Q-003]

Lord Halifax also says something else to Hitler: Germany is Europe’s bulwark against Bolshevism. [Q-004]

Because we must not forget, if we are to understand how they reason in those years, that there is this other factor as well. In Europe, in the East, there is, there is an unmentioned presence which everyone pretends does not exist and speaks of as little as possible: the Soviet Union. There is the Soviet Union, where by 1937 Stalin is in power. We know that Stalin has put world revolution away in a drawer, but they do not know it.

For them, the Soviet Union—for them, for Western politicians—is still the country not even of the communists, but of the Bolsheviks, of revolutionaries who are extremely dangerous. It is a country to be kept at a distance. Therefore the Germany of Herr Hitler, as British diplomats call it in their reports—they always call him that, Herr Hitler—is, all things considered, with its ferocious anti-communism and all those concentration camps into which it has thrown the German communists, well, all things considered, a useful country. [C-004]

And so it is in Britain, and so it is in France. In France, where unlike Britain there is a strong Communist Party and a strong Socialist Party, there is a left-wing government, the Popular Front. We have forgotten it today, but at the time the Popular Front has created—never mind Hollande—enormous hopes and enormous fears in France. And in France there is a ruling class: industrialists, generals, the clergy, who, faced with a communist government, say: well, the Nazis are better, much better; better Hitler than Stalin. “Nothing about the conduct of this fraction of the French bourgeoisie can be understood unless one hears it murmuring under its breath: ‘Hitler rather than Blum.’” [Q-005]

So, so Hitler senses that there is a climate in which certain things can be hoped for. Certain things can be done; one need only wait for the opportunity. There has been much debate over whether Hitler planned everything from the beginning, whether he had precise plans all the way to invading Poland. You know Woody Allen? “I can't listen to that much Wagner, you know? I start to get the urge to conquer Poland.” [Q-006] But the invasion of Poland is only the final act. There has been much debate: had Hitler already decided everything from the beginning? Perhaps not.

In the 1960s a famous book appears by an English historian, A. J. P. Taylor, who says: after all, criminal though Hitler is, in foreign policy he wants normal things. Everyone wants their country to be great and powerful, and Hitler wants that too, and he waits for the right opportunities, sniffing the air. The wind is favorable. “There was here no concrete plan, no directive for German policy in 1937 and 1938. Or if there were a directive, it was to wait upon events. [...] Hitler did not make plans—for world conquest or for anything else. He assumed that others would provide opportunities, and that he would seize them. [...] His foreign policy was a different matter. He aimed to make Germany the dominant Power in Europe and maybe, more remotely, in the world. Other Powers have pursued similar aims, and still do.” [Q-007]

Waiting for opportunities—and also creating the opportunities, however. Taylor does not know much about that; documents which emerge later show it: Hitler creates the opportunities. It begins with Austria. Austria is a German country. [C-005]
