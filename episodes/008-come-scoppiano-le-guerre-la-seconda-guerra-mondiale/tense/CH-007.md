## 7. Danzig, the Polish guarantee, and the failed Soviet opening

<!-- transcript: U-00444–U-00521; omissions: none -->

Ten days later, on 26 March, Hitler receives the Polish government’s formal rejection of the German demand to incorporate the Free City of Danzig into the Reich; German pressure over Danzig had in fact begun months earlier. Hitler speaks to the Reichstag at the end of April 1939. Three months before, in the same place, he had said that German-Polish friendship was one of the reassuring features of European political life. Three months later, he publicly presents his case this way: “This is, as I have emphasized before, perhaps the most difficult of all Germany’s problems. [...] I saw the peaceful resolution of this question as a further contribution to a lasting relaxation of tensions in Europe. [...] The Polish government rejected this offer of mine. [...] I sincerely regretted this attitude of the Polish government, which I could not understand.” The opening sentence concerns the broader territorial settlement and Polish access to the sea, not Danzig alone; this is Hitler’s public justification of German demands, not a neutral account of them. [Q-019] [C-019]

From that moment on, conflict intensifies in Danzig and along the frontier between Danzig and Poland: incidents and shootings. Nazi militias form in Danzig; outside stands the Polish customs post. There are incidents continually. But this time the British and French governments are beginning to think that perhaps something ought to be done. What?

Britain and France propose negotiating an alliance with Poland. Chamberlain even goes so far as to give an official guarantee: although no treaty has yet been signed, Britain—with France taking the same position—will give Poland all the support in its power if action clearly threatens Polish independence and Poland considers it vital to resist. Bear in mind that today, with hindsight, one says: it is about time. What else can they do? [C-020]

At the time, however, it is very difficult to make public opinion swallow this. Danzig? Are we supposed to go to war over Danzig? A famous headline appears in the French newspaper *L’Œuvre*: “Morire per Danzica?”—“Die for Danzig?” No. It does not represent all British and French opinion, but the slogan becomes influential. Nevertheless, the French and British governments, undaunted now that they have discovered Hitler might not be trustworthy, do something else unheard-of in April and May 1939. [Q-020] [C-020]

They begin exploring whether some kind of agreement can be reached with the Soviet Union. Negotiations begin and drag on for many months, to see whether a military-defense agreement can be concluded in the event of war. The story of these negotiations is sad and depressing, because one thinks: if only they had made that agreement. Instead, it appears that no one truly wants it enough; they are all afraid of one another. One apparently objective feature emerges from the telegrams and replies: whenever Britain and France make a proposal to the Russians, the Russians answer the next day. Then weeks pass—weeks and weeks—before the British and French finally take the next step. The Russians answer the next day, and so the negotiations drag on. This is an illustrative pattern rather than an exact rule for every exchange, and mistrust and obstruction exist on all sides. [C-021]

Meanwhile Stalin takes note. They are asking me to negotiate—but do they mean it, or do they want to draw me in and then, at the decisive moment, leave me to deal with Germany alone? Stalin takes note and decides that the risk is too great. He is negotiating a military agreement with Britain and France, but the British and French seem very lukewarm.

Stalin lets the Germans know that although their countries are enemies—one communist, the other, well...—they have commercial interests, and perhaps opening negotiations for a commercial agreement between Germany and the Soviet Union might be a good idea. The Germans are willing. And so a situation develops over the summer in which Stalin, without anyone knowing, is negotiating with Britain and France at one table and with Germany at another, waiting to see what will happen. More precisely, economic contacts revive unevenly and only develop into a political channel later, accelerating decisively in August. [C-021]
