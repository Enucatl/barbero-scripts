## 3. James I, Buckingham, and the Court's Disrepute

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00112–U-00179 -->

The court’s disrepute already begins in the time of his father, James I. Before that
Elizabeth reigns. Elizabeth is extremely popular; there is also great skill in propaganda,
but Elizabeth truly is extremely popular. Elizabeth dies without heirs, and James I, King of
Scotland, ascends the English throne. And already under James I public opinion suddenly begins
instead to distrust the court and the courtiers. The king, let’s put it this way, seems to do everything he can to encourage public opinion to be distrustful. The king
has a prime minister, the Duke of Buckingham, much younger than himself, a favorite, as they say then, but one who in fact governs the kingdom. The Duke of Buckingham is detested because
he sells everything: he sells noble titles, bishoprics—whatever anyone wants, if he pays, the
duke gives it to him. And King James has a sexual relationship with his prime minister, the Duke
of Buckingham. At the time it is gossip; today it is an established fact, because we have their
letters. King James writes to his prime minister, “And so God bless you, my sweet child and wife,
and grant that ye may ever be a comfort to your dear dad and husband …” Now, obviously the public does not know these letters; we know them today. Perhaps we have no reason to be scandalized
by a relationship of that kind, but imagine seventeenth-century public opinion. Everyone knows
there is a favorite governing the kingdom and that the king has a strange relationship with
this favorite, who is so much younger than he is. And gossip spreads across England about court
orgies, about the king and his friends having sexual relations and getting drunk. Then James I
dies and is succeeded by his son Charles, who many years later will end up on the scaffold in
London. Charles is a very different character: an austere, cold figure who gives no grounds for
gossip. But he does not get rid of the Duke of Buckingham, who is his friend and has a certain hold over him too. Buckingham continues to govern England for a few years
until he is murdered, whereupon the country rejoices and his murderer is celebrated in ballads
as a great English hero. In other words, the court’s image is beyond repair.

On top of that,
the king has married a French princess. Queen Henrietta is French in a country, England, that
detests the French; she is Catholic in a country where the majority is fiercely Protestant;
and, much younger than her husband, she loves dancing and theater. The Venetian ambassadors
write that the English court is the most sumptuous and cheerful in the world. In the eyes of
most English people, this is not a positive thing. In the English heartland, living far from
court, the idea takes hold that the king, the court, the royal family, the courtiers, and
the ministers are a foreign body, that the court has nothing to do with the country. A great
historian, Lawrence Stone, expressed this memorably, so I’ll quote him instead of trying to
put it in my own words. “The Country was virtuous, the Court wicked; the Country was thrifty,
the Court extravagant; the Country was honest, the Court corrupt; the Country was chaste and
heterosexual, the Court promiscuous and homosexual; the Country was sober, the Court drunken;
the Country was nationalist, the Court xenophile; … the Country was solidly Protestant,
even Puritan, the Court was deeply tainted by popish leanings.”
