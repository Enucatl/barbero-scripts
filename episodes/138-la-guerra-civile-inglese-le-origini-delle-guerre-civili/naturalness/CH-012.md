## 12. The Long Parliament and Strafford's Fall

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00951–U-01066 -->

The election is dramatic because everyone knows clearly that the point of no return has been
reached and this Parliament must necessarily do great things. The king tries everything to
elect reliable men and fails. The Court party is defeated everywhere. Courtiers and royal
officials are calculated to form only eleven per cent of the Commons, less than in any other
Parliament. The election campaign’s Puritan slogan against royal candidates is: do not elect
these godless courtiers, these papist priests. Poorer voters—craftsmen and shopkeepers who
usually merely make up the numbers and vote as the local gentleman tells them—participate with
unusual commitment. This time radicals seek election on protest programs. As a lord who will
nevertheless side with Parliament says, “... yet I shall not easily suffer myself hereafter
upon the provocations of others to appear in any popular assemblies where fellows without
shirts challenge as good a voice as myself, neither will I for any respect absent myself from
any assembly, how great soever, whither my duty to the service of my King and country doth call
me; where they shall know and (if they desire) shall feel my authority.” Puritan preachers
fan the flames and appeal to the people: “You that are of the meaner rank, common people,
be not discouraged, for God intends to make use of the common people in the great work of
proclaiming the kingdom of his dear Son.”

The air is charged with millenarian religious enthusiasm. Reading the news sheets, papers, and diaries of the days when the members arrive
is striking: it is the dawn of a fine day of long happiness for this kingdom; Parliament will
build a new world.

For the great majority of Commons members, gentlemen and merchants, this
does not mean social revolution. Absolutely not; almost nobody wants that. It means Parliament
must teach the king that Parliament commands, defend private property from royal taxes, and
eliminate the papist innovations beloved by the archbishop.

Parliament opens on 3 November
1640, and this time the king delivers the opening speech. Despite his stammer, he realizes he has to face Parliament himself and personally asks for money for war with Scotland. Parliament answers:
one moment, other matters must first be clarified. First, you cannot impose new taxes without
assembling Parliament. The king cannot manage this Parliament and his ministers cannot defend
him. He acknowledges that he indeed lacks this right.

But matters rapidly go further and assume
the features of a revolution. People say Parliament is Elizabeth’s true heir, as if James and
Charles never existed, and that the House of Commons—not the king—is invested by God. Within
weeks the Commons acts as the kingdom’s government, sending orders to county justices of the
peace. While they’re at it, they abolish press censorship, which already works poorly. Speaker
after speaker attacks the king and his ministers, and insists: what did Strafford mean by
recruiting an army in Ireland? A committee must investigate. In those days the Archbishop of
Canterbury records not a dream but another frightening event. In his study is a large portrait
of himself by Van Dyck. Entering, he finds it on the floor, fallen from the wall. He writes:
I am not superstitious, but God grant it is not an omen.

Meanwhile, remember William Prynne,
the Puritan printer whose ears were cut off, cheeks branded, and who was imprisoned for
life? Naturally he is triumphantly freed, and in his place Strafford is sent to the Tower by
Parliament’s order. A royal secretary’s son finds among the papers the minutes of the meeting
where Strafford spoke of an Irish army and delivers them to the opposition leaders. Ministers and
secretaries begin boarding ships and fleeing abroad. Meanwhile Puritan policy takes over. Catholic
priests formerly left alone are arrested and some hanged, drawn, and quartered, to the immense
satisfaction of the good Puritan people. The Commons passes extremist Puritan religious laws:
no games on Sunday, all holy images removed from churches, no kneeling during worship—those
papist grimaces. They throw the Archbishop of Canterbury into the Tower as well.

In March ’41
Strafford’s trial opens before Parliament. He is accused of many things, especially wanting
to recruit an Irish army to subjugate this kingdom, meaning us free Englishmen. The two-month trial grips the country. Contemporary chronicles say the courtroom is packed, people
camp there with bread, cheese, and beer, and supposedly relieve themselves on the spot rather
than lose their place.

Strafford manages to show that he meant an army to subdue Scotland, not
England. But English law allows Parliament to pass a special act declaring a person a guilty
criminal and condemning him to death without trial. The Commons passes such an act against
Strafford. It must pass the Lords and receive the king’s signature. The king declares he will
not sign. When it reaches the Lords, most wisely stay away. Few are present and a crowd rumbles
outside. The king has said he will not sign anyway, so the Lords wash their hands and pass it
too.

Then it reaches the king. He does not know what to do, consults lawyers and bishops, becomes
frightened, and signs—only to regret it immediately. Too late: two days later Strafford is
beheaded. Laud, imprisoned in the Tower, notes in his famous diary that serving that king has not been worthwhile. Up to here, as we approach the end, it seems like the French Revolution,
where every new royal concession pushes him closer to the abyss.
