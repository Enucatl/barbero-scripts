## 10. The Covenanters and the First Bishops' War

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00762–U-00837 -->

In Scotland the Covenant’s supporters, now universally called the Covenanters, are in power,
and there is talk of expelling everyone who refuses to sign. You see how rapidly one slips into
the extreme interpretation, don’t you? Clergy considered a little too lukewarm are attacked in
the street. And, as happens in such cases, some begin to fear that perhaps we have gone too far,
that all this might also produce results we do not want: revolution, social revolution. One of
the chief Scottish poets of the period, William Drummond, who will side with the king during the
Civil War, expresses this well. Drummond writes: “Whether these great commotions and discords
may not dissolve in bellum servile, and peasants, clowns, farmers, base people, all in arms, may
not swallow the nobles and gentry, invest their possessions, adhere together by a new Covenant,
and follow our example.” Do you see what’s so remarkable about “as we have done”? We—the lords,
masters, rich, nobles, gentlemen, and clergy—made the Covenant, and there is a risk that the
rabble will take it seriously.

On 1 July 1638 King Charles officially declares that rebellion is
under way in Scotland and that he intends to crush it by force. In England this war is extremely
unpopular. Good people would much rather the king to fight Spain. Instead he is on the best
of terms with papist Spain, who knows why, and now wants us to fight our Protestant brothers,
the Scots. People curse the archbishop, the Pope of London. Naturally the Scots print an appeal
to the English people that circulates clandestinely throughout England.

The king refuses to back down,
but it turns out that there is no money for war against Scotland. Someone tells him: if there
is no money, you know what you have to do, don’t you? Summon—no. The king refuses to summon
Parliament; he borrows instead, goes deeply into debt, yet still cannot raise enough money,
and recruits a poor, weak army.

He nevertheless marches north in spring 1639. At the border he
learns that Scotland is entirely in enemy hands: every arsenal, garrison, and fortress, including
Edinburgh Castle, has been occupied by Covenanters. The royal garrisons in Scotland have joined
the rebels. The king remains at the border with his weak, discontented army. Across from him camp
the heavily armed Scots; from their camp come preachers’ sermons and the collective singing of
psalms. The lords with the king begin telling him that he should not invade Scotland and that,
if he insists, he must first summon Parliament.

The king admits that for the moment he is not
strong enough, and makes a truce. He really intends only to gain time, but his advisers insist
that this is too great a matter to undertake without Parliament. The archbishop is among the
first to say so. Another who insists is someone I have not yet mentioned but whom, in this
last half hour—no, God willing, in these final fifteen or twenty minutes—we’ll mention again, because he is the protagonist of a dramatic episode: the Earl of Strafford, governor of
Ireland, one of the king’s chief and most trusted advisers. Both Laud and Strafford insist that
Parliament must be summoned, and the king summons it, after ten—eleven—years without doing so.
