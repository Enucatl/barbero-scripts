## 2. The Illusion of Peace and a Remote Court

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00056–U-00111 -->

And yet, shortly before that war breaks out, England considers itself a country blessed
by peace. Let us turn the clock back a dozen years. In seventeenth-century England a form
of entertainment they call the masque is all the rage. These are costumed performances
combining music, singing, ballet, and scenery; they are also tremendously fashionable at
court. Masques are staged in which the king and queen themselves rehearse their parts for
months, and the content is always allegorical, in keeping with the taste of the time. In 1634,
while England is the only major country outside the Thirty Years’ War, the London lawyers’
corporation stages a masque called *The Triumph of Peace* in honour of the king. [N-003] It
is the most expensive ever seen up to that time, with more than two hundred participants. And
its theme is the triumph of peace. That year Charles I is talking to his German nephews, the
sons of his sister, who married a German prince—his German nephews, who come from a Germany
devastated beyond description by the Thirty Years’ War—and in that year, in that ’37,
Charles I happens to say, lets slip, that he is the happiest king in Christendom. In reality
that happiness is illusory and discontent is brewing in England, but the king has not the slightest suspicion of it. And he had not the slightest suspicion because the lifestyle of a
king of that period separates him completely from his people. Court etiquette seems designed
expressly to isolate the king from the country and make him live in an unreal world. All courts of the period have extremely rigid etiquette that transforms the king’s daily existence into
a kind of spectacle. The English court, though, is perhaps the most formal in Europe. The King
of England is the only king in Europe who is served at table by courtiers, nobles, on their
knees. And ordinary people have no contact with the king. The king naturally always lives in
public, always under somebody’s eyes, but those people are the courtiers. Ordinary people
never see him, never meet him, except on two occasions, twice a year. There: twice a year
the King of England, like the King of France, touches sufferers from scrofula, because for
centuries in those two kingdoms there has been a tradition, a popular belief, that scrofula
is a special disease and that kings possess a magical power and can cure those who suffer from
it. Do not be surprised that such things exist in the seventeenth century: seventeenth-century
society and mentality are not so very different from those of three centuries earlier. Kings
touch the sick and the sick come to be touched. But to be admitted to the king’s touch, to
hope to recover from this illness that everyone calls the king’s evil, the sick must have a
certificate bearing the three signatures of a parish priest, an officer of their parish, and a
justice of the peace. Charles has no idea what the country’s true feelings are towards him,
his government, and his court. And I dwell on the court because it is not so much the king’s person at first, but the environment surrounding him—the court—that becomes the focus of
the population’s, the country’s, annoyance and hostility.
