## 4. Anglican Authority and Puritan Dissent

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00180–U-00246 -->

Let us pause over this last aspect, which is crucial. Why? Because religion is still
fundamental in this society which, I repeat, has not truly emerged from the Middle Ages. One
of the characteristics of our medieval past is precisely the pervasiveness of religion, the
importance religion has for people. We have said that the country is Puritan. What does that
mean? It means that officially—officially—in England after the Protestant Reformation
there is a single church, the Anglican Church, headed by the king. Mind you, this religious
aspect is enormously important. That is to say, we are already entering into the question of
the causes of the Civil War, in which political factors too—and we shall see this—will
play a gigantic role, but the religious dimension is fundamental. Now, the Anglican Church
is a Protestant church inasmuch as it rejects the authority of the Pope; indeed, it abhors
what they call popery. But in the way it actually operates, the Anglican Church of the period
greatly resembles the Catholic Church. It is an authoritarian church with its hierarchy of
parish priests and bishops; it is a church that expects the faithful to allow themselves to be
guided like a flock, without questioning the teachings that come from the pulpit. Except that
this is seventeenth-century England, which, although still a medieval country, is on its way
to becoming the most modern country in Europe. And, for example, it is a country where more and
more people know how to read and write. Knowing how to read means being exposed to circulating
propaganda and news sheets. A people among whom many, including the poor, can read and write;
where religious feeling is deeply rooted; and where a state church with a very authoritarian
outlook rules—you understand that this is a recipe for the birth of dissent. And England is
full of dissident movements, religious dissidents. Sects, as the authorities call them. They
are the Puritans. They begin as unauthorized reading groups, lay people who gather to read the
Bible together because they can read and have no need of the parish priest; gradually they
end up rejecting the official church. They are strict Protestants, even fanatics; they hate
all those Catholic elements that have remained in Anglican ritual—the crucifixes, the holy
images: idolatry and superstition to the Puritans. The Puritans favour personal reading of the
Bible; they would like pastors to be elected by the faithful, with no bishops to command them,
or even lay people themselves to preach to the others. Now, I would say that if we stopped here
we might even say, well, the Puritans are rather likeable. They have a decidedly democratic,
anti-authoritarian approach. Naturally, we must give the complete picture. The Puritans are
religious fundamentalists; they dress only in black; they would like to ban every sport, every
entertainment, dancing, and close the theatres in the England where Shakespeare had died only a
few years before. The Puritans consider themselves the chosen people, the new Israel, and expect
God to exterminate their opponents and lead them into a new Jerusalem. And perhaps we would like
that less if we had to meet them. But Puritanism nevertheless really does mean calling into
question the obligation to obey the authorities. As one of the great thinkers of the English
seventeenth century, Hobbes—Thomas Hobbes, who has no sympathy at all for any of this—says,
and I quote Hobbes: “For after the Bible was translated into English, every man, nay, every
boy and wench, that could read English, thought they spoke with God Almighty, and understood
what he said, when by a certain number of chapters a day they had read the Scriptures once or
twice over.”. Hobbes, of course, will side with the king during the Civil War and will be
forced to flee abroad.
