## 5. Archbishop Laud's Campaign for Conformity

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00247–U-00341 -->

Because in fact the point is that King Charles’s policy brings him into direct conflict with
the Puritans, with religious dissent. Why? Because he is the head of the Church of England and
insists that all his subjects belong to the Church of England and obey the Anglican bishops. Let
us pay attention to this matter of the bishops, because it is particularly significant. In the
Protestant world, the idea of a hierarchical church, with a head and then bishops, has almost
always been called into question. In England everyone knows that if the head of the church is the
king, it means that he chooses the bishops, and that the bishops have no autonomy at all: they
put on great airs and want to give orders, but in reality they are in the king’s service. It
is an old story. Already in Elizabeth’s time there is evidence that the weak point of the
Anglican Church is precisely the fact that the bishops cannot make the people respect them. As
an archbishop writes in Elizabeth’s time, “The messengers of Christ … are esteemed as
the filth of the world.”. But Charles is determined to put an end to all this and restore
the authority of the Anglican episcopate, and the man to whom he entrusts this task is the
Archbishop of Canterbury, William Laud, who is a crucial figure in our story. Archbishop William
Laud is the man who persuades the king—and he has no difficulty persuading him—that to save
throne and altar together, religious dissent must be crushed. The archbishop is a man of modest
origins, the son of a merchant, very learned, and according to contemporary observers not very
well mannered. They describe him as a small, ruddy man, irritable and intolerant. In reality,
Archbishop William Laud must have a few doubts in his unconscious, but he is unwilling to
admit them even to himself—and at that time they know less about the unconscious than we think
we know. The fact remains that the archbishop keeps a diary in which he notes the dreams he has,
especially when he has disturbing dreams. Once he writes: “I dreamed, that K. C. was to be
Married to a Minister’s Widow. And that I was called upon to do it. No Service-Book could
be found; and in my own Book, which I had, I could not find the Order for Marriage.”. Then
the archbishop wakes up wondering what message God might have sent him through this dream. We,
having read Dr Freud, are tempted to say that the archbishop knows there is something not quite
right about the king’s wife being a foreign, Catholic princess. And yet by now the king can
no longer marry the daughter of a good Protestant; he already has his Catholic wife. The fact
remains that if these doubts come to the archbishop in dreams, by day he forgets them. And by day
he proceeds relentlessly with his programme. And so, the Puritans want to destroy holy
images and even crucifixes, altars. The archbishop imposes stained glass, organs, surpliced
clergy, the obligation to kneel for communion, candles, holy images, the priest bowing to the
altar, the sign of the cross. In the Puritans’ judgement, monkey grimaces. And obviously the
archbishop’s programme calls for the elimination of the dissident sects. Discipline must be
restored. The Puritans read the Bible and then start preaching? And they are good at giving
sermons, at preaching, but the archbishop decides that far too many sermons are already heard
in this country. The people do not need sermons; they need catechism. They must learn by heart
what they need to know and concern themselves with nothing else. And when a parish priest,
or even a bishop, does not quite agree—because the suspicion that all this means returning
to Catholicism is widespread in this country, which has learned to have a damned fear of
papists—well, when a bishop or parish priest does not agree, he is replaced, dismissed,
perhaps even put in prison. The Puritans who feel most endangered flee the country. Do you all
know where they go? They go to America. Do you remember one of the founding legends of American
history? The Pilgrim Fathers who arrive on the *Mayflower* and found the Puritan colonies of
New England. And when do they go there? They go in the years of Charles and Archbishop Laud. So
many of these Puritans go to America that instead of being pleased, the king worries that the
country risks losing subjects. For a moment Charles thinks of banning emigration to America. Then
he does not do so.
