<!-- tense-reviewed: CH-011 --> ## 11. The Short Parliament and the Scottish Invasion

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00838–U-00950 -->

Parliament meets on 13 April 1640. The king attends the opening but does not deliver the speech,
partly because he has a slight stammer. Once, speaking to someone with the same defect, Charles
had said: I tried holding pebbles in my mouth, but it is no use. So the chancellor speaks
for him, saying: “My lords, and you, the knights, citizens, and burgesses of the House of
Commons: I doubt not but you rejoice at this day’s meeting ...; and good reason you have so
to do, and with all humbleness of heart to acknowledge the great goodness of his majesty who,
sequestering the memory of all former discouragements in preceding assemblies, is now, out of
a fatherly affection to his people and a confidence that they will not be failing in their
duty to him, pleased graciously to invite you and all his loving subjects to a sacred unity
of hearts and affection in the service of him and of the commonwealth.”. But Parliament
is flooded with petitions. This is the political mechanism: county members arrive in London
bringing letters received from neighbours, communities, rich men, and nobles, all protesting
against something—Ship Money, monopolies, the king’s religious policy. The Commons begins
appointing committees, once again a laboratory of modernity. It says: we must examine these
petitions and protests, and if the king wants money he must abolish Ship Money—indeed no,
that is not enough—he must redress every grievance and hear every protest. You can imagine
Charles’s reaction. On 5 May the king dissolves Parliament, which goes down in history as the
Short Parliament: two or three weeks. It is the fourth time in his reign that Charles dissolves
Parliament and the last time he succeeds. The Council immediately meets to decide how to fund this
damned Scottish war. Lord Strafford says that perhaps an army could be recruited in Ireland and
brought here to reduce this kingdom to obedience. At the time none of the listening ministers
pays much attention. Even today we do not know whether by “this kingdom” Strafford meant
Scotland, which must be reduced to obedience, or had something else in mind; we shall return to
it. Without Parliament there is no money. Charles is in such difficulty that he even contacts the
Pope to ask for a loan from Italy. He confiscates deposits London merchants keep in the Royal
Mint; you can imagine how popular that makes him. The City has had enough. When the king asks
the City merchants for loans, they refuse. The king—the procedures are these—has the London
aldermen arrested and threatens to hang them unless the money appears. But it does not appear,
and recruitment goes terribly. The wildest rumours spread: the recruits will supposedly be put
aboard ships on the pretext of landing in Scotland, but actually sold as slaves in America. The
result? The king cannot assemble a real army. Meanwhile the Scots do, and on 20 August 1640 they
cross the Tweed and invade England to bagpipes and psalm-singing. The king receives yet another
petition, this time signed by many lords asking for pardons for those imprisoned for refusing new
taxes or the archbishop’s reforms and, most dramatically, saying: we hear Strafford proposed
bringing an army from Ireland; we beg Your Majesty not to do it. Bear in mind what Ireland means
to the English then: a savage country we conquered and are filling with Protestant immigrants,
whose local people are savages and papists, ferocious cut-throats thirsting for our blood. Somehow
the rumour spreads that Strafford suggested recruiting an army among these papist savages to
tame this kingdom. What exactly did he mean? And the king—I must say, there begins one of
the most dishonourable pages from the king’s point of view, showing him terribly inadequate
and uncertain. Seeing that public opinion regards Strafford, one of his most trusted advisers,
as his evil genius who gives him bad advice, Charles treats him coldly and suggests he leave
the country, saying he is not sure he can guarantee Strafford’s physical safety. Strafford
decides to remain. The king then gives his word that whatever happens, Strafford need not
fear for his life. Remember that. Meanwhile the Protestant population of the Scottish-occupied
north sympathizes with the Scots and regards them as liberators. Penniless and without an army,
the king must sign a dishonourable agreement accepting Scottish occupation of northern England
and promising to pay the occupiers’ expenses. The only thing left is to summon a Parliament,
and he summons what will naturally go down in history as the Long Parliament.
