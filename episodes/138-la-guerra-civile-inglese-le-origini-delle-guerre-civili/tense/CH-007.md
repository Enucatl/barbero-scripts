seventeenth-century kings find it increasingly tiresome to submit to this obligation. After
all, is it not God who places them on the throne? And therefore they should answer only to
God. Charles grows up breathing this atmosphere. His father James is someone who said that he
wrote: “Kings are called Gods by the propheticall King David, because they sit upon God his
Throne in the earth, and have the count of their administration to give unto him.”. James had
written a handbook to instruct his son Charles in his future profession as king, in which he
taught him that the king must be a father to his people, but like every father has the right
of life and death and the right to absolute obedience. And James taught his son that the king
is above the law. He is not limited by any law, because it is he who makes the laws and, if
he wishes, abolishes them. Charles devotes his life to putting these principles into effect,
which in practical terms means trying to govern without summoning Parliament. Other European
kings do it too and eventually succeed. In England it is a particularly difficult undertaking,
because England is the country where Parliament has gained most ground and where already in the
Middle Ages kings have to swear not to govern without the country’s consent. You know Magna
Carta. Magna Carta reinforces the principle that the king is constrained by law and that certain
taxation requires common counsel, although Parliament develops only later. [N-005] Parliaments in
England are—this is good, you can all picture it—the House of Lords, where the great lords
and bishops sit, and they generally go where the king wishes. But the truly important house is
the House of Commons, representing the rural aristocracy, the gentry, as the English call them,
which is the social class governing the kingdom, because no state apparatus exists. The king can
govern only insofar as the country gentlemen keep order, act as judges, and collect taxes. And
then, besides the gentry, there are precisely those who have the money, those who have cash:
the merchants of the towns, the City of London. This is the House of Commons. And every time
Charles summons Parliament in the early years of his reign, he discovers that whatever he asks
of the Commons, someone disagrees, and somebody always gets up to speak against his demands
and question his policy. Parliaments are summoned, remain in session for a while, then go home;
perhaps for a few years there is no need for one, and when there is, another is summoned. The
king summons a new Parliament and finds the same faces as last time, because in the country,
in the counties, there are men who have by now gained experience and get themselves elected and
go to Parliament expressly to tell the king that the country disagrees with his policy. In other
words, I do not know whether you understand: in this country, which in its own way is a laboratory
of modernity, the concept of opposition is being born. In theory Parliament is not expected to
oppose the king’s demands on principle; it discusses them and then, broadly speaking, tries
to accommodate him. In the English Parliament, instead, there is an opposition: a substantial
group in the Commons that on principle disagrees with what the king wants. Every time Charles
summons Parliament, he finds it increasingly difficult to obtain what he wants, and in the end he
grows tired of it. In 1629 the king declares that since Parliament opposes his policy, he will
summon it no more. Many members of Parliament protest and end up in prison. From that moment,
for ten years, Charles governs without summoning Parliament again. And he can delude himself,
can tell himself that he has become an absolute sovereign, free of all restraints. The problem,
the problem is that his ministers are not at all certain that they can really impose new taxes
without summoning Parliament and without the country rebelling; they do not dare to do it. But
money is needed, and therefore the king is forced to try to make money by other means. Where do
you look for money? Where is it? In the economy, in trade. Making money means: we cannot impose
new taxes, but we can say, for example, that all the rag merchants in London must enrol in a
corporation and pay a licence to practise their trade; otherwise they cannot do so. And this is
done, and a whole series of controls, obligations, in effect taxes on commercial activity, are
introduced. They are introduced, and naturally the purpose is to take money, and then to monitor
people and catch the violators and fine them. Monopolies are granted, as I was telling you. I
shall give you an example. I hesitated for a long time over whether to give this example because,
as I told you, tonight’s lecture will be rather long. Then I decided to give it anyway, because
one does not imagine that such things could happen in a seventeenth-century kingdom. So, the king
grants an entrepreneur a monopoly over the manufacture of soap. The entrepreneur obviously pays
a great deal of money to be the only person in England allowed to produce soap. All the other
factories close, naturally, immediately creating a whole world of discontented people. The
monopolist makes soap, and since he is the only one who makes it, he need not worry too much
about quality. After a while protest spreads throughout the kingdom because the king’s soap is
of very poor quality. The government decides to prove to the country that this is not true. You
say it is poor quality? Let us see. Public tests are organized and washerwomen summoned, but the
tests are rigged to show that the king’s soap works extremely well. Contraband soap begins to
be produced, and throughout the country people secretly buy soap because it has been produced
without the king’s authorization. Then the king begins sending spies around to see whether we
can catch those secretly making soap. And among the craftsmen, the manufacturers, people begin to
say: the king’s spies are around, watch out. This atmosphere is created in which whatever one
does, there is always the impression that someone behind you is spying and will go and report
to the king. You can imagine that the king has really brought it upon himself with these petty
devices he has invented to make money. I shall quote from a text from the very period in which
the king was executed. “How many poor apple-women and broom-men, rag-merchants and people
of all sorts, sold and pawned their bedding and their clothes to buy themselves the freedom of
the new royal incorporation of the suburbs of London? And when all was done, it proved a cheat;
thus was the king’s coffers filled with oppression.”. The king does not declare it, but it
is—or everyone knows—that he does this to avoid having to summon Parliament. And then he
attempts the decisive experiment. Until now these have been petty devices, but now let us try
to introduce an actual new tax and see whether the country pays. He does it very skilfully. In
Elizabeth’s time there was a tax paid by coastal areas to maintain the war fleet.
