## 2. The Illusion of Peace and a Remote Court

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00056–U-00111 -->

And yet, shortly before that war broke out, England considered itself a country blessed by peace. Let us turn the clock back a dozen
years. In seventeenth-century England a form of entertainment they called the masque was all the rage. These were costumed performances
combining music, singing, ballet, and scenery; they were also tremendously fashionable at court. Masques were staged in which the
king and queen themselves rehearsed their parts for months, and the content was always allegorical, in keeping with the taste of
the time. In 1634, while England is the only major country outside the Thirty Years’ War, the London lawyers’ corporation stages
a masque called *The Triumph of Peace* in honour of the king. [N-003] It is
the most expensive ever seen up to that time, with more than two hundred participants. And its theme is the triumph of peace. That
year Charles I is talking to his German nephews, the sons of his sister, who married a German prince—his German nephews, who come
from a Germany devastated beyond description by the Thirty Years’ War—and in that year, in that ’37, Charles I happens to say,
lets slip, that he is the happiest king in Christendom. In reality that happiness was illusory and discontent was brewing in England,
but the king had not the slightest suspicion of it. And he had not the slightest suspicion because the lifestyle of a king of that
period separated him completely from his people. Court etiquette seemed designed expressly to isolate the king from the country
and make him live in an unreal world. All courts of the period had extremely rigid etiquette that transformed the king’s daily
existence into a kind of spectacle. The English court, though, is perhaps the most formal in Europe. The King of England is the only
king in Europe who is served at table by courtiers, nobles, on their knees. And ordinary people have no contact with the king. The
king naturally always lives in public, always under somebody’s eyes, but those people are the courtiers. Ordinary people never
see him, never meet him, except on two occasions, twice a year. There: twice a year the King of England, like the King of France,
touches sufferers from scrofula, because for centuries in those two kingdoms there has been a tradition, a popular belief, that
scrofula is a special disease and that kings possess a magical power and can cure those who suffer from it. Do not be surprised
that such things exist in the seventeenth century: seventeenth-century society and mentality are not so very different from those
of three centuries earlier. Kings touch the sick and the sick come to be touched. But to be admitted to the king’s touch, to hope
to recover from this illness that everyone calls the king’s evil, the sick must have a certificate bearing the three signatures
of a parish priest, an officer of their parish, and a justice of the peace. Charles has no idea what the country’s true feelings
are towards him, his government, and his court. And I dwell on the court because it was not so much the king’s person at first,
but the environment surrounding him—the court—that became the focus of the population’s, the country’s, annoyance and hostility.

## 3. James I, Buckingham, and the Court's Disrepute

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00112–U-00179 -->

The court’s disrepute had already begun in the time of his father, James I. Before that Elizabeth had reigned. Elizabeth was extremely
popular; there was also great skill in propaganda, but Elizabeth truly was extremely popular. Elizabeth dies without heirs, and James I,
King of Scotland, ascends the English throne. And already under James I public opinion suddenly begins instead to distrust the court
and the courtiers. It has to be said that the king, how shall I put it, seems to do everything he can to encourage public opinion to
be distrustful. The king has a prime minister, the Duke of Buckingham, much younger than himself, a favourite, as they said then,
but one who in fact governs the kingdom. The Duke of Buckingham is detested because he sells everything: he sells noble titles,
bishoprics—whatever anyone wants, if he pays, the duke gives it to him. And King James has a sexual relationship with his prime
minister, the Duke of Buckingham. At the time it was gossip; today it is an established fact, because we have their letters. King
James wrote to his prime minister, “And so God bless you, my sweet child and wife, and grant that ye may ever be a comfort to your dear dad and husband …”. Now, obviously the public did not know these letters; we know them today. Perhaps we have no reason to be scandalized
by a relationship of that kind, but imagine seventeenth-century public opinion. Everyone knows there is a favourite governing the
kingdom and that the king has a strange relationship with this favourite, who is so much younger than he is. And gossip spreads
across England about court orgies, about the king and his friends having sexual relations and getting drunk. Then James I dies and
is succeeded by his son Charles, who many years later will end up on the scaffold in London. Charles is a very different character:
an austere, cold figure who gives no grounds for gossip. But he does not get rid of the Duke of Buckingham, with whom he is actually
friends and by whom he too is somewhat dominated. Buckingham continues to govern England for a few years until he is murdered,
whereupon the country rejoices and his murderer is celebrated in ballads as a great English hero. In other words, the court’s
image is beyond repair. On top of that, the king has married a French princess. Queen Henrietta is French in a country, England, that
detests the French; she is Catholic in a country where the majority is fiercely Protestant; and, much younger than her husband, she
loves dancing and theatre. The Venetian ambassadors write that the English court is the most sumptuous and cheerful in the world. In
the eyes of most English people, this is not a positive thing. In the English heartland, living far from court, the idea takes hold
that the king, the court, the royal family, the courtiers, and the ministers are a foreign body, that the court has nothing to do with
the country. A great historian, Lawrence Stone, expressed this memorably, so I shall quote him instead of trying to put it in my own
words. “The Country was virtuous, the Court wicked; the Country was thrifty, the Court extravagant; the Country was honest, the Court corrupt; the Country was chaste and heterosexual, the Court promiscuous and homosexual; the Country was sober, the Court drunken; the Country was nationalist, the Court xenophile; … the Country was solidly Protestant, even Puritan, the Court was deeply tainted by popish leanings.”

## 4. Anglican Authority and Puritan Dissent

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00180–U-00246 -->

Let us pause over this last aspect, which is crucial. Why? Because religion is still fundamental in this society which, I repeat,
has not truly emerged from the Middle Ages. One of the characteristics of our medieval past is precisely the pervasiveness of
religion, the importance religion has for people. We have said that the country was Puritan. What does that mean? It means that
officially—officially—in England after the Protestant Reformation there was a single church, the Anglican Church, headed by
the king. Mind you, this religious aspect is enormously important. That is to say, we are already entering into the question of
the causes of the Civil War, in which political factors too—and we shall see this—will play a gigantic role, but the religious
dimension is fundamental. Now, the Anglican Church is a Protestant church inasmuch as it rejects the authority of the Pope; indeed,
it abhors what they call popery. But in the way it actually operates, the Anglican Church of the period greatly resembles the
Catholic Church. It is an authoritarian church with its hierarchy of parish priests and bishops; it is a church that expects the
faithful to allow themselves to be guided like a flock, without questioning the teachings that come from the pulpit. Except that
this is seventeenth-century England, which, although still a medieval country, is on its way to becoming the most modern country
in Europe. And, for example, it is a country where more and more people know how to read and write. Knowing how to read means being
exposed to circulating propaganda and news sheets. A people among whom many, including the poor, can read and write; where religious
feeling is deeply rooted; and where a state church with a very authoritarian outlook rules—you understand that this is a recipe for
the birth of dissent. And England is full of dissident movements, religious dissidents. Sects, as the authorities call them. They
are the Puritans. They begin as unauthorized reading groups, lay people who gather to read the Bible together because they can
read and have no need of the parish priest; gradually they end up rejecting the official church. They are strict Protestants, even
fanatics; they hate all those Catholic elements that have remained in Anglican ritual—the crucifixes, the holy images: idolatry and
superstition to the Puritans. The Puritans favour personal reading of the Bible; they would like pastors to be elected by the faithful,
with no bishops to command them, or even lay people themselves to preach to the others. Now, I would say that if we stopped here we
might even say, well, the Puritans are rather likeable. They have a decidedly democratic, anti-authoritarian approach. Naturally, we
must give the complete picture. The Puritans are religious fundamentalists; they dress only in black; they would like to ban every
sport, every entertainment, dancing, and close the theatres in the England where Shakespeare had died only a few years before. The
Puritans consider themselves the chosen people, the new Israel, and expect God to exterminate their opponents and lead them into a
new Jerusalem. And perhaps we would like that less if we had to meet them. But Puritanism nevertheless really does mean calling into
question the obligation to obey the authorities. As one of the great thinkers of the English seventeenth century, Hobbes—Thomas
Hobbes, who had no sympathy at all for any of this—said, and I quote Hobbes: “For after the Bible was translated into English, every man, nay, every boy and wench, that could read English, thought they spoke with God Almighty, and understood what he said, when by a certain number of chapters a day they had read the Scriptures once or twice over.”. Hobbes, of course, will side with the king during the Civil War and will be forced to flee abroad.

## 5. Archbishop Laud's Campaign for Conformity

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00247–U-00341 -->

Because in fact the point is that King Charles’s policy brings him into direct conflict with the Puritans, with religious
dissent. Why? Because he is the head of the Church of England and insists that all his subjects belong to the Church of England
and obey the Anglican bishops. Let us pay attention to this matter of the bishops, because it is particularly significant. In the
Protestant world, the idea of a hierarchical church, with a head and then bishops, has almost always been called into question. In
England everyone knows that if the head of the church is the king, it means that he chooses the bishops, and that the bishops have
no autonomy at all: they put on great airs and want to give orders, but in reality they are in the king’s service. It is an old
story. Already in Elizabeth’s time there is evidence that the weak point of the Anglican Church is precisely the fact that the
bishops cannot make the people respect them. As an archbishop wrote in Elizabeth’s time, “The messengers of Christ … are esteemed as the filth of the world.”. But Charles is determined to put an end to all this and restore the
authority of the Anglican episcopate, and the man to whom he entrusts this task is the Archbishop of Canterbury, William Laud, who
is a crucial figure in our story. Archbishop William Laud is the man who persuades the king—and he has no difficulty persuading
him—that to save throne and altar together, religious dissent must be crushed. The archbishop was a man of modest origins, the son
of a merchant, very learned, and according to contemporary observers not very well mannered. They describe him as a small, ruddy man,
irritable and intolerant. In reality, Archbishop William Laud must have had a few doubts in his unconscious, but he was unwilling
to admit them even to himself—and at that time they knew less about the unconscious than we think we know. The fact remains that
the archbishop kept a diary in which he noted the dreams he had, especially when he had disturbing dreams. Once he wrote: “I dreamed, that K. C. was to be Married to a Minister’s Widow. And that I was called upon to do it. No Service-Book could be found; and in my own Book, which I had, I could not find the Order for Marriage.”. Then the archbishop wakes up wondering what message God might have sent him through
this dream. We, having read Dr Freud, are tempted to say that the archbishop knew there was something not quite right about the
king’s wife being a foreign, Catholic princess. And yet by now the king can no longer marry the daughter of a good Protestant; he
already has his Catholic wife. The fact remains that if these doubts came to the archbishop in dreams, by day he forgot them. And
by day he proceeded relentlessly with his programme. And so, the Puritans would like to destroy holy images and even crucifixes,
altars. The archbishop imposes stained glass, organs, surpliced clergy, the obligation to kneel for communion, candles, holy images,
the priest bowing to the altar, the sign of the cross. In the Puritans’ judgement, monkey grimaces. And obviously the archbishop’s
programme calls for the elimination of the dissident sects. Discipline must be restored. The Puritans read the Bible and then start
preaching? And they are good at giving sermons, at preaching, but the archbishop decides that far too many sermons are already heard
in this country. The people do not need sermons; they need catechism. They must learn by heart what they need to know and concern
themselves with nothing else. And when a parish priest, or even a bishop, does not quite agree—because the suspicion that all this
means returning to Catholicism is widespread in this country, which has learned to have a damned fear of papists—well, when a bishop
or parish priest does not agree, he is replaced, dismissed, perhaps even put in prison. The Puritans who feel most endangered flee
the country. Do you all know where they go? They go to America. Do you remember one of the founding legends of American history? The
Pilgrim Fathers who arrive on the *Mayflower* and found the Puritan colonies of New England. And when do they go there? They go in
the years of Charles and Archbishop Laud. So many of these Puritans go to America that instead of being pleased, the king worries
that the country risks losing subjects. For a moment Charles thinks of banning emigration to America. Then he does not do so.

## 6. Print, Persecution, and Fear of Popery

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00342–U-00457 -->

The fact remains that for every Puritan who leaves, there are ten operating clandestinely in the country. Now, now, up to this
point, once again, Middle Ages and modernity. There is nothing different from what already happened in the Middle Ages, when
Christian society was full of rebels, dissidents, people who did not believe. But now, in the seventeenth century, there is an
enormous difference. There is printing. And printing means that innumerable treatises, sermons, prayers, orations, homilies, and
pamphlets circulate throughout the kingdom in defence of the good Protestants persecuted by the king and his archbishop, the friends
of papists. In theory there is a law controlling the press: nothing may be published without authorization. But the state apparatus
at that time is too weak to enforce it. Everyone prints whatever they like, clandestinely. The archbishop, yes, when he manages to
lay hands on somebody who has written and published something wrong, has to content himself with making the occasional example. I
shall give you one. There is a Puritan called William Prynne; he is a one-hundred-percent Puritan who criticizes men for wearing
their hair long, the fashion among young court nobles at the time, and criticizes women for wearing their hair short—how dare they
confuse themselves with men. William Prynne is one of those who would like to abolish not only the theatre but even the celebration
of Christmas. He is someone who maintains that drinking a toast to someone’s health is already a diabolical thing, a sin. William
Prynne publishes a little book in which he criticizes the theatre and actresses in particular, because in Shakespeare’s time women
were not seen on stage—the female parts were played by boys—but by now this taboo too has fallen. Women go on stage; in William
Prynne’s eyes it is something—it is proof of the moral abyss into which England is sinking. So in this book he condemns actresses
in particular. Unfortunately, the queen at court has just taken part in a performance, acting in person before the courtiers. The
result? William Prynne’s book is regarded as an outrage against the court, so Prynne is seized, sentenced to the pillory, to
have his ears cut off, and to life imprisonment. It is 1634. Then, imprisoned in the Tower of London, Prynne manages to continue
writing and publishing. He writes another pamphlet protesting that a kind of Catholic Inquisition has now arisen in England and is
hunting down true Protestants; he manages to have it published, and they discover this in 1637, the year in which he had said that
he was the happiest king in Christendom. <!-- omitted: confirmed source-audio duplication --> <!-- omitted: confirmed source-audio

## 7. Parliament, Royal Authority, and Fiscal Expedients

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00458–U-00600 -->

the conflict with Parliament. What is Parliament? From your school studies, you all remember that at a certain point people
begin to speak of absolute monarchy, absolute sovereigns. They begin to speak of it in the sixteenth century and especially the
seventeenth—the Sun King. When people spoke about the Middle Ages, you never heard of absolute sovereigns. Because a medieval king
had a strong limit on his power. He was not above the law and was unable to introduce new taxes without securing the consent of the
country, without consulting the country. How did a medieval king consult the country when he needed money? He summoned an assembly
attended by the nobles and bishops—the nobility, the clergy—and then by the merchants from the towns, who were the people
with the money; in France, at a certain point, they begin to be called the Third Estate. These assemblies, which had a thousand
names in the various countries, but in England were already called Parliament, were summoned and had to agree if the king asked for
money. But seventeenth-century kings find it increasingly tiresome to submit to this obligation. After all, was it not God who placed
them on the throne? And therefore they ought to answer only to God. Charles grew up breathing this atmosphere. His father James was
someone who said that he wrote: “Kings are called Gods by the propheticall King David, because they sit upon God his Throne in the earth, and have the count of their administration to give unto him.”. James had written a handbook to instruct
his son Charles in his future profession as king, in which he taught him that the king must be a father to his people, but like
every father has the right of life and death and the right to absolute obedience. And James taught his son that the king is above
the law. He is not limited by any law, because it is he who makes the laws and, if he wishes, abolishes them. Charles devotes his
life to putting these principles into effect, which in practical terms means trying to govern without summoning Parliament. Other
European kings do it too and eventually succeed. In England it is a particularly difficult undertaking, because England is the
country where Parliament has gained most ground and where already in the Middle Ages kings had to swear not to govern without the
country’s consent. You know Magna Carta. Magna Carta reinforces the principle that the king is constrained by law and that certain
taxation requires common counsel, although Parliament develops only later. [N-005] Parliaments in England are—this is good, you can all picture it—the House of Lords, where
the great lords and bishops sit, and they generally go where the king wishes. But the truly important house is the House of Commons,
representing the rural aristocracy, the gentry, as the English call them, which is the social class governing the kingdom, because no
state apparatus exists. The king can govern only insofar as the country gentlemen keep order, act as judges, and collect taxes. And
then, besides the gentry, there are precisely those who have the money, those who have cash: the merchants of the towns, the City
of London. This is the House of Commons. And every time Charles summons Parliament in the early years of his reign, he discovers
that whatever he asks of the Commons, someone disagrees, and somebody always gets up to speak against his demands and question his
policy. Parliaments are summoned, remain in session for a while, then go home; perhaps for a few years there is no need for one, and
when there is, another is summoned. The king summons a new Parliament and finds the same faces as last time, because in the country,
in the counties, there are men who have by now gained experience and get themselves elected and go to Parliament expressly to tell
the king that the country disagrees with his policy. In other words, I do not know whether you understand: in this country, which in
its own way is a laboratory of modernity, the concept of opposition is being born. In theory Parliament is not expected to oppose the
king’s demands on principle; it discusses them and then, broadly speaking, tries to accommodate him. In the English Parliament,
instead, there is an opposition: a substantial group in the Commons that on principle disagrees with what the king wants. Every
time Charles summons Parliament, he finds it increasingly difficult to obtain what he wants, and in the end he grows tired of it. In
1629 the king declares that since Parliament opposes his policy, he will summon it no more. Many members of Parliament protest and
end up in prison. From that moment, for ten years, Charles governs without summoning Parliament again. And he can delude himself,
can tell himself that he has become an absolute sovereign, free of all restraints. The problem, the problem is that his ministers
are not at all certain that they can really impose new taxes without summoning Parliament and without the country rebelling; they do
not dare to do it. But money is needed, and therefore the king is forced to try to make money by other means. Where do you look for
money? Where is it? In the economy, in trade. Making money means: we cannot impose new taxes, but we can say, for example, that all
the rag merchants in London must enrol in a corporation and pay a licence to practise their trade; otherwise they cannot do so. And
this is done, and a whole series of controls, obligations, in effect taxes on commercial activity, are introduced. They are introduced,
and naturally the purpose is to take money, and then to monitor people and catch the violators and fine them. Monopolies are granted,
as I was telling you. I shall give you an example. I hesitated for a long time over whether to give this example because, as I told
you, tonight’s lecture will be rather long. Then I decided to give it anyway, because one does not imagine that such things
could happen in a seventeenth-century kingdom. So, the king grants an entrepreneur a monopoly over the manufacture of soap. The
entrepreneur obviously pays a great deal of money to be the only person in England allowed to produce soap. All the other factories
close, naturally, immediately creating a whole world of discontented people. The monopolist makes soap, and since he is the only one
who makes it, he need not worry too much about quality. After a while protest spreads throughout the kingdom because the king’s
soap is of very poor quality. The government decides to prove to the country that this is not true. You say it is poor quality? Let
us see. Public tests are organized and washerwomen summoned, but the tests are rigged to show that the king’s soap works extremely
well. Contraband soap begins to be produced, and throughout the country people secretly buy soap because it has been produced without
the king’s authorization. Then the king begins sending spies around to see whether we can catch those secretly making soap. And
among the craftsmen, the manufacturers, people begin to say: the king’s spies are around, watch out. This atmosphere is created in
which whatever one does, there is always the impression that someone behind you is spying and will go and report to the king. You
can imagine that the king has really brought it upon himself with these petty devices he has invented to make money. I shall quote
from a text from the very period in which the king was executed. “How many poor apple-women and broom-men, rag-merchants and people of all sorts, sold and pawned their bedding and their clothes to buy themselves the freedom of the new royal incorporation of the suburbs of London? And when all was done, it proved a cheat; thus was the king’s coffers filled with oppression.”. The king
does not declare it, but it is—or everyone knows—that he does this to avoid having to summon Parliament. And then he attempts
the decisive experiment. Until now these have been petty devices, but now let us try to introduce an actual new tax and see whether
the country pays. He does it very skilfully. In Elizabeth’s time there was a tax paid by coastal areas to maintain the war fleet.

## 8. Ship Money and the Defence of Property

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00601–U-00658 -->

And England is a country that traditionally needs a fleet to defend its coasts and, all things considered, willingly pays for it. So
the tax in Elizabeth’s time—the Ship Money, as they called it, money for ships—served a purpose: there was the Spanish Armada
sailing towards England. In Charles’s time the threat is not so obvious, but it is true that there are pirates in the Channel,
Dutch and French pirates; there are even cases of Barbary pirates arriving from the Mediterranean, landing in Cornwall to plunder
and carry off slaves. In short, the king tells public opinion, we are in an emergency, so once again I ask the coastal areas to pay
the tax, and the coastal areas pay. The next year, 1635, the king decides that Ship Money will no longer be paid only by coastal
towns, the maritime counties, but by the entire kingdom. This is the next step. And this time he cannot manage it, because protest
immediately begins to spread through public opinion, and the protest is expressed like this: if he wants us to pay, let him summon
Parliament. There are lords who tell the king to his face that they will tell their tenants not to pay. If instead the king summons
Parliament and listens to them, then everyone will gladly pay. At last a gentleman does everything he can to be put on trial so as to
bring the matter to a head. This gentleman is tried in London before the kingdom’s supreme court for inciting his dependants not
to pay, and the judges of the supreme court split. Half say, remember what King James used to say: the king is above the law. They,
who know Latin, put it this way, in Anglo-Latin: “I never read nor heard that Lex was Rex; but it is common and most true that Rex is Lex, for he is a living, a speaking, an acting law.”. And so he can do whatever he likes. But some of
the judges, appointed by the king—some of the judges say no, that is not true, the king cannot go that far. Because if we were to
admit that the king can force us to pay taxes without asking our consent, that is, without summoning Parliament, do you understand
me? If we were to admit this, it would mean there was no longer any defence of private property. And private property is sacred; it
is more sacred than the authority of the king placed upon the throne by God. Some of the judges declare that the king has no right
to put his hands into his subjects’ pockets, because if he did, the principle of property would collapse. And bear this in mind
because the English Revolution, the Civil War, is also a revolution; both definitions are fine. The English Revolution is the first
great bourgeois revolution, in the sense that it is not a revolt to change society; it is a revolt to guarantee private property. As
you see, we are already faced with a divided country and a king heading for a collision with enormous collective interests without
even realizing the gravity of his position. But the spark that will precipitate the Civil War is yet another matter.

## 9. Scotland, the Prayer Book, and the Covenant

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00659–U-00761 -->

It is a spark struck in the other kingdom of which Charles is king, Scotland. In the Kingdom of Scotland too. But Scotland is
Calvinist. For the Scots—not merely an avant-garde of Puritans but the mass of the population—the things the archbishop likes so
much, incense and holy images, are papist superstitions. Besides, as you can imagine, the Scots see the idea that their church, the
kirk, should be forced to adopt the same rules as the English church as an attack on their independence. And, Calvinists even more
than the English Puritans, the Scots are terrified that behind it all stands that monstrous reality, Rome, the Pope, the whore. As a
Scottish lord said, “If I have lain with never so many whores, I’ll never lie with the whore of Babylon.”. Rome, obviously. Yet the king and
archbishop are determined to bend Scotland and force a showdown. There is a new Anglican service book, the Book of Common Prayer,
and on Sunday, 23 July 1637 it is officially imposed in Scotland. In Edinburgh the crowd gathers outside the cathedral and beats on
the barred doors while the archbishop conducts the service inside an empty cathedral; throughout the service the crowd throws stones
at the cathedral windows. In theory Scotland is governed by the king’s council in Edinburgh, which ought to execute his orders. The
council declares the riot treason, but does so after shutting itself safely inside Holyrood Palace away from the Edinburgh crowd, and
at the same time prudently suspends use of the new service book in Scottish churches. When informed, the king disagrees and orders the
service book restored and the rioters immediately prosecuted. Arresting the rioters proves utterly impossible in Scotland: the whole
country is in the streets. It is said that some bishops appear at the altar with two loaded pistols in order to conduct the Anglican
rite. In fact the country refuses to accept it. They are not yet rebelling against the king. They send him a petition signed by very
many Scottish nobles, burgesses, and clergy asking for withdrawal of the prayer book. Notice modernity emerging here too: collecting
signatures as a political act. We send the king a request and beneath it are many signatures of important people. In February ’38 the
king replies that all dissenters must submit immediately if they wish to avoid his wrath. The proclamation is read in Edinburgh before
a furious crowd; in response Scottish nobles and clergy draft the National Covenant, declaring that no one will ever accept changes to
the rules of Scotland’s national church, not even if the king wishes them. The Covenant is read in every Scottish church, and for
days people queue to sign. Illiterate people swear to it in crowds, on their knees and weeping. The atmosphere is one of millenarian
enthusiasm. An Edinburgh lawyer writes in his diary: “On Wednesday, 28 February, that glorious marriage day of the Kingdom with God, … you will find a very close parallel between Israel and this church—the only two nations sworn to the Lord—between our two cases in our journeys.”. The primate of Scotland, the Archbishop of St Andrews, flees to London,
declaring: everything we have done in these thirty years has been destroyed in a day. And the king’s council writes from Edinburgh
that, given the results of his orders, it can no longer enforce them. Naturally news spreads throughout England. Because there is
printing, the Scottish Covenant is printed in a thousand clandestine presses and distributed. Archbishop Laud notes in his diary:
in London everyone has it in his hands. King Charles’s court jester, Armstrong—for the king naturally had court fools; he had
a giant, Muckle John, “muckle” being dialect for large, and he had a dwarf and a jester—meets the Archbishop of Canterbury
after this news from Scotland arrives and asks him: who is the fool now? Naturally he is immediately dismissed from his post.

## 10. The Covenanters and the First Bishops' War

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00762–U-00837 -->

In Scotland the Covenant’s supporters, now universally called the Covenanters, are in power, and there is talk of expelling everyone
who refuses to sign. You see how rapidly one slips into the extreme interpretation, don’t you? Clergy considered a little too
lukewarm are attacked in the street. And, as happens in such cases, some begin to fear that perhaps we have gone too far, that all
this might also produce results we do not want: revolution, social revolution. One of the chief Scottish poets of the period, William
Drummond, who will side with the king during the Civil War, expresses this well. Drummond writes: “Whether these great commotions and discords may not dissolve in bellum servile, and peasants, clowns, farmers, base people, all in arms, may not swallow the nobles and gentry, invest their possessions, adhere together by a new Covenant, and follow our example.”. Do you understand the marvel of “as we have done”? We—the lords, masters, rich, nobles, gentlemen, and clergy—made the
Covenant, and there is a risk that the rabble will take it seriously. On 1 July 1638 King Charles officially declares that rebellion
is under way in Scotland and that he intends to crush it by force. In England this war is extremely unpopular. Good people would
infinitely prefer the king to fight Spain. Instead he is on the best of terms with papist Spain, who knows why, and now wants us to
fight our Protestant brothers, the Scots. People curse the archbishop, the Pope of London. Naturally the Scots print an appeal to
the English people that circulates clandestinely throughout England. The king presses on, but it turns out that there is no money
for war against Scotland. Someone tells him: if there is no money, you know what you have to do, don’t you? Summon—no. The king
refuses to summon Parliament; he borrows instead, goes deeply into debt, yet still cannot raise enough money, and recruits a poor,
weak army. He nevertheless marches north in spring 1639. At the border he learns that Scotland is entirely in enemy hands: every
arsenal, garrison, and fortress, including Edinburgh Castle, has been occupied by Covenanters. The royal garrisons in Scotland have
joined the rebels. The king remains at the border with his weak, discontented army. Across from him camp the heavily armed Scots; from
their camp come preachers’ sermons and the collective singing of psalms. The lords with the king begin telling him that he should not
invade Scotland and that, if he insists, he must first summon Parliament. The king admits that for the moment he is not strong enough,
and makes a truce. He really intends only to gain time, but his advisers insist that this is too great a matter to undertake without
Parliament. The archbishop is among the first to say so. Another who insists is someone I have not yet mentioned but whom, in this
last half hour—no, God willing, in these final fifteen or twenty minutes—we shall mention again, because he is the protagonist
of a dramatic episode: the Earl of Strafford, governor of Ireland, one of the king’s chief and most trusted advisers. Both Laud
and Strafford insist that Parliament must be summoned, and the king summons it, after ten—eleven—years without doing so.

## 11. The Short Parliament and the Scottish Invasion

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00838–U-00950 -->

Parliament meets on 13 April 1640. The king attends the opening but does not deliver the speech, partly because he has a slight
stammer. Once, speaking to someone with the same defect, Charles had said: I tried holding pebbles in my mouth, but it is no
use. So the chancellor speaks for him, saying: “My lords, and you, the knights, citizens, and burgesses of the House of Commons: I doubt not but you rejoice at this day’s meeting ...; and good reason you have so to do, and with all humbleness of heart to acknowledge the great goodness of his majesty who, sequestering the memory of all former discouragements in preceding assemblies, is now, out of a fatherly affection to his people and a confidence that they will not be failing in their duty to him, pleased graciously to invite you and all his loving subjects to a sacred unity of hearts and affection in the service of him and of the commonwealth.”. But Parliament is flooded with petitions. This is the political mechanism:
county members arrive in London bringing letters received from neighbours, communities, rich men, and nobles, all protesting against
something—Ship Money, monopolies, the king’s religious policy. The Commons begins appointing committees, once again a laboratory of
modernity. It says: we must examine these petitions and protests, and if the king wants money he must abolish Ship Money—indeed no,
that is not enough—he must redress every grievance and hear every protest. You can imagine Charles’s reaction. On 5 May the king
dissolves Parliament, which goes down in history as the Short Parliament: two or three weeks. It is the fourth time in his reign that
Charles dissolves Parliament and the last time he succeeds. The Council immediately meets to decide how to fund this damned Scottish
war. Lord Strafford says that perhaps an army could be recruited in Ireland and brought here to reduce this kingdom to obedience. At
the time none of the listening ministers pays much attention. Even today we do not know whether by “this kingdom” Strafford
meant Scotland, which must be reduced to obedience, or had something else in mind; we shall return to it. Without Parliament there
is no money. Charles is in such difficulty that he even contacts the Pope to ask for a loan from Italy. He confiscates deposits
London merchants keep in the Royal Mint; you can imagine how popular that makes him. The City has had enough. When the king asks
the City merchants for loans, they refuse. The king—the procedures are these—has the London aldermen arrested and threatens to
hang them unless the money appears. But it does not appear, and recruitment goes terribly. The wildest rumours spread: the recruits
will supposedly be put aboard ships on the pretext of landing in Scotland, but actually sold as slaves in America. The result? The
king cannot assemble a real army. Meanwhile the Scots do, and on 20 August 1640 they cross the Tweed and invade England to bagpipes
and psalm-singing. The king receives yet another petition, this time signed by many lords asking for pardons for those imprisoned
for refusing new taxes or the archbishop’s reforms and, most dramatically, saying: we hear Strafford proposed bringing an army
from Ireland; we beg Your Majesty not to do it. Bear in mind what Ireland means to the English then: a savage country we conquered
and are filling with Protestant immigrants, whose local people are savages and papists, ferocious cut-throats thirsting for our
blood. Somehow the rumour spreads that Strafford suggested recruiting an army among these papist savages to tame this kingdom. What
exactly did he mean? And the king—I must say, there begins one of the most dishonourable pages from the king’s point of view,
showing him terribly inadequate and uncertain. Seeing that public opinion regards Strafford, one of his most trusted advisers,
as his evil genius who gives him bad advice, Charles treats him coldly and suggests he leave the country, saying he is not sure
he can guarantee Strafford’s physical safety. Strafford decides to remain. The king then gives his word that whatever happens,
Strafford need not fear for his life. Remember that. Meanwhile the Protestant population of the Scottish-occupied north sympathizes
with the Scots and regards them as liberators. Penniless and without an army, the king must sign a dishonourable agreement accepting
Scottish occupation of northern England and promising to pay the occupiers’ expenses. The only thing left is to summon a Parliament,
and he summons what will naturally go down in history as the Long Parliament.

## 12. The Long Parliament and Strafford's Fall

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00951–U-01066 -->

The election is dramatic because everyone knows clearly that the point of no return has been reached and this Parliament must necessarily
do great things. The king tries everything to elect reliable men and fails. The Court party is defeated everywhere. Courtiers and royal
officials are calculated to form only eleven per cent of the Commons, less than in any other Parliament. The election campaign’s
Puritan slogan against royal candidates was: do not elect these godless courtiers, these papist priests. Poorer voters—craftsmen
and shopkeepers who usually merely make up the numbers and vote as the local gentleman tells them—participate with unusual
commitment. This time radicals seek election on protest programmes. As a lord who will nevertheless side with Parliament says,
“... yet I shall not easily suffer myself hereafter upon the provocations of others to appear in any popular assemblies where fellows without shirts challenge as good a voice as myself, neither will I for any respect absent myself from any assembly, how great soever, whither my duty to the service of my King and country doth call me; where they shall know and (if they desire) shall feel my authority.”. Puritan preachers fan the flames and appeal to the people:
“You that are of the meaner rank, common people, be not discouraged, for God intends to make use of the common people in the great work of proclaiming the kingdom of his dear Son.”. There is this atmosphere of millenarian religious enthusiasm. Reading the news sheets, papers, and diaries of the days
when the members arrive is striking: it is the dawn of a fine day of long happiness for this kingdom; Parliament will build a new
world. For the great majority of Commons members, gentlemen and merchants, this does not mean social revolution. Absolutely not;
almost nobody wants that. It means Parliament must teach the king that Parliament commands, defend private property from royal
taxes, and eliminate the papist innovations beloved by the archbishop. Parliament opens on 3 November 1640, and this time the
king delivers the opening speech. Despite his stammer, he realizes he must show his face and personally asks for money for war
with Scotland. Parliament answers: one moment, other matters must first be clarified. First, you cannot impose new taxes without
assembling Parliament. The king cannot manage this Parliament and his ministers cannot defend him. He acknowledges that he indeed
lacks this right. But matters rapidly go further and assume the features of a revolution. People say Parliament is Elizabeth’s
true heir, as if James and Charles never existed, and that the House of Commons—not the king—is invested by God. Within weeks
the Commons acts as the kingdom’s government, sending orders to county justices of the peace. While about it, they abolish press
censorship, which worked poorly already. Speaker after speaker attacks the king and his ministers, and insists: what did Strafford
mean by recruiting an army in Ireland? A committee must investigate. In those days the Archbishop of Canterbury records not a dream
but another frightening event. In his study was a large portrait of himself by Van Dyck. Entering, he found it on the floor, fallen
from the wall. He writes: I am not superstitious, but God grant it is not an omen. Meanwhile, remember William Prynne, the Puritan
printer whose ears were cut off, cheeks branded, and who was imprisoned for life? Naturally he is triumphantly freed, and in his
place Strafford is sent to the Tower by Parliament’s order. A royal secretary’s son finds among the papers the minutes of the
meeting where Strafford spoke of an Irish army and delivers them to the opposition leaders. Ministers and secretaries begin boarding
ships and fleeing abroad. Meanwhile Puritan policy takes over. Catholic priests formerly left alone are arrested and some hanged,
drawn, and quartered, to the immense satisfaction of the good Puritan people. The Commons passes extremist Puritan religious laws:
no games on Sunday, all holy images removed from churches, no kneeling during worship—those papist grimaces. They also throw the
Archbishop of Canterbury into the Tower. In March ’41 Strafford’s trial opens before Parliament. He is accused of many things,
especially wanting to recruit an Irish army to subjugate this kingdom, meaning us free Englishmen. The two-month trial causes gigantic
excitement. Contemporary chronicles say the courtroom is packed, people camp there with bread, cheese, and beer, and supposedly
relieve themselves on the spot rather than lose their place. Strafford manages to show that he meant an army to subdue Scotland,
not England. But English law allows Parliament to pass a special act declaring a person a guilty criminal and condemning him to death
without trial. The Commons passes such an act against Strafford. It must pass the Lords and receive the king’s signature. The king
declares he will not sign. When it reaches the Lords, most wisely stay away. Few are present and a crowd rumbles outside. The king
has said he will not sign anyway, so the Lords wash their hands and pass it too. Then it reaches the king. He does not know what to
do, consults lawyers and bishops, becomes frightened, and signs—only to regret it immediately. Too late: two days later Strafford
is beheaded. Laud, imprisoned in the Tower, notes in his famous diary that serving that king had not been worthwhile. Up to here,
as we approach the end, it seems like the French Revolution, where every new royal concession pushes him closer to the abyss.

## 13. Polarization, Civil War, and the Limits of Revolution

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01067–U-01183 -->

But this time events go somewhat differently, because Parliament’s extremism, the violence of the Puritans, and Strafford’s
execution begin to frighten many people. Within Parliament a change of direction appears. With every vote a stronger minority supports
the king. The radical momentum that risks becoming a revolution is frightening. We undertook these reforms believing we were remedying
injustices and returning to Elizabeth’s good old days when everything went well; now innovations appear that nobody wanted or at
least expected. As if that were not enough, London has plague. Opposition leaders in the Commons receive abusive letters blaming
them for it; one receives an envelope containing what he immediately judges to be a plague-infected rag, sent by an enemy. The king
plays on this. He understands that more members are frightened and can be drawn to his side. He lets Parliament know that if this
continues—and here I quote—“... at last the common people (who in the mean time must be flattered, and to whom license must be given in all their wild humours, how contrary soever to established law, or their own real good) discover this arcanum imperii, that all this was done by them, but not for them, grow weary of journey-work, and set up for themselves, call parity and independence liberty, devour that estate which had devoured the rest; destroy all rights and properties, all distinctions of families and merit; and by this means this splendid and excellently distinguished form of government end in a dark equal chaos of confusion, and the long line of our many noble ancestors in a Jack Cade, or a Wat Tyler.”. The king knows he is
touching a nerve because the members are all lords and gentlemen. He counts his minority at every vote, and it grows. Finally, in
January ’42, he feels strong enough to force a showdown. He enters Parliament with soldiers and a list of members to arrest. He
does not find them. Parliament resists, the wanted men escape, and the Speaker tells the king he is not there to obey him but the
Commons. The king leaves hostile London for the more loyal north and begins recruiting soldiers; Parliament does likewise. He goes
to Hull, near the Scottish border, where a large arms depot was established for the Scottish war. The governor refuses him entry. The
king orders his arrest as a traitor—but who will arrest him? Impossible. The king tries to take Hull by force and is repelled. On 22
August Charles raises his standard at Nottingham, declaring war on Parliament’s rebels. The country splits, and Parliament splits
too. A little over half the Commons remain with Parliament, but two fifths go to the king. Most Lords support the king, but not all;
about a quarter support Parliament. The north is for the king, the south and London for Parliament; the merchants and money are for
Parliament. One surprising fact emerges from every possible analysis: the young support the king. Royalist members and lords are
on average much younger than Parliamentarians. This is generally explained by saying that Charles’s absolutist project was not so
anachronistic, but in keeping with the times. Young people who had known only that did not necessarily reject it; older people who
remembered a time when the king did not claim absolute power did. Thus war begins, amid a thousand fears, because everyone wants
victory but fears a social revolution, which nobody wants. As an anti-royalist member writes: “And there is no doubt that all right and property, all meum et tuum, must cease in a civil war, and we know not what advantage the meaner sort also may take to divide the spoils of the rich and noble amongst them, who begin already to allege that all being of one mould there is no reason that some should have so much and others so little.”. Parliament will win after six long years, and the rabble will gain nothing. Six
years of Civil War end where we began tonight, with the king’s beheading at Whitehall. Laud preceded him by four years. Tried
for treason in ’45, he was prosecuted by none other than William Prynne, the man whose ears he had cut off. Prynne, appointed
public prosecutor by the Commons, does everything to convict the archbishop: rummages through his papers, reads his diary, bribes
witnesses. He cannot secure a conviction. No problem: Parliament passes an act declaring the archbishop an enemy of the people and
condemning him to death. He is beheaded four years before his king. The people’s covenant, as the Scottish poet called it, and
social revolution will not happen. The idea that all men are equal remains confined to a few extremists’ sermons. Those who try
to realize it—and some will—discover that the Levellers seek political and legal equality while generally defending private
property; economic levelling and common ownership are associated more closely with the Diggers, or True Levellers. [N-007] They discover that Parliament’s army is entirely ready to suppress by shooting and bloodshed
those who have not understood that at a certain point one must stop. But of course this was neither the first time, nor will it be
the last. Thank you. Thank you all; until tomorrow evening, then. Thank you, Professor. As we were saying.
