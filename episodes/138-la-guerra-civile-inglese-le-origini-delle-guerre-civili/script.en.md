# Civil Wars: England 1642

## 1. Charles I's Execution and the Civil War Question

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00055 -->

How does a country where people speak the same language and share the same traditions split so completely that civil war breaks out? That is much harder to explain than a war between rival states. This episode begins our civil-war series.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-002 -->The subject is seventeenth-century England, perhaps the least known of the civil wars in this series.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-003 -->

Let us begin at the end. We are in London,
it is 30 January 1649, and a scaffold has been erected in the center of London, at Whitehall. It
is intended for the King of England and Scotland, Charles I, who is led there on foot from his
palace of St James’s, which is just a stone’s throw away. On the scaffold he is allowed to
make one final speech. The king declares: “And truly I desire their [the people's]
Liberty and Freedom, but I must tell you, That their Liberty
and their Freedom consists in having of Government. It is not for having share in Government; that is nothing
pertaining to them.”

It is two in the afternoon when the king places his head on the block,
says a prayer, then stretches out his hands to signal that he is ready. And the executioner,
who has been hired in secret, is masked, and nobody knows who he is; the executioner cuts off
his head with a single blow. Years later, a Protestant preacher who witnessed the execution as a boy remembers: “There was such a groan by the thousands then present
as I never heard before and desire I may never hear again.” The king’s head is shown to
the people; the next day it is sewn back onto the body, which is embalmed with ointments and
buried in the chapel of Windsor Castle.

Now, since Constantine had converted to Christianity
1,300 years earlier, many Christian emperors and kings had been murdered. Others had died in
battle. The English themselves had murdered at least two of their kings in the Middle Ages,
Edward II and Richard II. But Charles I is the first king in Europe to die after being legally
tried and sentenced to death for high treason. The execution of Charles I is the end point of
a civil war that lasted six years.

## 2. The Illusion of Peace and a Remote Court

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00056–U-00111 -->

And yet, shortly before that war breaks out, England considers itself a country blessed
by peace. Let us turn the clock back a dozen years. In seventeenth-century England an entertainment called the masque is all the rage. These are costumed performances
combining music, singing, ballet, and scenery; they are also tremendously fashionable at
court. Masques are staged in which the king and queen themselves rehearse their parts for
months, and the content is always allegorical, in keeping with the taste of the time. In 1634,
while England is the only major country outside the Thirty Years’ War, London’s lawyers stage a masque called *The Triumph of Peace* in honor of the king. [N-003] It
is the most expensive ever seen up to that time, with more than two hundred participants. And
its theme is the triumph of peace.

That year Charles I is talking to his German nephews, the
sons of his sister, who married a German prince—his German nephews, who come from a Germany
devastated beyond description by the Thirty Years’ War—and in that year, in that ’37,
Charles I lets slip, that he is the happiest king in Christendom. In reality
that happiness is illusory and discontent is brewing in England, but the king has not the slightest suspicion of it. He has no idea because the lifestyle of a
king of that period separates him completely from his people. Court etiquette seems designed
expressly to isolate the king from the country and make him live in an unreal world. All courts of the period have extremely rigid etiquette that transforms the king’s daily existence into
a kind of reality show. The English court, though, is perhaps the most formal in Europe. The King
of England is the only king in Europe who is served at table by courtiers, nobles, on their
knees. And ordinary people have no contact with the king. Of course, the king always lives in public, always under somebody’s eyes, but those people are the courtiers. Ordinary people
never see him, never meet him, except on two occasions, twice a year. There: twice a year
the King of England, like the King of France, touches sufferers from scrofula, because for
centuries in those two kingdoms there has been a tradition, a popular belief, that scrofula
is a special disease and that kings possess a magical power and can cure those who suffer from
it. Do not be surprised that such things exist in the seventeenth century: seventeenth-century
society and mentality are not so very different from those of three centuries earlier. Kings
touch the sick and the sick come to be touched. But to be admitted to the king’s touch, to
hope to recover from this illness that everyone calls the king’s evil, the sick must have a
certificate bearing the three signatures of a parish priest, an officer of their parish, and a
justice of the peace. Charles has no idea what the country’s true feelings are towards him,
his government, and his court. And I dwell on the court because it is not so much the king’s person at first, but the environment surrounding him—the court—that becomes the focus of the country’s resentment and hostility.

## 3. James I, Buckingham, and the Court's Disrepute

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00112–U-00179 -->

The court’s disrepute already begins in the time of his father, James I. Before that
Elizabeth reigns. Elizabeth is extremely popular; there is also great skill in propaganda,
but Elizabeth truly is extremely popular. Elizabeth dies without heirs, and James I, King of
Scotland, ascends the English throne. And already under James I public opinion suddenly begins
instead to distrust the court and the courtiers. The king, let’s put it this way, seems to do everything he can to encourage public opinion to be distrustful. The king
has a prime minister, the Duke of Buckingham, much younger than himself, a favorite, as they say then, but one who in fact governs the kingdom. The Duke of Buckingham is detested because
he sells everything: he sells noble titles, bishoprics—whatever anyone wants, if he pays, the
duke gives it to him. And King James has a sexual relationship with his prime minister, the Duke
of Buckingham. At the time it is gossip; today it is an established fact, because we have their
letters. King James writes to his prime minister, “And so God bless you, my sweet child and wife,
and grant that ye may ever be a comfort to your dear dad and husband …” Now, obviously the public does not know these letters; we know them today. Perhaps we have no reason to be scandalized
by a relationship of that kind, but imagine seventeenth-century public opinion. Everyone knows
there is a favorite governing the kingdom and that the king has a strange relationship with
this favorite, who is so much younger than he is. And gossip spreads across England about court
orgies, about the king and his friends having sexual relations and getting drunk. Then James I
dies and is succeeded by his son Charles, who many years later will end up on the scaffold in
London. Charles is a very different character: an austere, cold figure who gives no grounds for
gossip. But he does not get rid of the Duke of Buckingham, who is his friend and has a certain hold over him too. Buckingham continues to govern England for a few years
until he is murdered, whereupon the country rejoices and his murderer is celebrated in ballads
as a great English hero. In other words, the court’s image is beyond repair.

On top of that,
the king has married a French princess. Queen Henrietta is French in a country, England, that
detests the French; she is Catholic in a country where the majority is fiercely Protestant;
and, much younger than her husband, she loves dancing and theater. The Venetian ambassadors
write that the English court is the most sumptuous and cheerful in the world. In the eyes of
most English people, this is not a positive thing. In the English heartland, living far from
court, the idea takes hold that the king, the court, the royal family, the courtiers, and
the ministers are a foreign body, that the court has nothing to do with the country. A great
historian, Lawrence Stone, expressed this memorably, so I’ll quote him instead of trying to
put it in my own words. “The Country was virtuous, the Court wicked; the Country was thrifty,
the Court extravagant; the Country was honest, the Court corrupt; the Country was chaste and
heterosexual, the Court promiscuous and homosexual; the Country was sober, the Court drunken;
the Country was nationalist, the Court xenophile; … the Country was solidly Protestant,
even Puritan, the Court was deeply tainted by popish leanings.”

## 4. Anglican Authority and Puritan Dissent

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00180–U-00246 -->

Let us pause over this last aspect, which is crucial. Why? Because religion is still
fundamental in this society which, I repeat, has not truly emerged from the Middle Ages. One
of the characteristics of our medieval past is precisely the pervasiveness of religion, the
importance religion has for people. We have said that the country is Puritan. What does that
mean? It means that officially—officially—in England after the Protestant Reformation
there is a single church, the Anglican Church, headed by the king. Mind you, this religious
aspect is enormously important. That is to say, we are already entering into the question of
the causes of the Civil War, where political factors, as we’ll see, will play a gigantic role, but the religious dimension is fundamental.

Now, the Anglican Church
is a Protestant church inasmuch as it rejects the authority of the Pope; indeed, it abhors
what they call popery. But in the way it actually operates, the Anglican Church of the period
greatly resembles the Catholic Church. It is an authoritarian church with its hierarchy of
parish priests and bishops; it is a church that expects the faithful to allow themselves to be
guided like a flock, without questioning the teachings that come from the pulpit. Except that
this is seventeenth-century England, which, although still a medieval country, is on its way
to becoming the most modern country in Europe. And, for example, it is a country where more and
more people know how to read and write. Knowing how to read means being exposed to circulating
propaganda and news sheets. When many people, even poor people, can read and write, religious feeling runs deep, and an authoritarian state church tells everyone what to believe, you have a recipe for dissent. And England is
full of dissident movements, religious dissidents. Sects, as the authorities call them. They
are the Puritans. They begin as unauthorized reading groups, lay people who gather to read the
Bible together because they can read and have no need of the parish priest; gradually they
end up rejecting the official church. They are strict Protestants, even fanatics; they hate
all those Catholic elements that have remained in Anglican ritual—the crucifixes, the holy
images: idolatry and superstition to the Puritans. The Puritans favor personal reading of the
Bible; they want pastors elected by the faithful, with no bishops to command them,
or even lay people themselves to preach to the others. Now, I would say that if we stopped here
we might even say, well, the Puritans are rather likeable. They have a decidedly democratic,
anti-authoritarian approach.

Naturally, we must give the complete picture. The Puritans are
religious fundamentalists; they dress only in black; they want to ban every sport, every
entertainment, dancing, and close the theaters in the England where Shakespeare had died only a
few years before. The Puritans consider themselves the chosen people, the new Israel, and expect
God to exterminate their opponents and lead them into a new Jerusalem. And perhaps we would like
that less if we had to meet them. But Puritanism nevertheless really does mean calling into
question the obligation to obey the authorities. As one of the great thinkers of the English
seventeenth century, Hobbes—Thomas Hobbes, who has no sympathy at all for any of this—says,
and I quote Hobbes: “For after the Bible was translated into English, every man, nay, every
boy and wench, that could read English, thought they spoke with God Almighty, and understood
what he said, when they had read the Scriptures once or
twice.” Hobbes, of course, will side with the king during the Civil War and will be
forced to flee abroad.

## 5. Archbishop Laud's Campaign for Conformity

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00247–U-00341 -->

Because in fact the point is that King Charles’s policy brings him into direct conflict with
the Puritans, with religious dissent. Why? Because he is the head of the Church of England and
insists that all his subjects belong to the Church of England and obey the Anglican bishops. Pay attention to the bishops, because it is particularly significant. In the
Protestant world, the idea of a hierarchical church, with a head and then bishops, has almost
always been called into question. In England everyone knows that if the head of the church is the
king, it means that he chooses the bishops, and that the bishops have no autonomy at all: they
put on great airs and want to give orders, but in reality they are in the king’s service. It
is an old story. Already in Elizabeth’s time there is evidence that the weak point of the
Anglican Church is precisely the fact that the bishops cannot make the people respect them. As
an archbishop writes in Elizabeth’s time, “The messengers of Christ … are esteemed as
the filth of the world.”

But Charles is determined to put an end to all this and restore
the authority of the Anglican episcopate, and the man to whom he entrusts this task is the
Archbishop of Canterbury, William Laud, who is a crucial figure in our story. Archbishop William
Laud is the man who persuades the king—and he has no difficulty persuading him—that to save
throne and altar together, religious dissent must be crushed. The archbishop is a man of modest
origins, the son of a merchant, very learned, and according to contemporary observers not very
well mannered. They describe him as a small, ruddy man, irritable and intolerant. In reality,
Archbishop William Laud must have a few doubts in his unconscious, but he is unwilling to
admit them even to himself—and at that time they know less about the unconscious than we think
we know.

Still, the archbishop keeps a diary in which he notes the dreams he has,
especially when he has disturbing dreams. Once he writes: “I dreamed, that K. C. was to be
Married to a Minister’s Widow. And that I was called upon to do it. No Service-Book could
be found; and in my own Book, which I had, I could not find the Order for Marriage.” Then
the archbishop wakes up wondering what message God might have sent him through this dream. Having read Dr. Freud, we’re tempted to say that the archbishop knows there is something not quite
right about the king’s wife being a foreign, Catholic princess. And yet by now the king can
no longer marry the daughter of a good Protestant; he already has his Catholic wife. If these doubts come to the archbishop in dreams, by day he forgets them. And by day
he proceeds relentlessly with his program. And so, the Puritans want to destroy holy
images and even crucifixes, altars. The archbishop imposes stained glass, organs, surpliced
clergy, the obligation to kneel for communion, candles, holy images, the priest bowing to the
altar, the sign of the cross. In the Puritans’ judgement, monkey grimaces. And obviously the
archbishop’s program calls for the elimination of the dissident sects. Discipline must be
restored. The Puritans read the Bible and then start preaching? And they are good at giving
sermons, at preaching, but the archbishop decides that far too many sermons are already heard
in this country. The people do not need sermons; they need catechism. They must learn by heart
what they need to know and concern themselves with nothing else. And when a parish priest,
or even a bishop, does not quite agree—because the suspicion that all this means returning
to Catholicism is widespread in this country, which has learned to have a damned fear of
papists—well, when a bishop or parish priest does not agree, he is replaced, dismissed,
perhaps even put in prison.

The Puritans who feel most endangered flee the country. Do you know where they go? They go to America. Do you remember one of the founding legends of American
history? The Pilgrim Fathers who arrive on the *Mayflower* and found the Puritan colonies of
New England. And when do they go there? They go in the years of Charles and Archbishop Laud. So
many of these Puritans go to America that instead of being pleased, the king worries that the
country risks losing subjects. For a moment Charles thinks of banning emigration to America. Then
he does not do so.

## 6. Print, Persecution, and Fear of Popery

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00342–U-00457 -->

Still, for every Puritan who leaves, ten more operate underground at home. So far, once again, we’re looking at both the medieval and the modern world. There is nothing
different from what already happened in the Middle Ages, when Christian society was full of
rebels, dissidents, people who did not believe.

But now, in the seventeenth century, there is an
enormous difference. There is printing. And printing means that innumerable treatises, sermons,
prayers, orations, homilies, and pamphlets circulate throughout the kingdom in defense of the
good Protestants persecuted by the king and his archbishop, the friends of papists. In theory
there is a law controlling the press: nothing may be published without authorization. But the
state apparatus at that time is too weak to enforce it. Everyone prints whatever they like,
clandestinely. The archbishop, yes, when he manages to lay hands on somebody who has written and
published something wrong, has to settle for making an example of someone now and then.

I’ll give you one. There is a Puritan called William Prynne; he is a one-hundred-percent Puritan who
criticizes men for wearing their hair long, the fashion among young court nobles at the time, and
criticizes women for wearing their hair short—how dare they make themselves look like men. William
Prynne is one of those who would like to abolish not only the theater but even the celebration
of Christmas. He is someone who maintains that drinking a toast to someone’s health is already
a diabolical thing, a sin. William Prynne publishes a little book in which he criticizes the
theater and actresses in particular, because in Shakespeare’s time women are not seen on stage—the female parts are played by boys—but by now this taboo too has fallen. Women go on
stage; in William Prynne’s eyes it is something—it is proof of the moral abyss into which
England is sinking. So in this book he condemns actresses in particular. Unfortunately, the
queen at court has just taken part in a performance, acting in person before the courtiers. The
result? William Prynne’s book is regarded as an outrage against the court, so Prynne is seized,
sentenced to the pillory, to have his ears cut off, and to life imprisonment. It is 1634. Then,
imprisoned in the Tower of London, Prynne manages to continue writing and publishing. He writes
another pamphlet protesting that a kind of Catholic Inquisition has now arisen in England and
is hunting down true Protestants; he manages to have it published, and they discover this in
1637, the year in which he had said that he was the happiest king in Christendom. <!-- omitted:
confirmed source-audio duplication --> <!-- omitted: confirmed source-audio duplication -->

In 1637 Prynne, who is already in prison in the Tower, is again sentenced to the pillory, to
have the little bits of ear they had left him the first time cut off, and to be branded on the
cheeks with the letters S and L. Seditious libeller. The crowd watching him being taken to the
pillory lays flowers and fragrant herbs on the road he must travel, and shouts and protests
against the police. The executioner treats him with particular brutality when he has to brand
him. Incidentally, it appears that on these occasions the condemned man can pay the executioner
a bribe and is treated better or worse according to how much he pays. Prynne must not
have paid. The people are furious and shout that if he had been a papist nothing would have
happened to him, because the good people of England are increasingly convinced that the king
and his courtiers are papists at heart.

In reality Charles is a convinced Protestant, but he
is not a fanatic. And in foreign policy, for example, he has allied himself with Spain. Spain
is a great Catholic kingdom, certainly, but it is France’s enemy, and we too are France’s
enemies. And so Charles pursues a policy of alliance with Spain. For many people in England,
allying with Spain means making a pact with the devil.

Then there are still Catholics in
England. It is not forbidden to be Catholic, partly because many lords and gentlemen have
remained attached to the old religion. They have no civil rights of any kind, but they are not
persecuted. There are brutal laws against the Catholic clergy; Catholic worship is obviously
forbidden in a Protestant country, and an Englishman is forbidden to be a Catholic priest. The
law says that if a Catholic priest who is an English subject is arrested in the kingdom, he must
be sentenced to death. But the king does not really enforce these laws. The priests are there;
nobody arrests them. There are even Franciscan friars, clandestine but known to everyone. And
then the queen is Catholic. And Catholic Mass is celebrated for the queen at court. I hope
you are beginning to understand what it means for a craftsman in the City or a small Yorkshire
landowner to hear that there are Catholic priests at court celebrating Catholic Mass for the
queen. And the courtiers attend because it is fashionable to be seen at the queen’s Mass. The
king does not attend, but people believe that he does. Then the king really does sometimes
enjoy discussing things with his wife’s Catholic priests. The queen has a small community
of Capuchins; once the king goes to visit them and remains to eat at their table. All these
things give rise to a flood of insulting pamphlets and writings that circulate throughout the
kingdom and that everyone reads. The good Puritans become convinced that the king is determined
to return the country to popery. Yet in the archbishop’s reports to the king he describes a
devout, obedient, loyal population; the campaign to restore authority is going extremely well;
there is nothing to worry about. And precisely because he is certain that all is well—and
certain that God is with him, naturally—certain that all is well and that God is on his side,
the king commits himself to another conflict parallel to the first, which is likewise supposed
to allow him to assert his authority and which will instead be his ruin:

## 7. Parliament, Royal Authority, and Fiscal Expedients

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00458–U-00600 -->

the conflict with Parliament. What is Parliament? From your school studies, you all remember
that people eventually begin to speak of absolute monarchy, absolute sovereigns. They
begin to speak of it in the sixteenth century and especially the seventeenth—the Sun King. When
people spoke about the Middle Ages, you never heard of absolute sovereigns. Because a medieval king has a strong limit on his power. He is not above the law and is unable to introduce new
taxes without securing the consent of the country, without consulting the country. How does a medieval king consult the country when he needs money? He summons an assembly attended by the
nobles and bishops—the nobility, the clergy—and then by the merchants from the towns, who are the people with the money; in France they eventually come to be called the Third
Estate. These assemblies, which have a thousand names in the various countries, but in England are already called Parliament, are summoned and have to agree if the king asks for money. But
seventeenth-century kings find it increasingly tiresome to submit to this obligation. After
all, is it not God who places them on the throne? And therefore they should answer only to
God. Charles grows up breathing this atmosphere. His father James writes: “Kings are called Gods by the propheticall King David, because they sit upon God his
Throne in the earth, and have the count of their administration to give unto him.” James had
written a handbook to instruct his son Charles in his future profession as king, in which he
taught him that the king must be a father to his people, but like every father has the right
of life and death and the right to absolute obedience. And James taught his son that the king
is above the law. He is not limited by any law, because it is he who makes the laws and, if
he wishes, abolishes them.

Charles devotes his life to putting these principles into effect,
which in practical terms means trying to govern without summoning Parliament. Other European
kings do it too and eventually succeed. In England it is a particularly difficult undertaking,
because England is the country where Parliament has gained most ground and where already in the
Middle Ages kings have to swear not to govern without the country’s consent. You know Magna
Carta. Magna Carta reinforces the principle that the king is constrained by law and that certain
taxation requires common counsel, although Parliament develops only later. [N-005] Parliaments in
England are—this is good, you can all picture it—the House of Lords, where the great lords
and bishops sit, and they generally go where the king wishes. But the truly important house is
the House of Commons, representing the rural aristocracy, the gentry, as the English call them,
which is the social class governing the kingdom, because no state apparatus exists. The king can govern only as long as the country gentlemen keep order, act as judges, and collect taxes. And
then, besides the gentry, there are precisely those who have the money, those who have cash:
the merchants of the towns, the City of London. This is the House of Commons. And every time
Charles summons Parliament in the early years of his reign, he discovers that whatever he asks
of the Commons, someone disagrees, and somebody always gets up to speak against his demands
and question his policy. Parliaments are summoned, remain in session for a while, then go home;
perhaps for a few years there is no need for one, and when there is, another is summoned. The
king summons a new Parliament and finds the same faces as last time, because in the country,
in the counties, there are men who have by now gained experience and get themselves elected and
go to Parliament expressly to tell the king that the country disagrees with his policy.

In other words—and I hope you see what this means— in this country, which in its own way is a laboratory
of modernity, the concept of opposition is being born. In theory Parliament is not expected to
oppose the king’s demands on principle; it discusses them and then, broadly speaking, tries
to accommodate him. In the English Parliament, instead, there is an opposition: a substantial
group in the Commons that on principle disagrees with what the king wants. Every time Charles
summons Parliament, he finds it increasingly difficult to obtain what he wants, and in the end he
grows tired of it.

In 1629 the king declares that since Parliament opposes his policy, he will
summon it no more. Many members of Parliament protest and end up in prison. From that moment,
for ten years, Charles governs without summoning Parliament again. And he can delude himself,
can tell himself that he has become an absolute sovereign, free of all restraints. The problem is that his ministers are not at all certain that they can really impose new taxes
without summoning Parliament and without the country rebelling; they do not dare to do it. But
money is needed, and therefore the king is forced to try to make money by other means. Where do you look for money? Where is the money? In the economy, in trade. Making money means: we cannot impose
new taxes, but we can say, for example, that all the rag merchants in London must enrol in a
corporation and pay a license to practise their trade; otherwise they cannot do so. And this is
done, and a whole series of controls, obligations, in effect taxes on commercial activity, are
introduced. They are introduced, and naturally the purpose is to take money, and then to monitor
people and catch the violators and fine them.

Monopolies are granted, as I was telling you. I’ll give you an example. Here is one example, because it is hard to imagine such a thing happening in a seventeenth-century kingdom.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-004 --> So, the king
grants an entrepreneur a monopoly over the manufacture of soap. The entrepreneur obviously pays
a great deal of money to be the only person in England allowed to produce soap. All the other
factories close, naturally, immediately creating a whole world of discontented people. The
monopolist makes soap, and since he is the only one who makes it, he need not worry too much
about quality. After a while protest spreads throughout the kingdom because the king’s soap is
of very poor quality. The government decides to prove to the country that this is not true. You
say it is poor quality? Let us see. Public tests are organized and washerwomen summoned, but the
tests are rigged to show that the king’s soap works extremely well. Contraband soap begins to
be produced, and throughout the country people secretly buy soap because it has been produced
without the king’s authorization. Then the king begins sending spies around to see whether we
can catch those secretly making soap. And among the craftsmen, the manufacturers, people begin to
say: the king’s spies are around, watch out. This atmosphere is created in which whatever one
does, there is always the impression that someone behind you is spying and will go and report
to the king. You can imagine that the king has really brought it upon himself with these petty
devices he has invented to make money. I’ll quote from a text from the very period in which
the king was executed. “How many poor apple-women and broom-men, rag-merchants and people
of all sorts, sold and pawned their bedding and their clothes to buy themselves the freedom of
the new royal incorporation of the suburbs of London? And when all was done, it proved a cheat;
thus was the king’s coffers filled with oppression.” The king does not declare it, but it
is—or everyone knows—that he does this to avoid having to summon Parliament. And then he
attempts the decisive experiment. Until now these have been petty devices, but now let us try
to introduce an actual new tax and see whether the country pays. He does it very skilfully. In
Elizabeth’s time there was a tax paid by coastal areas to maintain the war fleet.

## 8. Ship Money and the Defence of Property

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00601–U-00658 -->

And England is a country that traditionally needs a fleet to defend its coasts and, all
things considered, willingly pays for it. So the tax in Elizabeth’s time—the Ship Money,
as they called it, money for ships—served a purpose: there was the Spanish Armada sailing
towards England. In Charles’s time the threat is not so obvious, but it is true that there
are pirates in the Channel, Dutch and French pirates; there are even cases of Barbary pirates
arriving from the Mediterranean, landing in Cornwall to plunder and carry off slaves. In short,
the king tells public opinion, we are in an emergency, so once again I ask the coastal areas
to pay the tax, and the coastal areas pay.

The next year, 1635, the king decides that Ship
Money will no longer be paid only by coastal towns, the maritime counties, but by the entire
kingdom. This is the next step. And this time he cannot manage it, because protest immediately
begins to spread through public opinion, and the protest is expressed like this: if he wants us
to pay, let him summon Parliament. There are lords who tell the king to his face that they will
tell their tenants not to pay. If instead the king summons Parliament and listens to them, then
everyone will gladly pay.

At last a gentleman does everything he can to be put on trial so as
to bring the matter to a head. This gentleman is tried in London before the kingdom’s supreme
court for inciting his dependants not to pay, and the judges of the supreme court split. Half
say, remember what King James used to say: the king is above the law. They, who know Latin,
put it this way, in Anglo-Latin: “it is common
and most true that Rex is Lex, for he is a living, a speaking, an acting law.” And so he can
do whatever he likes. But some of the judges, appointed by the king—some of the judges say
no, that is not true, the king cannot go that far. Because if we were to admit that the king
can force us to pay taxes without asking our consent, that is, without summoning Parliament,
you see what that means? If they admit that, private property no longer has any protection. And private property is sacred; it is more sacred than the authority of
the king placed upon the throne by God. Some of the judges declare that the king has no right
to put his hands into his subjects’ pockets, because if he did, the principle of property
would collapse.

And bear this in mind because the English Revolution, the Civil War, is also
a revolution; either term works. The English Revolution is the first great bourgeois
revolution, in the sense that it is not a revolt to change society; it is a revolt to guarantee
private property. As you see, we are already faced with a divided country and a king heading
for a collision with enormous collective interests without even realizing the gravity of his
position. But the spark that will precipitate the Civil War is yet another matter.

## 9. Scotland, the Prayer Book, and the Covenant

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00659–U-00761 -->

The spark that turns the English crisis into a war begins in Charles’s other kingdom: Scotland. Scotland is Calvinist.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-005 --> For the Scots—not merely an avant-garde of Puritans
but the mass of the population—the things the archbishop likes so much, incense and holy
images, are papist superstitions. Besides, as you can imagine, the Scots see the idea that
their church, the kirk, should be forced to adopt the same rules as the English church as an
attack on their independence. And, Calvinists even more than the English Puritans, the Scots
are terrified that behind it all stands that monstrous reality, Rome, the Pope, the whore. As a
Scottish lord says, “If I have lain with never so many whores, I’ll never lie with the whore
of Babylon.” Rome, obviously. Yet the king and archbishop are determined to bend Scotland and
force a showdown.

There is a new Anglican service book, the Book of Common Prayer, and on Sunday,
23 July 1637 it is officially imposed in Scotland. In Edinburgh the crowd gathers outside the
cathedral and beats on the barred doors while the archbishop conducts the service inside an
empty cathedral; throughout the service the crowd throws stones at the cathedral windows. In
theory Scotland is governed by the king’s council in Edinburgh, which is supposed to carry out his orders. The council declares the riot treason, but does so after shutting itself safely inside
Holyrood Palace away from the Edinburgh crowd, and at the same time prudently suspends use
of the new service book in Scottish churches. When informed, the king disagrees and orders the
service book restored and the rioters immediately prosecuted. Arresting the rioters proves utterly
impossible in Scotland: the whole country is in the streets. It is said that some bishops appear
at the altar with two loaded pistols in order to conduct the Anglican rite. The country simply refuses to accept it.

They are not yet rebelling against the king. They send him a petition
signed by very many Scottish nobles, burgesses, and clergy asking for withdrawal of the prayer
book. Here too, you can see modern politics emerging: collecting signatures as a political act. We send the
king a request and beneath it are many signatures of important people.

In February ’38 the
king replies that all dissenters must submit immediately if they wish to avoid his wrath. The
proclamation is read in Edinburgh before a furious crowd; in response Scottish nobles and clergy
draft the National Covenant, declaring that no one will ever accept changes to the rules of
Scotland’s national church, not even if the king wishes them. The Covenant is read in every
Scottish church, and for days people queue to sign. Illiterate people swear to it in crowds,
on their knees and weeping. The atmosphere is one of millenarian enthusiasm. An Edinburgh
lawyer writes in his diary: “On Wednesday, 28 February, that glorious marriage day of the
Kingdom with God, … you will find a very close parallel between Israel and this church—the
only two nations sworn to the Lord—between our two cases in our journeys.” The primate of
Scotland, the Archbishop of St Andrews, flees to London, declaring: everything we have done in
these thirty years has been destroyed in a day. And the king’s council writes from Edinburgh
that, given the results of his orders, it can no longer enforce them.

Naturally news spreads
throughout England. Because there is printing, the Scottish Covenant is printed in a thousand
clandestine presses and distributed. Archbishop Laud notes in his diary: in London everyone has
it in his hands. King Charles’s court jester, Armstrong—for the king naturally has court fools; he has a giant, Muckle John, “muckle” being dialect for large, and he has a dwarf
and a jester—meets the Archbishop of Canterbury after this news from Scotland arrives and
asks him: who is the fool now? Naturally, he is fired on the spot.

## 10. The Covenanters and the First Bishops' War

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00762–U-00837 -->

In Scotland the Covenant’s supporters, now universally called the Covenanters, are in power,
and there is talk of expelling everyone who refuses to sign. You see how rapidly one slips into
the extreme interpretation, don’t you? Clergy considered a little too lukewarm are attacked in
the street. And, as happens in such cases, some begin to fear that perhaps we have gone too far,
that all this might also produce results we do not want: revolution, social revolution. One of
the chief Scottish poets of the period, William Drummond, who will side with the king during the
Civil War, expresses this well. Drummond writes: “Whether these great commotions and discords
may not dissolve in bellum servile, and peasants, clowns, farmers, base people, all in arms, may
not swallow the nobles and gentry, invest their possessions, adhere together by a new Covenant,
and follow our example.” Do you see what’s so remarkable about “as we have done”? We—the lords,
masters, rich, nobles, gentlemen, and clergy—made the Covenant, and there is a risk that the
rabble will take it seriously.

On 1 July 1638 King Charles officially declares that rebellion is
under way in Scotland and that he intends to crush it by force. In England this war is extremely
unpopular. Good people would much rather the king to fight Spain. Instead he is on the best
of terms with papist Spain, who knows why, and now wants us to fight our Protestant brothers,
the Scots. People curse the archbishop, the Pope of London. Naturally the Scots print an appeal
to the English people that circulates clandestinely throughout England.

The king refuses to back down,
but it turns out that there is no money for war against Scotland. Someone tells him: if there
is no money, you know what you have to do, don’t you? Summon—no. The king refuses to summon
Parliament; he borrows instead, goes deeply into debt, yet still cannot raise enough money,
and recruits a poor, weak army.

He nevertheless marches north in spring 1639. At the border he
learns that Scotland is entirely in enemy hands: every arsenal, garrison, and fortress, including
Edinburgh Castle, has been occupied by Covenanters. The royal garrisons in Scotland have joined
the rebels. The king remains at the border with his weak, discontented army. Across from him camp
the heavily armed Scots; from their camp come preachers’ sermons and the collective singing of
psalms. The lords with the king begin telling him that he should not invade Scotland and that,
if he insists, he must first summon Parliament.

The king admits that for the moment he is not
strong enough, and makes a truce. He really intends only to gain time, but his advisers insist
that this is too great a matter to undertake without Parliament. The archbishop is among the
first to say so. Another is the Earl of Strafford, governor of Ireland and one of the king’s most trusted advisers. He will become the protagonist of a dramatic episode.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-006 --> Both Laud and Strafford insist that
Parliament must be summoned, and the king summons it, after ten—eleven—years without doing so.

## 11. The Short Parliament and the Scottish Invasion

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00838–U-00950 -->

Parliament meets on 13 April 1640. The king attends the opening but does not deliver the speech,
partly because he has a slight stammer. Once, speaking to someone with the same defect, Charles
had said: I tried holding pebbles in my mouth, but it is no use. So the chancellor speaks for him. He praises the king for setting aside earlier clashes and summoning Parliament out of “a fatherly affection to his people,” and calls for “a sacred unity of hearts and affection in the service of him and of the commonwealth.”
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-008 --> But Parliament
is flooded with petitions. This is the political mechanism: county members arrive in London
bringing letters received from neighbors, communities, rich men, and nobles, all protesting
against something—Ship Money, monopolies, the king’s religious policy. The Commons begins
appointing committees, once again a laboratory of modernity. It says: we must examine these
petitions and protests, and if the king wants money he must abolish Ship Money—indeed no,
that is not enough—he must redress every grievance and hear every protest. You can imagine
Charles’s reaction.

On 5 May the king dissolves Parliament, which goes down in history as the
Short Parliament: two or three weeks. It is the fourth time in his reign that Charles dissolves
Parliament and the last time he succeeds. The Council immediately meets to decide how to fund this
damned Scottish war. Lord Strafford says that perhaps an army could be recruited in Ireland and
brought here to reduce this kingdom to obedience. At the time none of the listening ministers
pays much attention. Even today we do not know whether by “this kingdom” Strafford meant
Scotland, which must be reduced to obedience, or had something else in mind; we’ll come back to it.

Without Parliament there is no money. Charles is in such difficulty that he even contacts the
Pope to ask for a loan from Italy. He confiscates deposits London merchants keep in the Royal
Mint; you can imagine how popular that makes him. The City of London has had enough. When the king asks
the City merchants for loans, they refuse. The king—the procedures are these—has the London
city councillors arrested and threatens to hang them unless the money appears. But the money does not appear,
and recruitment goes terribly. The wildest rumours spread: the recruits will supposedly be put
aboard ships on the pretext of landing in Scotland, but actually sold as slaves in America. The
result? The king cannot assemble a real army. Meanwhile the Scots do, and on 20 August 1640 they
cross the Tweed and invade England to bagpipes and psalm-singing. The king receives yet another
petition, this time signed by many lords asking for pardons for those imprisoned for refusing new
taxes or the archbishop’s reforms and, most dramatically, saying: we hear Strafford proposed
bringing an army from Ireland; we beg Your Majesty not to do it.

Remember what Ireland means
to the English then: a savage country we conquered and are filling with Protestant immigrants,
whose local people are savages and papists, ferocious cut-throats thirsting for our blood. Somehow
the rumour spreads that Strafford suggested recruiting an army among these papist savages to
tame this kingdom. What exactly did he mean? And the king—I must say, there begins one of
the most dishonourable pages from the king’s point of view, showing him terribly inadequate
and uncertain. Seeing that public opinion regards Strafford, one of his most trusted advisers,
as his evil genius who gives him bad advice, Charles treats him coldly and suggests he leave
the country, saying he is not sure he can guarantee Strafford’s physical safety. Strafford
decides to remain. The king then gives his word that whatever happens, Strafford need not
fear for his life. Remember that.

Meanwhile the Protestant population of the Scottish-occupied
north sympathizes with the Scots and regards them as liberators. Penniless and without an army,
the king must sign a dishonourable agreement accepting Scottish occupation of northern England
and promising to pay the occupiers’ expenses. The only option left is to summon Parliament,
and he summons what will naturally go down in history as the Long Parliament.

## 12. The Long Parliament and Strafford's Fall

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00951–U-01066 -->

The election is dramatic because everyone knows clearly that the point of no return has been
reached and this Parliament must necessarily do great things. The king tries everything to
elect reliable men and fails. The Court party is defeated everywhere. Courtiers and royal
officials are calculated to form only eleven per cent of the Commons, less than in any other
Parliament. The election campaign’s Puritan slogan against royal candidates is: do not elect
these godless courtiers, these papist priests. Poorer voters—craftsmen and shopkeepers who
usually merely make up the numbers and vote as the local gentleman tells them—participate with
unusual commitment. This time radicals seek election on protest programs. As a lord who will
nevertheless side with Parliament says, “... yet I shall not easily suffer myself to appear in any popular assemblies where fellows without
shirts challenge as good a voice as myself” Puritan preachers
fan the flames and appeal to the people: “You that are of the meaner rank, common people,
be not discouraged, for God intends to make use of the common people in the great work of
proclaiming the kingdom of his dear Son.”

The air is charged with millenarian religious enthusiasm. Reading the news sheets, papers, and diaries of the days when the members arrive
is striking: it is the dawn of a fine day of long happiness for this kingdom; Parliament will
build a new world.

For the great majority of Commons members, gentlemen and merchants, this
does not mean social revolution. Absolutely not; almost nobody wants that. It means Parliament
must teach the king that Parliament commands, defend private property from royal taxes, and
eliminate the papist innovations beloved by the archbishop.

Parliament opens on 3 November
1640, and this time the king delivers the opening speech. Despite his stammer, he realizes he has to face Parliament himself and personally asks for money for war with Scotland. Parliament answers:
one moment, other matters must first be clarified. First, you cannot impose new taxes without
assembling Parliament. The king cannot manage this Parliament and his ministers cannot defend
him. He acknowledges that he indeed lacks this right.

But matters rapidly go further and assume
the features of a revolution. People say Parliament is Elizabeth’s true heir, as if James and
Charles never existed, and that the House of Commons—not the king—is invested by God. Within
weeks the Commons acts as the kingdom’s government, sending orders to county justices of the
peace. While they’re at it, they abolish press censorship, which already works poorly. Speaker
after speaker attacks the king and his ministers, and insists: what did Strafford mean by
recruiting an army in Ireland? A committee must investigate. In those days the Archbishop of
Canterbury records not a dream but another frightening event. In his study is a large portrait
of himself by Van Dyck. Entering, he finds it on the floor, fallen from the wall. He writes:
I am not superstitious, but God grant it is not an omen.

Meanwhile, remember William Prynne,
the Puritan printer whose ears were cut off, cheeks branded, and who was imprisoned for
life? Naturally he is triumphantly freed, and in his place Strafford is sent to the Tower by
Parliament’s order. A royal secretary’s son finds among the papers the minutes of the meeting
where Strafford spoke of an Irish army and delivers them to the opposition leaders. Ministers and
secretaries begin boarding ships and fleeing abroad. Meanwhile Puritan policy takes over. Catholic
priests formerly left alone are arrested and some hanged, drawn, and quartered, to the immense
satisfaction of the good Puritan people. The Commons passes extremist Puritan religious laws:
no games on Sunday, all holy images removed from churches, no kneeling during worship—those
papist grimaces. They throw the Archbishop of Canterbury into the Tower as well.

In March ’41
Strafford’s trial opens before Parliament. He is accused of many things, especially wanting
to recruit an Irish army to subjugate this kingdom, meaning us free Englishmen. The two-month trial grips the country. Contemporary chronicles say the courtroom is packed, people
camp there with bread, cheese, and beer, and supposedly relieve themselves on the spot rather
than lose their place.

Strafford manages to show that he meant an army to subdue Scotland, not
England. But English law allows Parliament to pass a special act declaring a person a guilty
criminal and condemning him to death without trial. The Commons passes such an act against
Strafford. It must pass the Lords and receive the king’s signature. The king declares he will
not sign. When it reaches the Lords, most wisely stay away. Few are present and a crowd rumbles
outside. The king has said he will not sign anyway, so the Lords wash their hands and pass it
too.

Then it reaches the king. He does not know what to do, consults lawyers and bishops, becomes
frightened, and signs—only to regret it immediately. Too late: two days later Strafford is
beheaded. Laud, imprisoned in the Tower, notes in his famous diary that serving that king has not been worthwhile. Up to here, as we approach the end, it seems like the French Revolution,
where every new royal concession pushes him closer to the abyss.

## 13. Polarization, Civil War, and the Limits of Revolution

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01067–U-01183 -->

But this time events go somewhat differently, because Parliament’s extremism, the violence
of the Puritans, and Strafford’s execution begin to frighten many people. Within Parliament a
change of direction appears. With every vote a stronger minority supports the king. The radical
momentum that risks becoming a revolution is frightening. We started these reforms believing we were correcting injustices and returning to Elizabeth’s good old days when everything went
well; now innovations appear that nobody wanted or at least expected.

As if that were not enough,
London has plague. Opposition leaders in the Commons receive abusive letters blaming them for
it; one receives an envelope containing what he immediately judges to be a plague-infected rag,
sent by an enemy. The king exploits this fear. He understands that more members are frightened
and can be drawn to his side. He warns Parliament that once common people realize the reforms were done by them but not for them, they may “set up for themselves, call parity and independence liberty” destroy “all rights and properties, all distinctions of families and merit” and reduce government to “a dark equal chaos of confusion.”
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-009 --> The king knows he is touching a
nerve because the members are all lords and gentlemen. He counts his minority at every vote,
and it grows.

Finally, in January ’42, he feels strong enough to force a showdown. He enters
Parliament with soldiers and a list of members to arrest. He does not find them. Parliament
resists, the wanted men escape, and the Speaker tells the king he is not there to obey him but the
Commons. The king leaves hostile London for the more loyal north and begins recruiting soldiers;
Parliament does likewise. He goes to Hull, near the Scottish border, where a large arms depot
was established for the Scottish war. The governor refuses him entry. The king orders his arrest
as a traitor—but who will arrest him? Impossible. The king tries to take Hull by force and is
repelled.

On 22 August Charles raises his standard at Nottingham, declaring war on Parliament’s
rebels. The country splits, and Parliament splits too. A little over half the members of the Commons remain
with Parliament, but two fifths go to the king. Most Lords support the king, but not all; about
a quarter support Parliament. The north is for the king, the south and London for Parliament;
the merchants and money are for Parliament.

Every analysis turns up one surprising fact: the young support the king. Royalist members and lords are on average much younger
than Parliamentarians. This is generally explained by saying that Charles’s absolutist project is not so anachronistic, but in keeping with the times. Young people who have known only that do not necessarily reject it; older people who remember a time when the king does not claim absolute power do.

Thus war begins amid a thousand fears: everyone wants victory, but fear of social revolution now fractures the reform coalition.
<!-- editorial-recommendation: ER-010 --> As an anti-royalist member writes: “And there
is no doubt that all right and property, must cease in a civil war, and we
know not what advantage the meaner sort also may take, who begin already to allege that there is no reason that
some should have so much and others so little.”

Parliament will win after six long years,
and the rabble will gain nothing. Six years of Civil War end where we began this episode, with the
king’s beheading at Whitehall. Laud precedes him by four years. Tried for treason in ’45,
he is prosecuted by none other than William Prynne, the man whose ears he had cut off. Prynne,
appointed public prosecutor by the Commons, does everything to convict the archbishop: rummages
through his papers, reads his diary, bribes witnesses. He cannot secure a conviction. No problem:
Parliament passes an act declaring the archbishop an enemy of the people and sentencing him
to death. He is beheaded four years before his king.

The people’s covenant, as the Scottish
poet called it, and social revolution will not happen. The idea that all men are equal remains
confined to a few extremists’ sermons. Some people do try. The Levellers seek political and legal equality; the Diggers seek common ownership. [N-007] They soon discover that Parliament’s army is entirely ready to suppress by
shooting and bloodshed those who have not understood that there comes a point when they have to stop.
