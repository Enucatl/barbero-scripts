# Structural outline

## Overview

Barbero explains the English Civil War by tracing three converging conflicts that Charles I failed to perceive: estrangement between court and country, a religious struggle over enforced Anglican conformity, and a constitutional-fiscal struggle over taxation without Parliament. Resistance in Scotland forced the king to recall Parliament, where reform became revolution, royal concessions destroyed confidence in the crown, and fear of social revolution then divided the reforming coalition. The lecture begins and ends with regicide, while stressing that Parliament's victory did not produce social equality.

## 1. Charles I's execution and the problem of civil war

- Transcript: U-00001–U-00055; original audio 02:50–06:32.
- The lecture opens at Whitehall on 30 January 1649, narrates Charles I's final speech and execution, and contrasts judicial regicide with earlier royal murders.
- This endpoint frames the causal question: how did a kingdom that prized peace reach six years of civil war?
- Quotations: Charles on liberty and government (Q-001); a witness on the crowd's groan (Q-002).
- Claims: execution circumstances and novelty of the trial (C-001); six-year-war framing (C-002).

## 2. Peace, court ritual, and political isolation

- Transcript: U-00056–U-00111; original audio 06:38–10:36.
- The 1637 masque *The Triumph of Peace* and Charles's claim to be Christendom's happiest king embody an illusory calm amid the Thirty Years' War.
- Court etiquette, royal-touching ritual, and limited access isolate the king from public feeling.
- Claims: England's position in 1637 and the masque (C-003); court ritual and the royal touch (C-004).

## 3. James I, Buckingham, and the discredited court

- Transcript: U-00112–U-00179; original audio 10:37–14:47.
- James I's accession, Buckingham's favour and patronage, and rumours about sexuality and drunkenness damage the court's reputation; Charles's marriage to the Catholic Henrietta Maria deepens alienation.
- Lawrence Stone's antithetical portrait summarizes the imagined divide between virtuous country and corrupt court.
- Quotations: James to Buckingham (Q-003); Stone's country/court catalogue (Q-004).
- Claims: Buckingham's position and relationship with James (C-005); anti-court and anti-Catholic opinion (C-006).

## 4. Anglican authority and Puritan dissent

- Transcript: U-00180–U-00246; original audio 14:50–19:25.
- Literacy, print, lay Bible reading, and hostility to episcopal hierarchy nourish Puritan dissent within an officially Protestant but ceremonially conservative church.
- Barbero balances Puritan resistance to authority against its religious intolerance and hostility to recreation.
- Quotations: Hobbes on vernacular Bible readers believing they spoke with God (Q-005); *tamquam excrementa mundi* (Q-006).
- Claims: Anglican structure and dissent (C-007); Puritan beliefs and social programme (C-008).

## 5. Laud's campaign for conformity

- Transcript: U-00247–U-00341; original audio 19:28–24:31.
- Charles and Archbishop William Laud enforce episcopal authority, ceremony, catechism, and clerical discipline; ministers are removed and Puritans emigrate to New England.
- Laud's dream diary supplies an ironic glimpse of suppressed anxiety.
- Quotations: Laud's dream and interpretation (Q-007).
- Claims: Laudian reforms and punishments (C-009); scale and causes of Puritan migration (C-010).

## 6. Print, Prynne, and fear of popery

- Transcript: U-00342–U-00457; original audio 24:33–31:35.
- Weak censorship permits clandestine polemic. William Prynne's prosecutions, mutilation, imprisonment, renewed writing, and public support become an emblem of persecution.
- Catholic worship around the queen, leniency toward priests, and alliance with Spain feed false but politically potent beliefs that Charles is secretly papist.
- Claims: Prynne's prosecutions (C-011); legal status and practical treatment of Catholics (C-012); political consequences of anti-popery (C-013).

## 7. Parliament, divine-right monarchy, and fiscal expedients

- Transcript: U-00458–U-00600; original audio 31:42–40:58.
- Medieval consent traditions and the representative strength of the Commons clash with Charles's divine-right education and his Personal Rule after 1629.
- Unable to secure parliamentary taxation, the crown raises money through monopolies, licenses, fines, and intrusive regulation; the soap monopoly illustrates popular resentment.
- Quotations: James on kings sitting on God's throne (Q-008); contemporary complaint about poor London tradespeople (Q-009).
- Claims: parliamentary consent and Magna Carta framing (C-014); Personal Rule (C-015); fiscal devices and soap monopoly (C-016).

## 8. Ship Money and property

- Transcript: U-00601–U-00658; original audio 41:01–44:40.
- Charles extends Ship Money inland in 1635. John Hampden's test case divides the judges and turns taxation into a defence of property against prerogative.
- Barbero interprets the coming revolution as bourgeois in its protection of property rather than social levelling.
- Quotation/slogan: *rex is lex* (Q-010).
- Claims: Ship Money chronology and Hampden judgment (C-017); bourgeois-revolution interpretation (C-018).

## 9. Scotland, the Prayer Book, and the Covenant

- Transcript: U-00659–U-00761; original audio 44:53–50:35.
- Imposition of the English Prayer Book produces riots, petitions, and the National Covenant; Scottish resistance joins Calvinist conviction, national autonomy, print mobilization, and millenarian enthusiasm.
- Quotations: Scottish lord on the 'whore of Babylon' (Q-011); Edinburgh lawyer on the nation's marriage with God (Q-012).
- Claims: Prayer Book riot and government retreat (C-019); Covenant mobilization (C-020).

## 10. Covenanters and the First Bishops' War

- Transcript: U-00762–U-00837; original audio 50:39–55:10.
- Covenanter victory produces coercion and elite fear that popular mobilization may become social revolution.
- Charles declares rebellion but lacks money and military strength; the inconclusive First Bishops' War forces him toward Parliament.
- Quotation: William Drummond's warning of a *bellum servile* (Q-013).
- Claims: Covenanter coercion (C-021); First Bishops' War and royal weakness (C-022).

## 11. Short Parliament and Scottish invasion

- Transcript: U-00838–U-00950; original audio 55:14–61:38.
- The Short Parliament refuses supply before redress and is dissolved. Charles's failed financing and recruitment end with Scottish invasion, occupation, and the recall of the Long Parliament.
- Strafford's ambiguous proposal for an Irish army becomes politically fatal.
- Quotation: the chancellor's praise of king and queen at the Short Parliament (Q-014).
- Claims: Short Parliament chronology (C-023); Second Bishops' War and Treaty settlement (C-024); Strafford's council words (C-025).

## 12. Long Parliament and Strafford's fall

- Transcript: U-00951–U-01066; original audio 61:41–70:04.
- An unusually mobilized election produces a hostile Parliament. The Commons dismantles royal government, frees victims, imprisons Laud and Strafford, and promotes Puritan reform.
- Strafford defeats the legal case but is condemned by attainder; Charles breaks his promise and signs the death warrant.
- Quotations: complaint about voters 'without a shirt' (Q-015); Puritan appeal to the common people (Q-016).
- Claims: election and parliamentary radicalization (C-026); Strafford's trial, attainder, and execution (C-027).

## 13. Polarization, civil war, and limits of revolution

- Transcript: U-01067–U-01183; original audio 70:07–77:19.
- Fear of Puritan and social revolution rebuilds a royal party. Charles's failed arrest of five members, departure from London, and attempt on Hull precede the raising of his standard at Nottingham.
- Parliament, elites, regions, and generations divide. Parliament ultimately wins and executes king and archbishop while suppressing Leveller challenges to property.
- Quotations: royal warning about liberty, equality, and independence (Q-017); parliamentary ally's fear that the poor will attack property (Q-018).
- Claims: road to war and alignment (C-028); age pattern among royalists (C-029); Laud, regicide, Levellers, and repression (C-030).
