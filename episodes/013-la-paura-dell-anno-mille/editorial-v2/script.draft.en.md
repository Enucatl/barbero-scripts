# The Fear of the Year 1000: Unbelievable Middle Ages

## 1. The familiar terror and the precise claim

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00069 -->

Here we are at the final episode of Unbelievable Middle Ages, looking at things we thought we knew about the Middle Ages that turn out not to be true.

We all have a picture of this period in our heads, full of scenes and stories. And yet, much of that picture was invented later. I'm not telling you Charlemagne never existed. But look into the things we take for granted about the Middle Ages, and many turn out to have more to do with legend than with reality.

Our subject this time is perhaps the least sensational of the three. When I started telling friends what I was planning to talk about, I'd say, “You know, the right of the first night, or the idea of a flat earth—it isn't necessarily true that people in the Middle Ages…” And they looked quite surprised.

The fears of the year 1000 are a little more out of fashion. Perhaps not everyone still believes in them. Even online, if you search for the fears or terrors of the year 1000, most of the sites you find talk about the *legend* of those fears. So perhaps I'm preaching to the converted. Still, I think it's interesting to see how an image of the Middle Ages was put together, an image that turns out to be entirely legendary, entirely invented, and that learned people believed for a very long time. And did they ever believe it.

<!-- C-001 -->

Since this is an old story, and perhaps a slightly unfashionable one, let me remind you exactly what we're talking about. “The terrors of the year 1000” doesn't just mean that, from time to time in the Middle Ages, movements appeared announcing that the world was about to end. Nor does it simply mean that medieval Christians cared about when the end would come. Nobody's disputing that.

It means something much more specific: the idea that medieval people believed the world would end in the year 1000.

Now, it's one thing to predict this back in the year 750. But as 1000 gets closer, according to the story, people begin to get seriously frightened. In the last few years, the world almost stops. What's the point of making an effort, starting a family, working? The world's going to end in three years. In two years. Next year.

And in the last months of 999, the world really does stop. That's the story told by so many learned writers in the nineteenth century, and even in the twentieth. People crowd into churches, praying and weeping, hoping to save themselves. Because as soon as the year 999 is over, the world will be over too.

I've put that in my own words. But I want to show you how deeply this idea took hold, especially in nineteenth-century culture, and how powerfully it appealed to the Romantic imagination: a whole world bowed down by terror, waiting for the end.

Nothing has happened.

Wonderful, of course. Carducci is a great writer. But my colleagues, the nineteenth-century historians, wrote this sort of thing too. Well, we've established one thing, at least. In the nineteenth century, everyone was convinced that there had been this collective terror, and that it had brought European society to a halt.

## 3. What people actually planned in 999

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00130–U-00240 -->

Can we check? Can we find out what people were actually doing in 999?

With a little work, yes. We can look for documents written that year. What, for example, is the pope doing at the end of 999? You'd think the end of the world would interest him.

<!-- Q-005; Q-006; Q-007; C-003 -->

And, as luck would have it, we have a papal bull dated December 31, 999. On that day, Pope Sylvester II confirms the privileges of a great German monastery, Fulda. The abbot has asked Rome to confirm various privileges, and the pope hands him this document, dated December 31, 999. In effect, he tells him:

“I confirm all your monastery's privileges, for you and your successors, on condition that every future abbot, once elected, comes to Rome to ask for confirmation of his election. What's more, in exchange for these privileges, you monks of Fulda must make an annual payment of twelve denarii to Rome. Every year, from now on.”

At least these two aren't expecting the end of the world, apparently.

<!-- C-004 -->

True, Sylvester II is an unusual man. A great scholar. So learned that some people even suspect he's been dabbling in magic. Before becoming pope, he was called Gerbert of Aurillac. Perhaps he's a special case. Perhaps the less sophisticated, ordinary people really are expecting the end.

Let's see what ordinary people are doing in 999. I'll pick one example from the documents I found. I looked in Piedmont; that was more convenient for me.

<!-- C-005 -->

We're in Tortona in 999. We don't know the month. Two brothers—Adalberto, a priest, and Erenzone, a layman—are going into business on a small scale. They rent houses and farmland from the monastery of San Marziano in Tortona.

<!-- C-006 -->

They take out a written lease. In 999, that isn't something people do very often in some parts of Europe. In plenty of places, things are more informal. You want a contract? Spit in your palm, shake hands. In Italy, closer to its classical roots, people are more likely to go to a notary, even for a lease. They draw up what they call a “little book,” a *libellus* in Latin. And we have this lease.

The abbot rents the land to the two brothers for a period of…

Let me prepare you for this. Until fairly recently, sharecropping was common in the Italian countryside. On the face of it, the arrangement looks like an equal partnership. In practice, it heavily favors the landowner. The contract normally lasts one year. At the end of the harvest, at Martinmas, the tenant settles up, and if the owner wants to throw him out, he can. Think of Ermanno Olmi's film *The Tree of Wooden Clogs*, which some of you may have seen.

In the Middle Ages, things aren't like that. There aren't many hands to work the land. One of a landowner's great problems is finding people who will stay. That's especially true for monks, who have other things to think about besides constantly looking after their fields. If anything, you'd like to make the workers stay, if you can, rather than throw them out. So medieval leases tend to run for a long time.

<!-- Q-008; C-007 -->

In 999, the abbot of San Marziano rents this land to the two brothers for twenty-nine years. They must live there, cultivate it, and keep it from falling into ruin. Every year, they'll pay the monastery one-third of the grain they harvest and half the wine.

The owner is happy to have them there for twenty-nine years. And they're happy too. It gives them some real security.

<!-- Q-009 -->

And the guarantee is written into the contract. In the abbot's words, as I'm paraphrasing them: “We give you this land for twenty-nine years. If, during those twenty-nine years, one of my successors as abbot tries to take it back, he'll pay the penalty laid down in the contract: twenty silver soldi.”

So these people, too, are making arrangements in 999 without expecting the world to end the following year.

Does absolutely nobody mention the end of the world in 999? Eventually, I did find one document that mentions it. But we have to see how.

<!-- C-008 -->

It's a diploma, an official charter, issued by Emperor Otto III. That year, Otto has gone to Rome to attend to various matters. He arrives, and naturally petitioners crowd around him, asking for donations, confirmations, charters, grants, and so on. Among them are the monks of Farfa.

Farfa is a great monastery in the region around Rome. Naturally, it has privileges too. It's an imperial monastery: it belongs to the emperor. But the monks have a privilege that prevents the emperor from giving it away to somebody else. Emperors are quite fond of doing that. You have an old adviser who deserves a reward, an important man who's ready to retire. What do you do with him? Make him abbot of a monastery. Off he goes, to enjoy its riches.

Monks aren't very enthusiastic about these arrangements.

The monks of Farfa have a privilege protecting them from being handed over to anyone in that way. They elect their own abbot. And Emperor Otto III says, “Very well. Agreed. We'll grant it.”

<!-- Q-010; C-009 -->

“We confirm this privilege,” the emperor says on October 3, 999. I'm translating his Latin loosely: “From now on, forever, none of our successors may grant the monastery of Farfa to anyone as a benefice. It must always remain the property of the state.” The *res publica*.

<!-- Q-011 -->

“If any future pope, or any emperor who succeeds us, should instead subject the monastery to someone else, let him remember that when Christ comes, and we all have to give an account of ourselves, he will have to answer for breaking my promise.”

Because Christ will come. The world will end. And we will all have to answer for ourselves.

Sooner or later.

But clearly, the emperor isn't expecting the world to end next year either. Is there another way to check?

## 4. Chronicles, Revelation, and millenarian reasoning

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00241–U-00334 -->

<!-- C-010 -->

Yes. There are chroniclers. Of course there are. Scholars—usually churchmen or monks in this period—who sit down and record the great events they witness. A few lines each year, or perhaps more.

Several chroniclers live through the year 1000. They write: in 998, this happened; in 999, that happened; in 1000, something else. The emperor went to Rome; in 1001, he returned to Germany, and so on. Nobody says there was terror at the approach of the year 1000, with crowds gathering and penitential movements. Nothing.

Does that mean medieval people were so relaxed and secular that they didn't care about the end of the world? Obviously not. We've just seen that they cared. The question is when it will happen. Is there any way of knowing?

<!-- C-011 -->

Let's go back a little. Carducci gave us “A thousand, and no more than a thousand.” He referred to Revelation. What did medieval people know about the end of the world?

Everything. Except the date.

They know everything because the Book of Revelation describes exactly how it will happen. Revelation is the last book in the New Testament. It's attributed to Saint John the Evangelist, though that probably isn't right. At any rate, it's an ancient text, from the end of the first century. And, it has to be said, it's utterly delirious. Visionary. It describes the most extraordinary things and presents them, taken together, as an account of the end of the world.

For medieval churchmen, that's very important. And useful, too. Imagine a chronicler who wants to write the history of the world. He goes to the library and starts with the ancient historians. The Greeks, the Romans, the Carthaginians, Hannibal, and so on. Constantine, the great Christian emperor. The barbarians. Charlemagne. And now we've reached the present.

“I'll leave a few pages blank. And if I want, I can already put in the last part, because we know how the world will end.”

All he has to do is get out Revelation. The Antichrist will come and commit every kind of atrocity. Then he'll be defeated. Christ will return. There we are.

As I said, Revelation is a visionary, tangled book. You can find just about anything in it. At one point, its author starts playing with the number one thousand. It's impossible to make sense of it. And you don't have to take my word for that; I'll read it to you. But clearly, the year 1000—or rather, the number one thousand—has something to do with it.

<!-- Q-012 -->

Here is the opening of Revelation, chapter twenty:

“Then I saw an angel coming down from heaven, with the key to the abyss and a great chain in his hand. He seized the dragon, the ancient serpent, who is the Devil and Satan, and bound him for a thousand years. Then he threw him into the abyss, shut it, and sealed it over him, so that he could no longer deceive the nations until the thousand years were complete.”

<!-- Q-013 -->

You read that and think, “What can it mean?” It seems to say that, at some point, Satan is rendered powerless for a thousand years. Why? Because Christ has come. Of course: it must mean a thousand years from Christ's coming. But then it says that after those thousand years, Satan is released. And shortly afterward comes the end of the world.

That's one possible line of reasoning. So possible, if you'll allow me a brief digression, that people still follow it today. We don't necessarily know any more about these things than people did in the Middle Ages. Every so often, a sect appears and announces that the world is about to end. Admittedly, that happens more readily in Protestant Texas than here in Italy. But it still happens. The followers give all their property to the leader, and sometimes they even commit suicide together.

What do we call these movements, these sects? Millenarian movements. Millenarian sects. We speak of millenarianism when people preach that the end of the world is approaching. We still use that word because we're steeped in the ideas in Revelation, with its association between a thousand years and the end of the world.

So, as the year 1000 approaches, does anyone actually make that calculation? We've just seen people making plans for the future in 999. But in the preceding decades, does anyone read Revelation this way and start telling people, “Get ready. The world is going to end in the year 1000”?

## 5. Abbone’s memories of failed predictions

Yes, someone does. We have a specific account. And the person telling it is a monk, for a change. At this time, almost everybody who feels like telling stories is a monk.

<!-- C-012 -->

His name is Abbo, abbot of Fleury in France. A great scholar and writer, a man who's read widely and understands a great deal. Around 994, he writes a short book addressed to the French kings Hugh Capet and Robert.

<!-- Q-014 -->

What Abbo tells King Robert, in effect, is this: “There are quite a few things wrong in your kingdom. Things that ought to be corrected.”

He isn't talking about counts pocketing bribes instead of administering justice. He's talking about beliefs, learning, the way the Church works. “There are a lot of errors in your kingdom that need correcting.”

<!-- Q-015 -->

<!-- Q-016 -->

Others make mistakes working out the church calendar. They start Advent a day early. That won't do either, says Abbo. The king has to take action.

And then Abbo says that, in the past, these stories about the end of the world used to crop up in the kingdom. Not so much lately—we're around 994—but in the past, from time to time, and people had to work quite hard to calm everybody down.

<!-- Q-017; C-013 -->

“I remember when I was a boy,” Abbo says. The date is reckoned to be around 970. “When I was a boy, I myself heard a preacher in a church in Paris warning people that the world would end in the year 1000.”

And Abbo, already well read even as a boy, remembers trying to silence him. He tries to explain to everyone that the man is wrong, citing Revelation, the Book of Daniel, the Gospels, and so on.

<!-- Q-019 -->

Then, he says, there was that other mistake about the end of the world. “I remember, in the days of good Abbot Richard…” We're still around 970, more or less. Letters arrive at the monastery saying that somebody in Lorraine has made an absurd calculation.

Forgive me for dragging you through all these complications. But these people live and breathe this kind of reasoning, these fine distinctions.

Somebody has announced that, in a year when Good Friday falls on the Feast of the Annunciation, March 25, the world will end.

Why would that make sense to them? Well, it would bring everything full circle. The Annunciation marks Jesus's coming into the world—the greatest event in all history, in their eyes. Good Friday marks his death. If those two dates coincide…

We human beings can get the wildest ideas into our heads and believe them. And around 970, somebody is going around telling people to watch out.

Naturally, I looked it up in a perpetual calendar. When had this coincidence last happened? In 908. Quite a while before. When does Good Friday fall on the Annunciation again? In 970. There you are. Presumably, somebody made that calculation the year before.

<!-- Q-020 -->

But here's the interesting part. Abbo tells us that when the news arrives, good Abbot Richard says to him, in effect, “Abbo, you deal with this. Write to Lorraine and explain that it's all bullshit.”

He doesn't use quite that technical expression. But that's clearly what he means. And Abbo says, “I wrote back, explaining how crazy this reasoning was.”

<!-- C-014 -->

So, where does that leave us? It's established that the idea of an approaching end of the world circulates from time to time. Connecting it with the year 1000 is perfectly possible too. Somebody did think of it. But whenever these ideas crop up, they're immediately silenced. They have no chance to spread or circulate, because the shepherds of the Church step in at once and stop this kind of reasoning. Around 994, when Abbo writes, these are memories of his youth.

## 6. Why churchmen rejected prediction

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00418–U-00503 -->

Why does Abbot Richard tell Abbo, “It's all nonsense. You write back”? Why are churchmen so sure these calculations don't work? Why is it wrong to try to predict when the world will end?

<!-- C-015 -->

That's precisely the point. It goes beyond whether the arithmetic works. Medieval churchmen know that trying to predict the end of the world—the Last Judgment—is wrong, because the Bible says you cannot know.

<!-- Q-021 -->

Matthew, chapter twenty-four, verse thirty-six: “But about that day and hour, no one knows.”

<!-- Q-022 -->

Saint Paul, in his First Letter to the Thessalonians: “The day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.”

A thief doesn't tell you he's coming. He shows up when you aren't expecting him.

<!-- Q-023 -->

“When they say”—this is still Paul—“‘Peace and security,’ sudden destruction will come upon them.”

<!-- Q-024 -->

So the Lord's message is clear. To put it in a few words: “I will come, but you can't know when. In fact, I'll come when you least expect me.” No one knows that day or that hour. Trying to calculate it is pointless.

One person who makes the point very clearly is an early Christian thinker who will have an enormous influence on medieval Christianity: Saint Augustine. He thinks it through and puts it very plainly.

<!-- Q-025 -->

“Calculating the times to discover when this world will end or the Lord will come seems to me to be nothing other than wanting to know what he himself said no one can know.”

And Augustine, who knows his texts, has another small point to make.

<!-- Q-026 -->

“All right,” you might say, “we don't know when he's coming. But perhaps he'll come next year.”

Hold on. It's also written that, before the Antichrist comes and then the world ends, the Gospel must be preached to all the peoples of the earth. Has it been preached to everybody yet?

No.

So it won't be next year.

<!-- C-016 -->

Let's take stock again. Throughout the Middle Ages, millenarian tensions, anxieties about the end of the world, do appear from time to time. But they have no room to spread, because the people at the top won't accept them. Let me give you an example. These things can happen quite often, but they remain contained.

<!-- Q-027; C-017 -->

We'll go back well before the year 1000, to 847. We're in Germany. An annalist tells us that a woman appears in Mainz, claiming she can predict the future. A *pseudo-prophetissa*, a false prophetess. Her name is Thiota.

She says she knows the date of the end of the world. And, as it happens, it'll be next year. She claims to know this thing that nobody can know, the monk says, as though God had revealed it to her.

<!-- Q-028 -->

And the people—actually, the monk uses a revealing word: the “common people.” Because, in his view, only the poor fools at the bottom could believe such things. But there are plenty of them. Thiota goes around saying she knows the world will end next year, and many of these common people believe her. They come to her, bringing gifts, asking her to pray for them.

<!-- Q-029 -->

The bishop of Mainz gets a little annoyed. He summons her and questions her. And it emerges—or at least this is what she says—that a priest friend suggested the whole thing. You can put his advice like this: “Go around preaching that the world will end next year. Just see how many presents people bring us.”

And that's exactly what happens.

<!-- Q-030 -->

The bishop consults the other bishops. They decide she's in for a good beating. They have her flogged in the public square. Then it's, “Out of here. And don't let us hear another word from you.”

And that, you might say, puts an end to the millenarian movement in Mainz in 847.

## 7. A legend accumulates a line at a time

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00504–U-00620 -->

How much of our time have I used up? A good deal, fortunately for you. But remember where we started. We began with writers of real stature: Carducci, respected and authoritative historians of their day. They told us about collective terror in the last months and days before the year 1000, about churches packed with frightened people. I don't need to go through it again.

By now, it seems established that this picture is entirely imaginary. Invented. It didn't happen that way.

But that leaves the historian with a different question, and quite an interesting one. If it never happened, who invented the story? Who, when, why? How is it possible?

There's something rather distressing about discovering that we historians can believe and repeat these things. We can, unfortunately, convince ourselves of something, then convince our readers, only for someone a hundred years later to discover that it's entirely made up. That's not a pleasant thing to realize.

The remedy is to find out how it happened. If we understand how these things happen, perhaps we can be a little more careful. Perhaps.

A number of scholars have tried to trace the story back to its origins. And you really can follow it: one author copies another, each adding a little something. That's how it works. Sometimes it takes centuries.

While preparing this, I spent quite a while wondering whether to give you the whole list or just a few examples.

<!-- Q-031; C-018 -->

The first culprit is a medieval chronicler, a monk writing around 1100, a hundred years after the events. He's a good chronicler. His name is Sigebert of Gembloux. He's read a great many books, including the annalists who lived around the year 1000 and said nothing at all about these fears.

But one of those chroniclers does mention an earthquake in 1000. And an earthquake is serious. It's a sign. It means something.

Then Sigebert reads in his sources that there was a comet in 1002. A comet is serious too. God doesn't go to the trouble of disturbing the harmony of the stars unless he has a very definite reason to send humanity a warning.

Sigebert puts two and two together. And while he's at it, he simplifies things a little. Earthquake in 1000. Comet in 1002. “Well, the year 1000 is involved. Obviously, it was rather a special year.”

He puts it all under 1000: an earthquake, a comet, and a serpent appearing in the sky.

To Sigebert's credit, he doesn't say, “And everybody was frightened that the world would end.” He simply piles up the omens. The number one thousand clearly matters to the way he arranges the story. The year 1000 has passed, but that round number still has its power.

<!-- Q-032; C-019 -->

Another seventy years go by. Around 1170, another chronicler, a Cistercian named Guillaume Godel, has also been reading the chroniclers of the year 1000 and the years that follow. He discovers that in 1010 there were visions, famine, eclipses. Plenty to work with.

Godel writes that all these omens happened in 1010, and that many people were afraid the world would end.

That last part wasn't in his sources. The people who lived through 1010 told him there had been an eclipse. But Guillaume Godel isn't a fool. He knows what people think when they see an eclipse. So there we have the first writer to mention collective terror. Unfortunately for us, he puts it in 1010. Exasperating, but there it is.

The Middle Ages continue. And throughout this period, which Carducci and his friends picture as an age of ignorance and credulity, nobody else thinks to pick up that little sentence about the terrors.

Then, at last, comes the Renaissance.

<!-- Q-033; C-020 -->

In that enlightened age, historians start appearing who *do* believe that people were terrified the world would end. Take Trithemius. Most of us, quite reasonably, have never heard of him, but he's an important German humanist writing in the early sixteenth century.

He too has read Sigebert of Gembloux. So he too writes in his chronicle that in the year 1000 there was an earthquake, a comet, visions—and that many people were frightened, fearing the arrival of the last day.

He didn't read that last part in Sigebert. But you can see how it works. He's a humanist. It's the Renaissance now. Those poor people back then were living in the darkness of the Middle Ages. After seeing all those omens, of course they were frightened. How could they not be? And Trithemius writes it down.

It keeps going like that. Author after author repeats the story, each embellishing it a little. Because it's such a compelling scene to imagine: an entire world brought to a standstill by terror.

I realize that, put like this, it sounds incredible. We have this stubborn belief that if we read something, its author must know what he's talking about. So it must be true.

It doesn't work that way at all. People can write the most astonishing things, and perhaps convince themselves too. But if you ask them how they know… Well, let's leave that there, or I'll go off on a tangent.

In any case, it becomes common knowledge. In the seventeenth century, the eighteenth, the nineteenth, everybody knows there were terrors in the year 1000.

## 8. A national culture needs its beginning

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00621–U-00724 -->

<!-- C-021 -->

These writers have a reason to believe the story. And you can sympathize with them, poor souls. Especially in the eighteenth century, when a modern, scholarly way of writing history begins to develop. People start reconstructing the past by comparing documents, instead of simply copying the old chroniclers.

They also begin writing histories of their nations and their literatures. In the picture these historians are building, it all begins in the Middle Ages. But before the year 1000, or after?

After, obviously. Think of the history of Italian literature we learn at school in Italy. It all begins after 1000. Before that, nothing.

So these early scholars, putting together their great surveys, find that the Middle Ages up to about 1000 have left relatively little evidence. Not much seems to happen. Afterward, everything bursts into life. Provençal literature, French literature in the *langue d'oïl*, then Italian literature. The troubadours, all of it. The great cathedrals. A great civilization coming into being after the year 1000.

And all these scholars know that, in 1000, the world stopped in terror. It takes just one little step to make it all fit. The year 1000 becomes the starting point. Obviously, until then, people were too frightened to do anything. Afterward, they got moving.

Put like that, it sounds like a joke. But people argued it very seriously.

<!-- C-022 -->

Let me quote a book that matters enormously in the history of Italian culture. Toward the end of the eighteenth century, Abbot Bettinelli publishes an early, sweeping history of Italian literature and culture. Bettinelli is no minor figure. His book is extremely important. You might even call it prophetic. Published in 1773, its title is *Risorgimento d'Italia*—the revival of Italy—*in Learning, the Arts, and Customs After the Year One Thousand*.

So how does the book begin? By explaining how, fortunately, the world started moving again. Especially Italy, which had so many wonderful things to do, but until the year 1000 nobody would stir.

<!-- Q-034 -->

“After the year one thousand, people returned to some extent to the arts, which had previously been neglected because of a universal belief that the end of the tenth century would also be the end of the world.”

Bettinelli even scolds other historians for saying so little about all this. It's tremendously important, he tells them. Those fears kept Italian literature from getting started—and that's what interests him—along with everything else.

<!-- Q-035 -->

“It's hard to believe how much harm that belief did”—the belief that the world was about to end—“right up to the last day of the tenth century, and how much harm the unexpected beginning of the year one thousand removed.” In other words, how much good it did.

<!-- Q-036; Q-037 -->

“The ever-present horror of an approaching universal devastation robbed everyone of hope.” “This dreadful despair must have left room for no other thoughts…” And so on, and so on, piling on the Romantic drama.

Then the sun rises on the year 1000. But we've heard this story already. It's Carducci. That's where I began. What I'm reading now is Bettinelli, a century earlier.

And remember where Carducci writes his version? I told you earlier, but you've forgotten. In his first discourse on the development of the nation's literature.

There's the connection. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, a writer setting out to tell the story of Italian greatness, of Italian civilization, finds nothing before 1000. So he starts with the terrors. The same thing happens in France and in other countries, each in its own way.

Well, we're nearly there.

## 9. Ideology can produce the wrong history

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00725–U-00807 -->

We've made good progress, I'd say. There's one more aspect I'd like to emphasize. It may make this business feel a little more relevant than all the talk about twelfth-century monks I've inflicted on you.

<!-- C-023 -->

One reason nineteenth-century writers are so convinced about the terrors of the year 1000 is that they're fighting very specific ideological battles.

Carducci, who gives the legend such a powerful, romantic form, is also the poet who writes the *Hymn to Satan*. He rails against the Vatican, against the reactionary Church of his day, the enemy of progress. He attacks the pope, sitting in the Vatican, still playing king and cutting off his opponents' heads.

Among the many battles of the nineteenth century is a struggle by secular, progressive, scientific, positivist culture against superstition. Many of those fighting for a secular and progressive culture see the Church as an enemy. And not without reason.

Let me make a brief digression, because we have to put these things in context. If nineteenth-century anticlericalism is so fierce, that's partly because the Church of the time does everything it can to make itself look like an enemy. It's frightened of progress. We can say these things quite openly; the Church of today has amply acknowledged them.

The nineteenth-century Church is terrified of progress, terrified of liberalism, terrified of science. It fights modernism in all its forms. It excommunicates. It shuts doors. Naturally, people on the other side see it as the enemy of enlightenment.

And they reason like this. If the Church and the Christian religion generate superstition and ignorance now, how much worse must it have been in that horrible, distant age we've fortunately escaped, when the Church ruled the world? The Middle Ages.

That's how Carducci and so many others think, Michelet in France among them. Back then, darkness must really have reigned. No wonder people were so ignorant, so gripped by fear. No wonder the world stopped in terror as the year 1000 approached.

But there are fighters on the other side too. Churchmen study, write, and publish to argue that none of this is true. The Church didn't promote ignorance and superstition. Quite the opposite.

And they're the first to ask: “These terrors of the year 1000 that you're all so sure about—did they actually happen? Why don't we check? Why don't we look at what the chroniclers of those years were writing?”

In other words, more or less what I've been doing here. The people who first ask these questions aren't simply scholars driven by a love of truth. They're also combatants.

In France, the first article on the “supposed terrors of the year 1000” appears in 1873. Its author is Dom Plaine, a priest and scholar. For him, showing that the terrors never happened is more than a historical correction. It means proving the secularists, the positivist scientists, wrong when they describe the Church as a promoter of darkness and ignorance.

I have to confess, I find this rather embarrassing. I've said it already, and I'll say it again: given what was happening at the time, I think the priest-bashers were right and the Church was wrong. The Church really was fighting the wrong battles. Against everything modern: liberalism, democracy, the unification of Italy, all sorts of things. In my view, the other side was right.

But to prove they were right, they produced bad history. They constructed a legend and believed it.

Meanwhile, the priests of Pope Pius IX, defending perhaps the most reactionary and obscurantist Vatican of the last few centuries, got this historical question right. They did good historical work. They went looking, and they found the truth.

There's one last thing I'd like to tell you, which takes this a little further and brings it closer to our own time.

## 10. Modern polemics and the survival of myths

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00808–U-00907 -->

Strange as it may seem, this argument still isn't over. At least in France, where the ideological divisions differ from Italy's but run deep—especially the divide between the secular culture of the Republic and Catholic culture. People are still arguing about the terrors of the year 1000.

<!-- C-024 -->

A book published in 1999 makes its point right in the title: *The False Terrors of the Year 1000*. Its author is Sylvain Gouguenheim, very well known in France, though his work has not made the same impact in Italy. It is a very combative book.

<!-- Q-038; C-025 -->

His argument, in essence, runs like this. Yes, the terrors of the year 1000 never happened; everyone knows that now. But that isn't enough. We have to do more to dispel the legend. Look at the great French historians of the twentieth century, Duby and the others. When they wrote about the year 1000, of course they acknowledged that the terrors were a legend. But they insisted that millenarianism was still there. There were movements, an atmosphere heavy with unease.

So Gouguenheim takes on some of the towering figures of twentieth-century historical scholarship, Georges Duby among them. Great masters, certainly, he says in effect, but they've created a false impression all over again. They've brought back the picture of the year 1000 as a dark, anxious, troubled age. And, he argues, it isn't true.

Why is this interesting? Because I see a militant defense of Christian culture in Gouguenheim’s work. And the book that made him famous in France wasn’t the one about the terrors of the year 1000.

<!-- C-026 -->

It was a book published in 2008, called *Aristotle at Mont Saint-Michel*. You know Mont Saint-Michel, the famous abbey in Normandy, standing out in the sea, cut off from the mainland by the tide.

<!-- Q-039 -->

In that book, Gouguenheim makes a claim. And I say “claim” because the book caused furious arguments. Eminent scholars called it rubbish. Other eminent scholars said it was very interesting. I haven't read the whole book yet, so I'm only telling you what I know about it in outline.

Gouguenheim argues that, in the Middle Ages, Mont Saint-Michel was an important center for translating Aristotle directly from Greek into Latin.

<!-- Q-040; C-027 -->

I think you can already see the implications. But Gouguenheim spells them out in his book. Those pages I have seen. I went and looked at them. What he's saying, as I'm summarizing it, is this: the story we've always been told, that we owe our access to Greek philosophy and science to the Arabs, isn't true. We Christians translated them ourselves, directly from Greek. We had no need of the Arabs.

And, to make things even clearer, at the end of the book his argument, as I'm putting it here, is that Arabic is completely unsuited to discussing scientific subjects, that the Arabs don't have the mentality of a scientific people, and that it was therefore absurd to imagine we owed them anything. We owe nothing to medieval Islamic culture.

You can see that this is just a tiny bit provocative. And it did indeed set off furious debates in France.

<!-- Q-041; C-028 -->

What strikes me is that the same scholar who writes *Aristotle at Mont Saint-Michel* had already written the earlier book to remind us, in effect: the terrors of the year 1000 never happened. The secularists have muddled our thinking again. There's too much talk about medieval millenarianism. None of it is true.

I'm not taking sides here. The terrors never happened; that's perfectly clear. What interests me is how myths take shape and become so deeply rooted.

Perhaps a myth starts quite innocently. Someone adds a little line to a chronicle. It sits there quietly for centuries. Then a moment comes when that particular story is useful in the battles of the day. Out it comes. And critical thinking disappears.

It's an extraordinarily striking mechanism.
