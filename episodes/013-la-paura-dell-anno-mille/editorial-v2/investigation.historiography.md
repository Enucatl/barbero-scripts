## 1. Carducci: recovered original and quotation boundaries

### Source and access

Giosue Carducci, “Dello svolgimento della letteratura nazionale,” **Discorso primo**, section I, in *Pagine di storia letteraria* (1920 anthology), whose heading refers back to *Opere*, I, pp. 29–51. The discourses were delivered at Bologna in 1868–1871. Primary public-domain text retrieved in [Project Gutenberg HTML](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/77906/77906-h/77906-h.htm) and [plain text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/77906/pg77906.txt). The plain-text opening is around lines 1276 onward. This is a later anthology witness, not a claim to have inspected Carducci’s manuscript or first printing. Punctuation and historical spelling below follow this transcription.

### U-00070–U-00082: direct quotation

Recovered original:

> V’immaginate il levar del sole nel primo giorno dell’anno mille? Questo fatto di tutte le mattine ricordate che fu quasi miracolo, fu promessa di vita nuova, per le generazioni uscenti dal secolo decimo?

Proposed English translation of the recovered original:

> Can you imagine the sun rising on the first day of the year one thousand? Do you remember that this everyday event was almost a miracle, a promise of new life, for the generations emerging from the tenth century?

The following catalogue of terrors belongs to Carducci in the original, but Barbero summarizes it at U-00083–U-00085. Do not expand that summary into a long quotation merely because the original has been found.

### U-00087–U-00092: Carducci quoting tradition and compressing Revelation

Recovered Carducci wording:

> — Mille, e non piú mille — aveva, secondo la tradizione, detto Gesú: dopo mille anni, leggevasi nell’Apocalipsi, Satana sarà disciolto.

Proposed English:

> “A thousand, and no more than a thousand,” Jesus had said, according to tradition; after a thousand years, one read in Revelation, Satan would be released.

There are **two levels of attribution**. This is recoverable as Carducci’s wording; it is not evidence that Jesus said the first formula. Nor is the second clause a word-for-word quotation of Revelation 20:7. The transcript’s “dissolto” should not produce “dissolved”: the recovered word is **disciolto**, “released.” Keep “according to tradition,” or make the quotation frame equally clear. Coordinate biblical source treatment with the biblical investigation.

### U-00097–U-00107: excerpt with a middle omission

Recovered full sentence:

> E che stupore di gioia e che grido salí al cielo dalle turbe raccolte in gruppi silenziosi intorno a’ manieri feudali, accosciate e singhiozzanti nelle chiese tenebrose e ne’ chiostri, sparse con pallidi volti e sommessi mormorii per le piazze e alla campagna, quando il sole, eterno fonte di luce e di vita, si levò trionfale la mattina dell’anno mille!

Barbero omits the clause beginning **“sparse con pallidi volti”** and ending **“alla campagna.”** Preserve that selection; do not silently insert the omitted scene. Suggested translation of the actual selected portions:

> And what astonished joy, what a cry rose to heaven from the crowds gathered in silent groups around the feudal castles, crouching and sobbing in the dark churches and cloisters, […] when the sun, eternal source of light and life, rose triumphant on the morning of the year one thousand!

The ellipsis is useful in the written ledger. Spoken delivery can make the excerpt continuous without vocalizing it. U-00093–U-00096 and subsequent reactions are Barbero’s narration, not Carducci’s prose. The later return to Carducci, U-00708–U-00724, is comparison and commentary, not another long recoverable quotation.

## 2. Théophile Lavallée: passage recovered in a later edition

### Identity, edition, and evidence limits

Normalize “La Vallet” to **Théophile Lavallée**, and “Michelée” to **Jules Michelet**. A publisher’s [facsimile catalogue](https://www.hachettebnf.fr/livre/histoire-des-francais-depuis-le-temps-des-gaulois-jusquen-1830-edition-4tome-1-ed1844/) establishes *Histoire des Français depuis le temps des Gaulois jusqu’en 1830*, fourth edition, volume I, **1844**. Thus Barbero’s edition date is plausible, not the work’s first-publication date.

The identified [1844 Gallica witness](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k54738900) could not be retrieved as readable text in this investigation. The passage **was** retrieved in the [1863 volume I Internet Archive witness](https://archive.org/details/histoiredesfran01lavauoft), through its [full OCR](https://archive.org/download/histoiredesfran01lavauoft/histoiredesfran01lavauoft_djvu.txt), printed **p. 249**, chapter II, “987–1000 — Hugues-Capet.” OCR line breaks and obvious recognition errors have been normalized; the printed page image was not visually collated. Do not label the wording below “1844 text verified.”

“Allievo di Michelet” is not established as literal classroom pupilhood by the material accessed. “A follower of Michelet” could express intellectual affiliation, but even that should remain Barbero’s characterization unless separately sourced. No authoritative biographical proof of formal teacher–student status was found.

### U-00113–U-00126: direct translated quotation, interrupted by Barbero

Recovered French in the 1863 witness:

> D’ailleurs la croyance en la fin du monde, croyance qui semblait justifiée par les pestes, les famines, les calamités de tout genre dont l’Europe était désolée, répandait une atonie universelle. Tout était glacé d’effroi à l’attente du jour fatal, toute entreprise avait cessé, tout mouvement était arrêté; il n’y avait plus ni d’espoir ni d’avenir. On redoublait de ferveur religieuse, on se pressait dans les couvents, on donnait ses biens à l’Église, et de toutes parts on entendait ce cri lugubre : « La fin du monde approche ! »

Proposed English for Barbero’s selected opening, which omits “D’ailleurs”:

> Belief in the end of the world—a belief seemingly justified by the plagues, famines, and calamities of every kind that devastated Europe—spread a universal torpor. Everything was frozen with terror as the fatal day approached. Every undertaking had ceased; all movement had stopped. There was no longer any hope or any future. Religious fervour redoubled. People crowded into monasteries and gave their property to the Church. On every side the mournful cry was heard: “The end of the world is approaching!”

U-00112 explicitly announces an improvised translation (“Traduco alla buona”). The passage remains a quotation despite the roughness of the Italian rendering. U-00123, the Odifreddi joke, is **Barbero’s interruption**, not part of Lavallée. End the quote before that aside and resume afterward; never attach the joke to the nineteenth-century author. The later-edition recovery is strong enough to establish wording and boundaries, with an edition limitation recorded in the quote ledger; its adoption still needs the coordinator’s quotation decision.

## 3. Sigebert, pseudo-Godel, and Trithemius: transmission is more complex than a straight line

### Sigebert of Gembloux, U-00541–U-00565

Correct identity: **Sigebert of Gembloux** (*Sigebertus Gemblacensis*), whose chronicle extends into the early twelfth century. Edition locator: *Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Scriptores* VI, **p. 354**, entry 1000; [dMGH viewer](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_6/index.htm#page/354/mode/1up). The viewer shell was opened, but its scanned page was not successfully inspected. The Latin entry is reproduced by Gouguenheim, “L’histoire d’un mythe,” printed **p. 138, note 80**, [full PDF](https://electrodes-h-sinclair-502.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/invention_terreurs_an_mil__alma_1999_57_111.pdf), [official journal record](https://www.persee.fr/doc/alma_0994-8090_1999_num_57_1_1753).

Reliably readable opening of the medieval text:

> Anno Iesu Christi millesimo secundum supputationem Dionisii multa prodigia visa sunt. Terraemotus factus est permaximus, cometes apparuit.

Translation:

> In the thousandth year of Jesus Christ, according to the reckoning of Dionysius, many prodigies were seen. There was a very great earthquake; a comet appeared.

The continued entry describes a fiery light, an opening in the sky, and a serpent-like form. OCR in that continuation is unreliable, so no fully recovered quotation is offered for it. The entry does not itself say the population feared the world’s end. It is reasonable to distinguish a later chronicle’s grouping of portents from contemporary testimony of panic. The specific reconstruction “earthquake in 1000, comet in 1002, combined under 1000” was not independently traced back through each earlier annal here; keep it attributed to Barbero’s source criticism. Calling Sigebert personally a **millenarian** is an interpretation not demonstrated merely by this annal’s wording. Do not intensify it into a biographical fact that he expected an imminent end in his own lifetime.

### Chronicle traditionally attributed to Guillaume Godel, U-00566–U-00586

Auguste Molinier, *Les Sources de l’histoire de France*, II (1902), **pp. 316–317, no. 2203**, [full notice](https://www.persee.fr/doc/shf_0000-0000_1902_num_2_1_917_t1_0316_0000_2), identifies the *Chronique dite de Guillaume Godel*, ending in 1173, manuscript **BnF latin 4893**. Its attribution to Guillaume Godel of Saint-Martial arose from a later addition and was already abandoned in the specialist tradition Molinier records. He describes an English-born author with connections to Sens and Berry. This is sufficient evidence to qualify authorship.

A 2024 catalogue by Pierre Alexandre and Alexandre-Lamotte, [entry 194, printed p. 150](https://publi2-as.oma.be/record/6763/files/Alexandre%26Alexandre-Lamotte2024.pdf?version=3), likewise rejects the named attribution and suggests Cistercian Pontigny for the anonymous author. **Access limit:** this exact entry was retrieved as a substantial search excerpt, but direct PDF and repository landing access failed. Treat it as corroborating a caution, not as a fully inspected new edition. It also warns that the older RHGF text is defective. Edition locators: *Recueil des historiens des Gaules et de la France* X (1760), **259–263**; XI, 282–285; XIII, 671–677; supplementary extracts in MGH SS XXVI, 196–198.

Do not “correct” the lecture to “Guillaume Godel, a Benedictine.” The 1999 Iogna-Prat review criticizes the Cistercian label by treating Godel as the Saint-Martial monk; the attribution problem makes that shortcut unsafe.

For the **1010 entry**, Jules Roy, *L’an mille* (1885), discussion in [accessible retypeset PDF](https://www.mediterranee-antique.fr/Fichiers_PdF/PQRS/Roy/An_mille.pdf), PDF **p. 58**, links the chronicler’s report of fear to news about Jerusalem, rather than simply to a catalogue of natural prodigies. Roy is an old scholarly intermediary with outdated authorship assumptions, not a substitute for the Latin edition. A Latin sentence beginning *Anno Domini MX in multis locis per orbem, tali rumore audito…* was located in search results but not authenticated against a satisfactory primary witness. **Original text for quotation replacement remains null.**

Barbero’s account is a paraphrase of a later chronicle, not a verbatim quotation requiring invented Latin. The sound correction is to qualify the attribution and retain the chronological distance. Whether the specific 1010 fear should be linked explicitly to Jerusalem requires further primary collation or a deliberate contextual correction. “He invented it” is stronger than “we have not found it in his surviving sources”: preserve the distinction if drafting explanatory prose.

### Trithemius, U-00595–U-00607

The general content attributed to the annals is recoverable: earthquakes, a comet, and fear of the last day in the year 1000. **It is not secure that Johannes Trithemius himself authored this specific addition.** Edward Peters, “Mutations, Adjustments, Terrors, Historians, and the Year 1000,” in Michael Frassetto (ed.), *The Year 1000* (2002), **p. 14; p. 25, notes 22–24**, distinguishes the *Chronica* from the *Annales*. The former was printed in 1559/1601 and lacks this passage; the latter appeared in print in 1690. Earlier scholarship raised interpolation, and Peters leaves the authorship of this entry unresolved. Read in the [accessible full-volume transcription](https://www.scribd.com/document/624751552/Michael-Frassetto-Eds-The-Year-1000-Religious-and-Social-Response-to-the-Turning-of-the-First-Millennium-Palgrave-Macmillan-US-2002).

Peters points to Pietro Orsi, “L’Anno Mille,” *Rivista storica italiana* IV (1887), **pp. 52–53**, for the Latin, and Klaus Schreiner’s 1966/67 study of the manuscript transmission. Neither was directly inspected here. Gouguenheim’s 1999 article, p. 138 n. 81, supplies a French rendering but cites the Hirsau works confusingly. **No original Latin is supplied as recovered or eligible for substitution.** Barbero’s passage should be classified as paraphrase, with source-attribution qualification available for selection.

The genealogy also involves Baronius and later readership. Omission of these stages from a lecture does not require adding a new mini-lecture. It does mean the adaptation should not amplify the three examples into a fully demonstrated, exclusive chain of direct copying.

## 4. Saverio Bettinelli: two recovered excerpts and one unresolved

### Title and date

The work is **Saverio Bettinelli, *Del Risorgimento d’Italia negli studj, nelle arti e ne’ costumi dopo il Mille***, with title variants in later editions. The primary text accessed is the 1799 collected-works witness transcribed at [Wikisource](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Il_risorgimento_d%27Italia), especially [Part I, “Mille — Capo secondo”](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Il_risorgimento_d%27Italia/Parte_I/Mille_-_Capo_secondo). This is an openly available transcription with imperfect proofreading; no claim is made to have collated the first-edition scan.

The [Treccani scholarly essay “Italia e storiografia”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/italia-e-storiografia_%28altro%29/), section on Verri and Denina, distinguishes the **1773 date printed on the work from actual publication in 1775**. Thus Barbero’s “1773” is bibliographically understandable. Proposed optional precision: “a book dated 1773,” or “in the 1770s.” A silent switch to 1775 could obscure why the speaker says 1773. The word *Risorgimento* here concerns the revival of learning, arts, and customs; Barbero’s comparison with the later national movement is commentary, not a claim that Bettinelli coined the word.

### U-00681–U-00688: original not recovered

The lecture’s “Dopo il mille tornossi alquanto alle arti…” sentence was searched directly and against the retrieved opening chapters, without a secure matching source passage. It may belong elsewhere in the multi-part work; do not manufacture a match. **Original text: null.** Classify it as Barbero’s declared quotation, recoverability unresolved. A contextual English rendering of what he says is possible, with that limitation documented:

> After the year one thousand, people returned to some extent to the arts, which had previously been neglected because of a universal belief that the end of the tenth century would also be the end of the world.

This is a translation of the lecture’s Italian, **not an independently recovered Bettinelli quotation**.

### U-00689–U-00704: commentary, then composite quotation

Recovered chapter-II opening:

> Piccola cosa ne sembra, ed è perciò dagli storici appena accennata quell’opinion generale, che avea gli animi preoccupati della fine del mondo imminente. Eppur non è credibile quant’essa pregiudicasse insino all’ultimo giorno del secolo X., e quanto danno togliesse il non aspettato principio del mille.

Translation:

> That general belief which had filled people’s minds with the imminent end of the world seems a small matter to us, and historians therefore barely mention it. Yet it is hard to believe how much harm it did until the very last day of the tenth century, and how much harm the unexpected beginning of the year one thousand removed.

Barbero paraphrases the first sentence in U-00689–U-00694. His quotation at U-00695–U-00704 condenses the antecedent to “quell’opinione,” inserts his own clarification “che stesse per finire il mondo,” repeats an introductory phrase, and explains *quanto danno togliesse* with “cioè quanto bene abbia fatto.” These explanatory insertions are **Barbero’s**, not additional Bettinelli words. Preserve the quote/commentary alternation; replacing the entire stretch with the recovered two sentences would silently turn paraphrase into quotation.

Suggested treatment for the quoted core:

> “It is hard to believe how much harm it did until the very last day of the tenth century, and how much harm the unexpected beginning of the year one thousand removed”—in other words, how much good it did.

The final gloss lies outside the quotation. The negative-to-positive gloss is faithful to the rhetorical structure.

### U-00705–U-00707: two clipped excerpts

Recovered continuous original:

> L’orror sempre presente d’una prossima desolazione universale, rinnovato da ogni accidente non solito della natura, o tenuto per minaccioso tolse ad ognuno speranze e pensieri intorno ad un avvenire, in cui già disperavan d’esistere neppur col nome, neppur ne’ figli, neppur nella memoria degli uomini destinati tutti a perire.
>
> Questa orrenda disperazione non dovette lasciar altri pensieri, fuorchè di continuo terrore, di fuga, di scampo, e dovette chiamar tutti gli affetti a un’altra vita, restando inerzia e abbandono di tutto il presente.

Barbero omits the first sentence’s middle clause beginning *rinnovato*, stops at *speranze*, then gives only the opening of the second sentence. Suggested translation of those selections:

> “The ever-present horror of an approaching universal devastation […] robbed everyone of hope.” “This dreadful despair must have left room for no other thoughts…”

The “eccetera” and “via romanticheggiando” reaction is Barbero. The original says *non dovette*, a retrospective inference (“must have”), which should not become proof from eyewitness testimony. Bettinelli’s following discussion expressly allows gradual recovery: do not strengthen Barbero’s comparison into a claim that Bettinelli described a literal overnight transformation in all institutions.

## 5. Dom François Plaine and the politics of debunking

## 6. Gouguenheim, Duby, and the state of the debate

### Dates and what the terrors book actually addresses

**Sylvain Gouguenheim, *Les Fausses Terreurs de l’an mil: attente de la fin des temps ou approfondissement de la foi?*, Paris, Picard, 1999, 231 pp.** The date and title are established by two contemporary scholarly review records actually opened:

- Dominique Iogna-Prat, *Médiévales* 37 (1999), **165–166**, [review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/medi_0751-2708_1999_num_18_37_1473_t1_0165_0000_1).
- Georges Pon, *Cahiers de civilisation médiévale* 47 (2004), **192–193**, [review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmed_0007-9731_2004_num_47_186_2882_t1_0192_0000_3).

The reviews support the connection between the book and a critique of Duby’s diffuse fears, with debate extending to Fried and Landes. They do **not** establish Barbero’s claims about the author’s ancestry or private religious commitment. Keep “ultra-Catholic” and related labels, if retained, visibly within Barbero’s polemic rather than introducing them as neutral biographical credentials.

Georges Duby’s *L’An mil* dates to **1967**; subsequent editions can carry different dates. His position is not a simple endorsement of universal midnight panic. Barbero’s account of Duby retaining a wider atmosphere of unease is recognizable as a summary of the historiographical dispute. The sarcastic dialogue at U-00830–U-00849 is **Barbero ventriloquizing an argument**, not a recovered exchange or literal passage from Gouguenheim.

### *Aristote au Mont-Saint-Michel* (2008): claims versus findings

The 2008 publication and the controversy are established in Guillaume Dye’s scholarly review, [*Revue belge de philologie et d’histoire* 87 (2009), 811–835](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_2009_num_87_3_7707). **Access limit:** the landing page provided the opening page and bibliographical information, not every page of that review. It is not claimed as fully read.

Florian Louis, [“L’affaire Aristote: retour sur un emballement historiographico-médiatique,” *Acta fabula*, 22 May 2008](https://www.fabula.org/acta/document4195.php), was retrieved through its HTML and relevant paragraphs read. Paragraphs 4–6 identify the book’s arguments and page locators: Greek transmission, Jacques of Venice and Mont Saint-Michel, Christian translators within Arabic culture, and the linguistic claim on **p. 137**. One short recoverable fragment, reproduced by that review, is:

> les différences entre les deux systèmes linguistiques sont telles qu’elles défient presque toute traduction

English:

> the differences between the two linguistic systems are such that they defy almost any translation

This is a short quotation **through a review**, not independently collated against the book. It substantiates that a strong linguistic argument exists. It does not authenticate Barbero’s exact formulations “Arabic is completely unsuited to scientific subjects,” “Arabs are not people with a scientific mentality,” or “we owe Islamic civilization nothing.” Those are paraphrase and interpretation in the lecture; source originals for those precise formulations remain **null**. The book itself was located in an online OCR listing, but direct retrieval failed, so no claim is made to have read it.

A firmer historical check comes from the [Université de Caen / Bibliothèque virtuelle du Mont Saint-Michel, exhibition 37 (2017)](https://emmsm.unicaen.fr/emmsm/bvmsm/37.html), section **“Traduction arabo-latine et commentaire de la Métaphysique d’Aristote,” Avranches BM 220, ff. 14v–15r**. Its manuscript specialists distinguish Gouguenheim’s proposed translation centre from the surviving evidence: Jacques of Venice is not documented at the abbey; surviving Aristotle volumes postdate him; the collection also includes Arabic-to-Latin translations. Library holdings are not proof that the translations were made on site. This directly supports qualifying “discovered” at U-00867–U-00873. Ptolemy’s translation at the Mont was not corroborated.

U-00866 says Barbero has not read the book in full, and U-00875 says he consulted the relevant pages. Preserve that limitation; dropping it would give his account greater apparent authority. “Five years earlier” fits a 2013 lecture and the 2008 book; the subsequent claim that the 1999 terrors book was Gouguenheim’s “next book” does not.

### What contemporary scholarship supports

James Palmer, *The Apocalypse in the Early Middle Ages* (2014), chapter 7, **“The year 1000 and other apocalypticisms (c. 911–c. 1033),” pp. 189–226**, [Cambridge chapter record and summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/apocalypse-in-the-early-middle-ages/year-1000-and-other-apocalypticisms-c-911c-1033/CC4AED85E3CCAE26141E00673B64D3F7), distinguishes limited explicit evidence and the absence of mass hysteria from the broader, heterogeneous presence of apocalyptic ideas. The accessed material is the publisher’s chapter summary, **not the complete chapter**. It supports this editorial boundary: reject the universal shutdown legend without asserting that nobody around 1000 could feel eschatological concern.

The scholarly debate itself is documented by [“Qui a peur de l’an mil? Un débat électronique aux approches de l’an 2000,” *Médiévales* 37 (1999), 15–55](https://www.persee.fr/doc/medi_0751-2708_1999_num_18_37_1462). Richard Landes’s alternative argument is available in [“The Fear of an Apocalyptic Year 1000,” *Speculum* 75 (2000), 97–145](https://www.bu.edu/history/files/2011/10/11.Fear-of-an-Apocalyptic-Year-1000-Speculum.pdf). These were opened to establish relevant scholarship and scope; the complete works were not read in this bounded investigation, so no detailed verdict on their arguments is offered. A recording script need not append this research history. It should preserve the distinction between Barbero’s thesis and an alleged unanimous verdict on all millennial expectation.

## Remaining limitations and ledger guidance

- Recovered and well bounded: Carducci’s three quoted stretches; Lavallée’s passage in the **1863**, not directly verified 1844, witness; Bettinelli’s chapter-II composite and clipped excerpts.
- Unrecovered for source substitution: Bettinelli U-00681–U-00688; the complete Godel 1010 Latin; Trithemius Latin; precise Gouguenheim anti-Arabic/science formulations. Do not back-translate the lecture and call it recovered source text.
