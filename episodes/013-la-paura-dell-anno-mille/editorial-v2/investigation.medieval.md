## Material findings

1. **Fulda may combine two different papal privileges.** The recovered Fulda text dated 31 December 999 confirms successors and reserves future abbots' consecration to Rome. The annual twelve denarii provision is in the preceding privilege for **Seeon**, dated 18 April 999, in the same old collection. Fulda's recension history is also contested. Do not call the entire Fulda privilege a forgery, or claim the twelve denarii clause has been independently recovered for Fulda.
2. **Tortona is identifiable:** Gabotto–Legé, no. VII, pp. 11–12. The primary edition's searchable excerpts give Adelberto and Erenzone, a 29-year term, half the wine, and twenty soldi. Full page images were unavailable; the grain share and precise penalty conditions are still incompletely checked. The editor dates it to the sixth day of an unknown month between January and May 999; do not replace the lecture's unknown month with the unsupported internet date 6 January.
3. **Farfa is recovered in full:** Otto III diploma 331, 3 October 999, MGH pp. 759–760. The future privileges and Last Judgment sanction are real. The document says an infringing successor will answer **alongside Otto** at Christ's coming; it also admits Otto's own earlier alienation of the monastery.
4. **Revelation's spoken excerpt is 20:1–3a**, although the lecture introduces 20:1–7. Do not expand it to all seven verses. Satan's later release is discussed afterward.
5. **Abbo's Apologeticus belongs around 994, not 998, and addresses Hugh and Robert.** Its creed dispute concerns omission of “nor begotten,” not omission of “and the Son.” Its apocalypse paragraphs support elite rebuttal but explicitly say that one rumour had spread almost everywhere; they do not support a categorical assertion that these ideas had no opportunity to circulate.
6. **Thiota's prediction concerned the same year, 847, not the following year.** The annalist says clerics as well as common people followed her. The quoted conversational confession and dismissal are Barbero's dramatization, not recoverable direct speech.

## 1. Sylvester II: Fulda, Seeon, and an apparent composite

### Opened evidence and transmission

No. II expressly derives from E. F. J. Dronke, *Codex diplomaticus Fuldensis* (1850), p. 341. Medieval Latin and nineteenth-century edition are public domain; translations below are my own, not taken from a copyrighted English translator.

Also opened the [Dronke 1850 edition's digitized OCR](https://archive.org/download/codexdiplomatic00abbegoog/codexdiplomatic00abbegoog_djvu.txt), no. 728, pp. 341–342, including its critical note. The editor distinguishes three recensions, warns that B appears interpolated because of a St Andrew monastery provision associated with 1046, and prints C with B's fuller closing formula. This is a material warning about the transmitted text and date formula; the printed item is not a pristine original bull.

### Individual quotation targets

**U-00147–U-00148: confirmation to abbot and successors.** Identified, recovered Latin; lecture paraphrase, not literal quotation.

> Quocirca, dilectissime fili, omnia quae a praedecessoribus nostris jure ac rationabiliter tui postulavere praecessores, tibi tuisque successoribus perpetuo confirmamus.

Own English: “Therefore, dearest son, everything that your predecessors lawfully and reasonably requested from our predecessors, we confirm forever to you and your successors.”

**U-00149–U-00151: future abbots and Rome.** Identified and recovered; the lecture turns consecration into confirmation of election.

> ut nullus inde futurus abbas consecrationem unquam praesumat accipere nisi ab hac sede apostolica.

Own English: “so that no future abbot of that monastery shall ever presume to receive consecration except from this apostolic see.”

Proposed faithful documentary paraphrase, if authorized: “Every future abbot must receive his consecration from Rome.” The source does not literally state the lecture's visit to request election confirmation. Avoid claiming a recovered verbatim equivalent for that phrasing.

**U-00152–U-00155: annual twelve denarii.** Not recovered in Fulda no. II. A close and exact parallel is in **Seeon no. I**, immediately above:

> Et pro respectu testimonioque concessae libertatis, sub honore XII apostolorum XII denarii ad limina apostolorum Petri et Pauli omni anno deferantur. Et si omni anno praesentari impossibile fuerit, infra spatium annorum XII persolvantur.

Own English: “As acknowledgement and testimony of the freedom granted, twelve denarii, in honour of the twelve apostles, shall be brought every year to the shrines of the apostles Peter and Paul. If they cannot be presented every year, they shall be paid within twelve years.”

Seeon dating clause: `Data XIV Kal. Maii, anno Dominicae incarnationis 999, indictione XII.` That is 18 April 999. Its title identifies the monastery of St Lambert at Seeon, with Count Aribo and Otto III involved. Do **not** silently transfer this quotation into Fulda or say the recovered Seeon wording establishes a Fulda payment. Status: apparent composite/conflation, requiring decision or deferral. A cautious proposal is to retain Fulda's future-successor point and omit the unverified payment illustration, with the change explicitly authorized and logged; alternatively retain Barbero's contextual rendering with a research warning. No choice is made here.

**U-00138–U-00145: date/issuer/place claim.** Fulda's nineteenth-century heading gives 31 December 999; the Latin closing formula has `Datum II Kal. Januar.` and Sylvester's first pontifical year. The existence of a transmitted privilege under this date is supported. Describing a personally delivered, original document signed that day overstates what the recovered edition demonstrates. The modern critical recension question remains open.

**U-00156–U-00157: the pope and abbot expected no imminent end.** Historical inference, not the privilege's explicit statement. Future provisions support continuing administration. A formula anticipating successors does not by itself prove an author's complete lack of apocalyptic expectation. Separate this interpretive limitation from the document's date and clauses.

Other claims in U-00158–U-00161 (Gerbert's learning and magic reputation) were seen in the Italian, but are outside this bounded documentary check; `Gerbert of Aurillac` is the expected normalized identification, subject to source/audio correction.

## 3. Otto III and Farfa, 3 October 999

### Edition actually inspected

Theodor Sickel, ed., *Die Urkunden Otto des II. und Otto des III.*, MGH Diplomata regum et imperatorum Germaniae 2, **D O III 331, pp. 759–760**. Both original printed scan pages were opened and visually read through MGH:

The edition lists late-eleventh-century *Registrum Farfense* and early-twelfth-century *Chronicon Farfense* transmission; it does not identify a surviving contemporary original. All English below is translated directly for this research.

The edition's heading gives Rome, 3 October 999. **U-00226–U-00227 supported.**

### Recovered quotation segments

**U-00228–U-00231**, p. 760 lines 1–4:

> et cum hac nostra preceptali pagina predicto monasterio perdonavimus ut amplius in eternum nunquam detur per nos nec per nostros successores in beneficium, sed semper permaneat rei publice destinatum, abbati mancipatum, nulli alteri subiectum vel prestitum.

Own English: “And by this charter we have granted to the aforesaid monastery that henceforth, for all eternity, it shall never be given as a benefice by us or our successors, but shall always remain assigned to the public realm, committed to its abbot, subjected or handed over to no one else.”

`rei publice` is not an assertion of modern republican government. “The public realm” / “the imperial realm” is a useful contextual rendering; Barbero's “the state” is an explanatory modernization. Preserve his explanatory beat if desired, but do not identify it as the sole literal meaning. `per nos` means Otto too is bound, and is absent from the lecture's compressed version.

**U-00232–U-00236**, p. 760 lines 4–7:

> Unde deum omnipotentem precamur ut si aliquis papa aut imperator noster successor unquam memoratum monasterium alicui persone subiugaverit, nobiscum in Christi adventu, dum venerit iudicare seculum per ignem, rationem inde reddat et se deliquisse pro hac cautione recognoscat.

Own English: “Therefore we pray to Almighty God that, if any pope or emperor, a successor of ours, ever subjects the aforesaid monastery to any person, he may render account for it alongside us at Christ's coming, when he comes to judge the world by fire, and acknowledge that he has sinned against this guarantee.”

The lecture's future-accountability passage is recognizably based on this source, but compressed. Recovered-source replacement is possible **only after the quotation decision**. A performance-friendly contextual rendering can preserve the lecture's scope without adding the fire imagery it omits. “Alongside us” matters more than expanding the imagery because it connects with Otto's own conduct.

### Narrative and inference

At p. 759 lines 30–40, Otto describes leaving Rome, meeting Margrave Hugh about restoring the `res publica`, discussing imperial affairs with Pope Sylvester II and other magnates, and then camping at Farfa. Abbot Hugh approaches him there with earlier imperial precepts and laments the monastery's dispersal, especially under Otto: `quia id ipsum monasterium Hugoni episcopo in beneficium concessimus, quod ignorantes fecimus` — “because we granted that same monastery as a benefice to Bishop Hugh, which we did in ignorance.”

- **U-00213–U-00214:** lecture simplifies the petition's setting; the diploma describes the abbot approaching Otto at Farfa, not a queue of Farfa monks in Rome. Distinguish the document's date/place of issue (Rome) from its narrative of the encounter.
- **U-00221–U-00224:** the retiring adviser is an illustrative scenario in Barbero's own voice. Do not turn it into the identified cause of this specific transaction.
- **U-00225:** text inspected here explicitly commits the abbey to its abbot; its general predecessor privileges do not establish within this excerpt the precise free-election clause. Avoid claiming full documentary recovery for that detail.
- **U-00237–U-00240:** continued administration and a future judgment are explicit; “he certainly did not expect the world to end next year” is an inference. Future clauses and apocalyptic belief are not logically mutually exclusive. Preserve Barbero's argument as his interpretation unless an editorial adjustment is authorized.

### Creed — separate quotation target U-00353–U-00355

PL 139, 470D–471A:

> Primitus de fide dicendum credidi, quam alternantibus choris et in Francia et apud Anglorum Ecclesiam variari audivi. Alii dicunt, ut arbitror, secundum Athanasium, « Spiritus sanctus a Patre et Filio non factus, nec creatus, nec genitus, sed procedens: » alii vero tantum, « Spiritus sanctus a Patre et Filio non factus nec creatus, sed procedens, » qui dum, id quod est, nec genitus subtrahunt, synodicam domni Gregorii se sequi credunt, ubi ita est scriptum: « Spiritus sanctus nec genitus est, nec ingenitus, sed tantum procedens. »

Own English of the two contrasting forms: “The Holy Spirit is from the Father and the Son, not made, nor created, nor begotten, but proceeding”; versus “The Holy Spirit is from the Father and the Son, not made nor created, but proceeding.”

Both forms include **the Father and the Son**. The disputed omission is **nec genitus**, “nor begotten.” This is Athanasian creed language and must not be recast as a Filioque omission. Proposed short accurate spoken paraphrase, pending authorization: “Some clerics leave out ‘nor begotten’ when they recite the creed, and Abbo wants that corrected.” If the production keeps Barbero's explanation unchanged, flag the unresolved mismatch; no source recovered supports that explanation.

### Advent — separate quotation/allusion target U-00356–U-00357

PL 139, 472A, follows the apocalypse paragraph:

### Richard and the Annunciation — separate quotation targets

PL 139, 471–472A:

> Denique et errorem qui de fine mundi inolevit abbas meus beatae memoriae Richardus sagaci animo propulit, postquam litteras a Lothariensibus accepit, quibus me respondere jussit; nam fama pene totum mundum impleverat, quod, quando Annuntiatio Dominica in Parasceve contigisset absque ullo scrupulo finis saeculi esset.

Own English: “My abbot Richard, of blessed memory, also shrewdly drove back an error that had grown up about the end of the world, after receiving letters from the Lotharingians, which he ordered me to answer; for a rumour had filled almost the whole world that, whenever the Lord's Annunciation fell on Good Friday, that would unquestionably be the end of the age.”

- **U-00381–U-00387:** distinct attributed rumour/proposition, directly identified in the Latin. “Whenever the Annunciation fell on Good Friday, the world would end” is a faithful contextual translation. It is a reported prediction, not Abbo's belief.
- **U-00402–U-00404:** distinct instruction/reply paraphrase. Abbo says Richard ordered him to answer; no surviving reply has been recovered in this investigation. “These are all bullshit” is explicitly identified by Barbero himself as his comic gloss. Preserve that metacomment if using the joke; do not represent the phrase as Richard's recovered direct speech.
- **U-00397–U-00399:** the 908/970 coincidence check has not been independently settled by a primary calendar here. Do not treat the later 981/992 occurrences as refuting the claim that 908 was the previous occurrence before 970.

### Interpretation claim U-00407–U-00417

The primary source explicitly says `fama pene totum mundum impleverat` — “a rumour had filled almost the whole world.” That is rhetorical breadth, not measurable universal panic. Nevertheless, it directly undermines the categorical lecture claim that these ideas had **no possibility of spreading or circulating**. Abbo supports the existence of predictions and vigorous learned opposition, not their automatic immediate suppression everywhere. This is the highest-priority interpretive issue in this section.

Proposed minimal moderation, only if authorized: “These predictions did circulate, and churchmen such as Abbo and Richard worked to rebut them.” This preserves the lecture's learned-opposition point while avoiding a contrary claim about circulation. Do not use the source's rhetoric as proof of universal terror in 999/1000, either.

Independent corroboration consulted: Elizabeth Boyle, “[Rhetoric and Reality: The Eschatological Thought of an Eleventh-Century Irishman](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/7661/1/EB-Rhetoric-2016.pdf),” especially printed p. 288 n. 68, quotes the Abbo passage and distinguishes ordinary expectation of the end from predictions of its imminence. The article was opened. Its copyrighted translation has not been copied here; primary Latin and my own translation above are the quotation evidence.

### Augustine: exact source and limits of the inference

**U-00452–U-00458 — recovered direct quotation.** The source is **Augustine, Epistula 197, §3, to Hesychius of Salona**, not an unidentified passage from *The City of God*. The [Latin text](https://www.augustinus.it/latino/lettere/lettera_202_testo.htm) was opened and the entire short letter read. Its editorial heading dates it to late 419 or early 420. Notice that the website filename says `202`, while the actual document heading is **Epistola 197**. The [parallel Italian text](https://www.augustinus.it/italiano/lettere/lettera_202_testo.htm) was also opened; it does not establish which translation Barbero consulted.

> Tempora ergo computare, […] ut sciamus quando sit finis huius saeculi vel adventus Domini, nihil mihi aliud videtur, quam scire velle quod ipse ait scire neminem posse.

The marked omission is the parenthetical `hoc est` followed by a Greek gloss supplied on the website as an inline image. The image linkage appears inconsistent with the surrounding distinction between Greek words for times; the gloss is not silently transcribed or repaired here. The complete Latin main sentence on either side is recoverable and directly matches the lecture.

Own English: “Calculating the times, then, to discover when this world will end or the Lord will come seems to me to be nothing other than wanting to know what he himself said no one can know.” This is a close translated quotation. It needs an Augustine quotation entry, not only a general theological claim entry.

In **§§1–3**, Augustine addresses attempts to distinguish an unknowable exact day/hour from supposedly calculable longer periods. He invokes Matthew 24:36, but his decisive broader-times proof is **Acts 1:7**. Consequently, the statement is not merely a loose repetition of the Matthew sentence immediately before it in the lecture. **U-00446–U-00451**, calling Augustine “the first” to say this clearly, is a priority claim that this research does not establish. The letter itself invokes earlier scriptural authority and Jerome. If the coordinator chooses to soften that priority claim, “an enormously influential early Christian thinker” conveys his role without claiming a proved first occurrence. That is an editorial proposal, not an authorized source change.

**U-00465 — indirect biblical allusion through Augustine, with an added Antichrist sequence.** Augustine **Epistula 197, §4** quotes **Matthew 24:14**. The original Greek witness above reads:

> καὶ κηρυχθήσεται τοῦτο τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τῆς βασιλείας ἐν ὅλῃ τῇ οἰκουμένῃ εἰς μαρτύριον πᾶσιν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν, καὶ τότε ἥξει τὸ τέλος.

Own English: “And this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole inhabited world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come.” Augustine's quoted Latin is:

> Et praedicabitur hoc Evangelium regni in universo orbe in testimonium omnibus gentibus, et tunc veniet finis.

The lecture compresses this into “the Gospel must be preached to all the peoples of the earth” and adds “before the Antichrist and then the end of the world.” Neither Matthew 24:14 nor this paragraph of Letter 197 names the Antichrist. Treat the utterance as a composite theological paraphrase rather than quote the whole sentence as this verse or this Augustine passage. No separate source for the added Antichrist ordering has been established here.

**U-00466–U-00469 — the universal-preaching premise is supported; “not next year” is the lecturer's dramatized inference.** In §4 Augustine writes:

> Tunc veniet, quid est, nisi "ante non veniet"? Quanto post ergo veniat, incertum nobis est; ante tamen non esse venturum dubitare utique non debemus.

Own English: “What does ‘then it will come’ mean except ‘it will not come before’? How long afterward it will come is unknown to us; but we certainly must not doubt that it will not come before.” He adds that even reliable news of universal preaching would not reveal how much time remained. At the paragraph's close he allows that the remaining peoples might receive the Gospel rapidly, within a few years. Thus his argument is **not before universal preaching, and no knowable interval afterward**, not a dated guarantee that the end cannot occur in the next calendar year. The question-and-answer exchange in U-00466–U-00469 is not recovered direct speech.

Additional primary confirmation: **Epistula 199, 12.46–49**, the longer letter *De fine saeculi*, [Latin text](https://www.augustinus.it/latino/lettere/lettera_204_testo.htm), §§46–49 read in context. At **12.46**, Augustine expressly disputes the claim that the apostles had already preached to everyone and says that reports from African captives showed peoples still untouched by the Gospel:

> Sunt enim apud nos, hoc est, in Africa, barbarae innumerabiles gentes, in quibus nondum esse praedicatum Evangelium, ex iis qui ducuntur inde captivi, et Romanorum servitiis iam miscentur, quotidie nobis addiscere in promptu est.

Own English: “Here among us, that is, in Africa, there are countless non-Roman peoples among whom the Gospel has not yet been preached; we can learn this daily from people brought captive from there who are now mixed among the Romans' slaves.” His term `barbarae` is his classification; the English avoids presenting it as the investigator's judgment. **12.48** also distinguishes the promise to all peoples from a promise that every individual will believe. This supports the lecture's “not yet preached everywhere” premise without supplying its “next year” punchline.

**U-00470–U-00478 — interpretation remains separate.** These letters establish Augustine's arguments and their scriptural basis. They cannot by themselves establish the lecture's wider claims that medieval apocalyptic hopes had no scope to spread or were invariably stopped from above. The Abbo and Thiota passages examined earlier show why those categorical claims need their own treatment. No new authorization to change the argument is supplied by this supplement.

### Julian Good Friday on March 25: completed deterministic check

**Targets U-00397–U-00399**, with contextual relevance to the undated Abbo/Richard rumour. The calendar deferral above is now resolved as a **transparent calculation**, not as a newly discovered medieval dating statement.

The [US Naval Observatory's explanation of the date of Easter](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/easter) distinguishes Julian and Gregorian ecclesiastical calculations and ecclesiastical from astronomical full moons. Its displayed Gregorian algorithm was not used. The algorithm actually checked against an opened implementation is the `Julian` function, lines 21–30, in [Sonia Keys's *Meeus* package, `easter.go`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/soniakeys/meeus/master/v3/easter/easter.go), copyright 2013, MIT licence. The implementation explicitly returns a **Julian-calendar** month and day. The repository identifies its basis as Jean Meeus's *Astronomical Algorithms*; the book itself was not successfully opened and is not claimed as a read source.

For positive AD year `y`, `%` is the nonnegative remainder and `floor` integer division:

```text
a = y % 4
b = y % 7
c = y % 19
d = (19*c + 15) % 30
e = (2*a + 4*b - d + 34) % 7
s = d + e + 114
Easter month = floor(s / 31)
Easter day   = (s % 31) + 1
```

This arithmetic was actually executed in the tool's JavaScript environment. No Gregorian date library, Gregorian weekday calculation, conversion offset, or modern astronomical lunar phase was involved.

| Julian year | a | b | c | d | e | Easter Sunday, Julian | Good Friday, Julian |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| 908 | 0 | 5 | 15 | 0 | 5 | March 27 | March 25 |
| 970 | 2 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 1 | March 27 | March 25 |
| 981 | 1 | 1 | 12 | 3 | 2 | March 27 | March 25 |
| 992 | 0 | 5 | 4 | 1 | 4 | March 27 | March 25 |

An exhaustive run over integer years **900–1000 inclusive** returned exactly `[908, 970, 981, 992]` for Easter on March 27. Since Good Friday is two days before Easter, all four have Good Friday on March 25. This confirms that **908 was the previous occurrence before 970**, so 981 and 992 do not contradict that lecture claim. It supports the calendar possibility of an Annunciation/Good Friday coincidence in 970. It does **not** independently date Abbo's recalled Paris sermon or Richard's request; those events remain undated in the quoted paragraph and their approximate chronology still requires biographical reasoning.
