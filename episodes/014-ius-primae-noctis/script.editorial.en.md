# Unbelievable Middle Ages: The Right of the First Night

## 1. The modern image of the right of the first night

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00146 -->

Well, good evening. This episode's subject is the so-called right of the first night—*ius primae noctis*. The image is familiar, but let’s spell out what it means.
The idea is that the village lord had a right to a young woman’s virginity, especially when she married. He could sleep with her before her husband, on the first wedding night. This applied to peasant women and women bound to serve him; noblewomen were excluded. This image is extremely widespread in the way people perceive the Middle Ages today, so much so that we continually come across it whenever there is a historical novel or a film set in the Middle Ages. Maybe “whenever” is too strong, but we run into it constantly. Let me give you two examples—both will probably sound familiar.

The first comes from historical fiction. In 2006, a Spanish author named Ildefonso Falcones published a historical novel that became a huge success. The novel is called *The Cathedral of the Sea*. It is a grand story set in the Middle Ages, in fourteenth-century Catalonia, a story of struggle for freedom and against oppression, and it opens with a great scene entirely centered on the right of the first night. Like any popular adventure story, a historical novel needs a powerful opening—something that grabs you immediately. A dead body is even better, but whatever it is, it has to hit hard. *The Cathedral of the Sea* opens with this extremely violent scene of the right of the first night. There is a peasant wedding. There is the peasant who is to be married, and they are prosperous peasants, people who work, who are doing well, respectable people. There's a wedding banquet in the countryside. Then, out of nowhere, the lord turns up on horseback, accompanied by his henchmen. Everyone is very frightened that the lord has arrived there, but obviously they can do nothing. They invite him to sit down, and the lord sits at the table with the other guests and begins to drink. He notices that the bride is a beautiful woman and wants her to be the one who pours his wine. He forces her to come over, touches her, and then, when he has drunk his fill, the lord jumps up, takes the bride by the wrist, calls the bridegroom, and tells him: “In exercise of the right that belongs to me as your lord, I have decided to lie with your wife on her first night.” He drags the woman inside, then comes out after a while buttoning his trousers and says to the husband: “Now it is your turn. […] Do your duty as a good Christian husband!” And nobody has lifted a finger. They are all dismayed, horrified by what is happening, but not one of those peasants lifts a finger, because the lord has the right to do what he is doing. Then this right of the first night, so violent that it is actually rape, will have enormous consequences. The whole novel starts from there, which is simply to say that the right of the first night is a powerful way to drive a story. It sets the whole plot in motion: there is the villain, there are the good people who have suffered the abuse, there is the girl who loses her mind after this violence, and after a while the husband leaves the village and goes to live in the city, where at least there is freedom and no lords who have to be obeyed.

Here's one more example—probably another unnecessary one, but never mind: Mel Gibson's *Braveheart*, a movie many of us have seen. A great big medieval film, the story of the rebellion of the Scots under the leadership of the heroic William Wallace against the wicked English rulers. Here too, the right of the first night comes into play to move the plot forward. It is a most curious scene. The king of England—wicked, naturally—is talking with his barons, and then he says: the problem with Scotland is that it is full of Scots, but I have found the way to put an end to the Scots. The time has come to restore an old tradition. Let us grant the right of the first night. From now on our nobles will have the right to sleep with Scottish girls before they marry, and in this way the idea is that this will dilute the bloodline of those rebels. Notice the difference. In Falcones's novel the author has rather courageously tried to imagine a world where the right of the first night exists as something that one cannot avoid accepting—it is an obligation, it is a law, and nobody rebels. In *Braveheart*, the perspective is different—and you'll see later why that matters. The king says: let us restore an old custom. And of course the consequence, when the English begin to demand the right of the first night, is that the Scots finally rise in revolt.

This is a connection that we find extremely often when the right of the first night is discussed. There are lots of Italian towns or villages whose tradition, whose legend, includes a distant past in which an evil feudal lord oppresses the inhabitants by demanding the right of the first night, until naturally a girl braver than the others, or a husband braver than the others, rebels and puts an end to the tyranny. It's a long list, and an internet search turns up even more. Versions of the story appear in towns across Italy. Ivrea’s orange-throwing carnival, for example, is now said to recall Violetta, the beautiful miller, rebelling against a tyrant who demanded the right of the first night.


A quick aside. To imagine how old this story is, you need only consider that in the Middle Ages they had no idea what the name Violetta even was. The name Violetta became fashionable in the nineteenth century with *La traviata*. Before that there was nobody called Violetta. End of aside—I don't want to influence your judgment.

So we've established our first point: in today's culture, both in local folklore and in the culture of historical novelists and screenwriters—and therefore also of all of us who watch those films and read those novels—the right of the first night is a fairly regular presence whenever the Middle Ages are discussed. The question we’re asking is: all right, let's check. Let's see what evidence there is from the Middle Ages for the right of the first night. Let's go look for it.

## 2. Searching medieval stories for the alleged right

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00147–U-00280 -->

The investigation begins with a simple test: if this right existed, where should it appear in the sources handed down from the Middle Ages? Let’s start with two places.


The first place is literature: stories about ordinary people and everyday situations. Tales of the kind written by Boccaccio are enormously successful in the Middle Ages; people love them and write lots of them. We Italians know Boccaccio's tales well, but similar literary genres exist in other countries too. There are enormous numbers of these stories. They describe recognizable people and situations of the time, and they are very explicit. Sex is discussed a great deal, and without anyone having any difficulty about it. Sex is discussed to tell stories of successful tricks, to show how the poor manage to resist the abuses of the rich. Sex is discussed to show how much cleverness counts and how useful it is even in those situations.

Alongside Boccaccio's tales, there's another genre worth pausing over. In France, even before Boccaccio, as early as the thirteenth century, there is a literary genre called the *fabliaux*. These are short poems, because verse is popular at the time. They are usually very funny and, like the Italian tales, full of pranks, tricks, and strange encounters; and there too sex is discussed all the time, I repeat, realistically. There is the village priest who pursues the shoemaker's wife, and everyone knows that in the village the priest is the man who has studied, the one who speaks best, the one with the most money, and therefore, well, he is successful with the women who come to church. There is the passing student given a bed for the night by the miller who, that night, sets his eye on the miller's daughter, and so forth. Here too there is extreme freedom, including freedom of language, in describing these situations.

You'd think that if any source should tell us about the right of the first night, surely it will be in this medieval storytelling. What a subject for a story the right of the first night would be! It is never found. There is not a single medieval tale, not a single medieval joke or story about sex—and there are countless numbers of them—that ever speaks of the right of the first night. Does that mean they never show a village lord setting his eye on the girls? Of course not, because the village lord certainly does set his eye on the girls.

So how do they handle it? Here's an example, a fifteenth-century French tale. In the fifteenth century a collection of tales comes out in France modeled on Boccaccio’s collection, the *Decameron*: there are one hundred of them, and they are called *The Hundred New Tales*, *Les Cent Nouvelles nouvelles*. Tale 24 follows a count who learns that in one of his villages there is a beautiful girl. The count is a good man, but, well, he doesn’t like to miss an opportunity. So he goes to take a look. The girl really is pretty, and she lives under his rule. So what does the count do? He sends her a go-between, sends one of his servants to tell her: look, the count likes you; if you are clever, if you agree, your fortune is made, yours and your family's. The girl answers: I am a subject of the count; I am ready to obey any order of his except where my honor is concerned. But if they mean to ask me for something contrary to my honor, which I value more highly than my own life—I do not even know the count. The servant goes to the count and reports this answer. The count is disappointed and, since by now he has got the idea into his head, he wanders around the girl's village. Eventually he finds her alone. The girl is alone, cutting grass near the wood. The count arrives and starts making rather rough advances. The girl refuses, and the count becomes more forceful and tells her: listen, keep still, because in any case you are not getting away from me. At this point the girl comes up with an escape plan—and this is the whole point of the tale. She pretends to give in. All right, she says, but please take off those riding boots. They're covered in mud, and I don't want you getting me dirty. How am I supposed to get them off? the count asks. I'll take them off for you, she says. So he sits down and holds out his leg. The boots are very tight. She starts pulling at one, deliberately at the wrong angle. Halfway off, it gets stuck. And that's her chance: she runs, leaving him unable to chase her. [N-001]
The count thinks about it for a while, begins to laugh, decides that the girl has shown spirit and, since he is a good man, lets the matter drop. Indeed, at the first opportunity he arranges a grand marriage with another nobleman for this girl and gives her a generous marriage gift to reward her for her spirit. If ever a story should have hinted that all he had to do was say, I have the right, this would be it. But no, nothing.

There are medieval tales in which, every now and again, this idea of sex as a kind of tax, something people owe, does appear. There are a few tales, but in a very different way from the right of the first night as we imagine it. Here's one example. We are still in this fifteenth-century French collection. It is a tale set in Hostalrich in Catalonia, where Franciscan friars, members of a religious order, settle and build a monastery. [N-002] They begin to do what friars always do, that is, talk with people, hear confessions, give good advice; and, as always happens, many people go to the Franciscans to confess. Many women too, including many pretty women, and somehow or other, after a while, the Franciscans begin to—well, there are all these beautiful women coming to confess and the Franciscans begin to feel desire. I am still telling this fifteenth-century tale. As you can see, in the Middle Ages they have no difficulty telling stories; they are not afraid of offending anyone. Then one of the Franciscans has a brilliant idea. When a woman comes to confess, he tells her: you know, we friars are owed a tax too, in return for the blessing you receive. What do I have to do? she asks. Well, he says, you already give a tenth of this to your lord and a tenth of that to your parish church. We friars are owed a tenth as well: one in every ten times you sleep with your husband. So keep count, my dear, and come here to pay your share. The whole thing is a trick, but the woman is completely convinced. She pays the tax. [N-002] After a while the other Franciscans discover the trick, and all the women learn that the tax on nights of love has to be paid; they are all thoroughly convinced and, eager to save their souls, come to the monastery to pay the tax. The tale ends badly: the husbands find out, take it rather badly, and burn down the monastery with all the friars inside, and that is how it ends.

It's a joke, of course. It's a story. It shows us that these people think about sex and know that there is a connection between sex and power, that sex is bound up with complicated relationships and all sorts of conflicts. But the right of the first night as we know it? Not a trace. So let's try another test.

## 3. Peasant obligations and the documentary silence

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00281–U-00352 -->

The *ius primae noctis* is supposed to be one of the things a peasant owes his lord. So do we know what those duties and payments were? Which ones weighed most heavily, which ones people resented, which ones they rebelled against? We certainly do. We have a vast collection of records of medieval peasants' complaints against their lords. All kinds of conflicts show up in them. Take guard duty. Every night, someone has to stand guard on the castle tower, so each family has to send a man when its turn comes. It's a serious nuisance. As peasants become more prosperous, they start looking for ways to get rid of duties like this.

Then there are the heavier costs. All the village land belongs to the lord. The peasants have a permanent right to work it, but they pay rent. The peasants can also buy and sell their rights to the land, but the land still belongs to the lord, and they have to pay the lord a certain tax when they buy or sell his lands. And in many areas ruled by lords the lord says: when my man dies, if he has children, yes, they inherit the lands that are mine; but if he has no sons, I take the lands back. I do not allow one of my peasants to decide for himself, make a will, and leave the land perhaps to a woman, who might then marry somebody from another village, and nobody knows what happens on my lands. No wills.

Eventually the peasants get tired of all this, and very often—indeed, almost everywhere; there is barely a village in Europe where sooner or later in the Middle Ages this does not happen—the peasants sit down at a table with the lord, put money on the table, and say: we would like to abolish this obligation, that one, that one, and that one; in exchange, we will pay. And that's what happens. They write out a formal agreement on parchment. Everything is recorded. If the lord refuses, the peasants may go to the king and tell him: we are not doing well under our lord. Why not? Because he demands this, that, and the other. This one and this one were already paid by our grandfathers, fair enough, but this other thing he is demanding is new, an abuse. We do not want to do this. The king intervenes and judges. The lawyers become involved, the peasants pay a lawyer, and the lawyers submit their arguments. In the archives we have enormous bundles containing lists of all the obligations of these peasants who cannot bear any more and are prepared to do anything to eliminate them. If the lawsuit fails, the peasants may rebel. They rebel and perhaps even kill the lord, or, more often, the lord runs away. Then after a while an agreement is reached again; perhaps somebody has been killed, but in the end peace is made and everything is written down once more. I've mentioned only a few of the many obligations weighing on medieval peasants, and people complain about every one of them and pay to be freed from every one of them.

You already see where this is going: the right of the first night is never mentioned. There is not one list of peasant complaints that refers to this obligation. These people, who are willing to pay a large amount of money to be rid of the nuisance of having to do one shift of work every week in the castle ditches, never so much as mention the right of the first night.

A word here about how historians work. Missing evidence has to be handled carefully. We cannot simply say: nobody mentions this, so it never existed. That's a basic rule of the profession. But suppose we have records where people would surely have mentioned it if it had existed, and still find nothing. Then that silence becomes hard to explain. For almost the whole of the Middle Ages, we find no evidence of this supposed legal right. It is only in the fifteenth century that the recognizable story begins to emerge. There are a few earlier hints, though, that help us understand how people began to invent it.

## 4. The thirteenth-century evidence from Verson

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00353–U-00419 -->

Let’s look at an early hint. It begins with a document from 1247, in France.

It lists the usual duties and payments of the peasants in a village. Half the village belongs to a lord outside the Church, and half to the monks of a monastery. Because of this, the lord and the monks keep arguing—that piece is mine, this one is yours—so eventually they draw up a formal list of all the obligations. The village is called Verson, and one of its rules is this. When a peasant marries off his daughter, if she marries a young man from another village, he has to pay a tax. There's nothing unusual about this. It is a very common rule, and I’ll explain why. Follow the logic. The peasant works in the village and works the lord's land, and pays him rent. When his daughter marries, perhaps he gives her part of that land as her marriage gift; the son-in-law will continue to work that land and pay the lord the rent, so there is no problem. But if the son-in-law is from another village, who can assure the lord that he will manage to make him pay the rent, that he really will work, that he will come to live there? Nobody. It's another village under another lord. So the lord of Verson, like the lords of countless other medieval villages, is not pleased if his peasants' daughters marry a young man from another village. And so he says: if you insist, you have to pay for my permission.

This tax, then, is extremely widespread. But we are telling the specific story of the village of Verson in 1247. There is this list in Latin setting out all these duties and payments.

Around 1270, a monk from the monastery, apparently with time on his hands, writes a little rhyming poem in French that lists those same obligations: he translates this list of obligations into French and into verse, and adds something of his own. When he reaches the tax that peasants have to pay when their daughter marries a young man from another village, the monk says: today three coins are paid, but the peasants are happy with that, because once it was much worse. There was a time when so much had to be paid that the peasants sometimes could not manage it, and there were fathers who, rather than having to give the lord an animal, a vineyard, or a regular payment, chose the alternative recalled by the poem: “Long ago it happened that the peasant took his daughter by the hand and delivered her to his lord.”

The implied bargain is that the father hands over his daughter for the lord to sleep with, in exchange for cancelling the tax. Among the millions of medieval texts and documents, this is one of the earliest hints of something that vaguely resembles the right of the first night. Why only vaguely? Because it is not spoken of as a right, and it is spoken of as something the father does so as not to have to pay the heavy tax. But the crucial point is that already in this early mention—as we will see, it will happen regularly afterward—the right of the first night, or something resembling it, forgive me, something resembling it, is discussed by saying: in the bad old days. And this is the key to understanding how, at the end of the Middle Ages, people begin to imagine what happened in the bad old days.

## 5. The legend spreads in Europe

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00420–U-00598 -->

By the end of the Middle Ages, there were many villages and towns founded centuries earlier by groups of peasants. They had agreed to leave their villages, say goodbye to their lords, and build a town of their own where they could live freely. This happens constantly in the Middle Ages. In some regions it is more common than in others. For a mix of reasons, it's especially common in Piedmont, for example, where several towns, from Cuneo to Alessandria and Cherasco, were founded this way.

Cuneo gives us a good example. Three hundred years have passed since the town was founded. At the end of the fifteenth century a learned man from Cuneo, the lawyer Rebaccini, decides to write Cuneo's history and tells how Cuneo came into being: how our ancestors, long ago, decided to abandon their lords, leave their villages, and go to live in a new town. And the lawyer Rebaccini says they left because life under the lords was hard, and he begins to list a whole series of demands that the lords inflicted on their peasants: they did not allow them to make wills; they forced them to pay whenever someone had to sell land; they took a share of the harvest and demanded other taxes; on every contract they took a share; they forced them to work for free. Rebaccini says they made them work without restraint—as if a little restraint would have made it all right, but no. And notice that all these complaints correspond exactly to what documents from three hundred years earlier really tell us: these obligations and this burden existed in the lands ruled by lords. Rebaccini goes on and on about how bad those old days were, how tyrannical those feudal lords were, and then at the very end, when everything is truly finished, finally: “subordinating reason to lust and sensuality, they deflowered their subjects' daughters and likewise their brides, persuading the subjects that this was their ancient right and a privilege granted to them.” As with Rebaccini in Cuneo, so too in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in several Italian and French towns, there is some good citizen who sets about telling the history of the town and begins with the time when, three hundred years earlier, four hundred years earlier, their great-great-grandfathers rebelled against the wicked lords. And by now this idea starts creeping into many of their accounts. Those lords really were wicked, and they even claimed this. If someone did not pay, they took the girls' virginity.

In other words, by the time we reach the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries—and therefore between the end of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance—we suddenly find ourselves facing a Europe in which the tale of the right of the first night has entered circulation and is beginning to resurface. And it always, always resurfaces with this characteristic: it speaks of an extremely distant past. In the bad old days these things happened.

No—that's not quite right. It is not always the bad old days among us that people talk about. There is another way in which the tale of the right of the first night circulates in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe. Not among us in the bad old days, but now among the savages.

Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe—here's the quick context—is the Europe of the great geographical discoveries. During the fifteenth century the Portuguese venture out into the Atlantic; they cross over, go down below the Cape of Good Hope, and discover new lands everywhere, and then, of course, at the end of the fifteenth century Columbus sets out and reaches America, and then in the early sixteenth century the Spanish conquerors follow. A whole series of new worlds comes into view. Each time sailors arrive in a new place, they publish reports describing what that place is like. These reports by Portuguese and Spanish sailors and conquerors all resemble one another. Here is how they describe the people they encounter: the inhabitants of these islands are savages. You cannot imagine how savage they are. They go naked, they have no morality, they do the worst things. Just imagine that among them, when someone marries, the village chief has the right to sleep with the girl; and sometimes not just the village chief: if a merchant marries, all the merchants go; if a nobleman marries, all the noblemen; if instead he is a commoner, the master goes, naturally. When the Portuguese discover the Canaries in the mid-fifteenth century, they tell this story. Alvise Cadamosto reports a story like this about Tenerife, although he did not visit the island himself. When Cortés goes to Mexico he tells—or rather, earlier still, in Cuba—he tells that this used to happen in Cuba.

Why do the conquerors tell these stories? Because they are enslaving these peoples, if not wiping them out. Their justification is that they are bringing reason and the true faith. So they need to show how badly these peoples supposedly need them. That is their argument: we are bringing civilization to people who are less than human, who do unimaginable things. Of course, no Spaniard or Portuguese telling these stories imagines such things happening in his own world. If he did, he would hardly present them as something so strange. But when savages are discussed, anything goes.

But things are never quite that simple. These books appear, a great many appear, people believe them, and there is also someone who protests. Cortés's report saying that these wicked things happened in Cuba is published by Francisco López de Gómara in the *General History of the West Indies and the New Lands Discovered*, and someone protests, because in that strange Europe, after all, there is also someone who says: but none of this is true; we are wronging these Indians, they must be defended, what we are doing to them is shameful. Bartolomé de las Casas, a Dominican friar and bishop, accuses Gómara of spreading lies about Indigenous peoples. The crown later bans the book, but we cannot say that Las Casas personally caused the ban. [N-005] In the meantime, however, it has been translated into French, Montaigne reads it, everyone reads it, and in short these stories continue to circulate.

So let's take stock. In fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe the story of the right of the first night circulates widely. There are two possibilities: did it happen among us in the bad old days, or does it happen now among the savages? Among the savages, among the barbarians—because everyone, after all, can have whichever barbarian they want.

In England around the time of Queen Elizabeth and Shakespeare, there is a country that good English people think ought to be highly civilized, but has sunk into terrible wickedness. That country is Italy, naturally. It is Italy where, on the one hand, the pope rules—and for good English Protestants the authority of the pope is the worst thing in the world—and at the same time in Italy there is Machiavelli, whom they see as a man without morals. There: for the English in the early seventeenth century, Italy is the most immoral country in the world. Around 1619 or 1620, a play by two successful authors, John Fletcher and Philip Massinger, is staged in London. It is entitled *The Custom of the Country*. And it tells of a country, a town so barbaric that in that town the governor demands the right of the first night over all the women who marry there, until naturally a couple more energetic than the others rebels, et cetera, et cetera. Where is that town? Italy, naturally.

In short, for intellectuals, writers, and the wider public of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the right of the first night has become a powerful way to describe all those worlds we do not like, which we consider barbaric, whether the distant medieval past or the present of savages or Italians, depending on the case. That is not all. At this point something else happens that helps strengthen the belief that in the bad old days the right of the first night really existed.

## 6. Jurists, marriage taxes, and false etymologies

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00599–U-00735 -->

The sixteenth century belongs to the legal scholars. They begin taking a close look at the duties and payments peasants owe their lords—the feudal customs, as people begin to call them at the time. In France, for example, these scholars are in the king's service, and the king is very happy to cut the lords' power down a little. To them, the lords’ power begins to look outdated, something that should be brought to an end. Remember: in 1789, after two and a half centuries of reasoning of this kind, finally, with the French Revolution, the first thing they do is abolish the feudal system. All this begins in the sixteenth century. These scholars investigate and discover that in many areas ruled by lords a tax is paid on marriage.

These are educated men, but they also know how to have fun. They too like slightly dirty stories. It is the century of Aretino; it is the century of Rabelais. At the universities there is much joking and amusement, and all these learned men were students once, and, in short, they also know that very often, when a peasant pays a tax, it is because he once had to do a job for the lord and later paid to be freed from it. We have already seen how this works.

Now the story begins to produce its own apparent evidence. Picture one of these legal scholars at a desk piled high with law books. He’s extremely learned, but also ready to believe anything.
His job is to explain why this tax is paid. There is a tax on marriages. When the peasant marries off his daughter, he has to pay. Could this payment have replaced something the peasant once had to do? And of course it must be so. Wherever they find a marriage tax, these scholars imagine that the right of the first night existed before it. Soon whole books present this as an established fact. By now, people take it for granted that this happened in the past. Historians read these legal scholars and think: surely they must know. And if a piece is missing, well, the historians invent it.

There is a book that has enormous influence. Hector Boece's *History of Scotland* was first printed in 1527. [N-007] Boece is an educated Renaissance scholar, but here is an idea of how reliable he is. According to him Scotland was founded by a princess named Scotia who was the daughter of the pharaoh, the one from Moses, the one in the Bible. In this work, whose credibility you can appreciate, Boece says: among us in Scotland peasants pay a marriage tax. This tax was introduced by the good King Malcolm in the eleventh century, on the advice of his wife, the saintly Queen Margaret, to replace an earlier tax—no, not a tax, an earlier obligation, a wicked, filthy obligation that I do not even dare tell you what it was—which had been introduced so many centuries earlier, in the days of the barbarians, the Celts, by King Evenus of Scotland: the obligation whereby, indeed, the girls with the lord, et cetera, et cetera, et cetera. At this point, you can only despair, because—no, for heaven's sake—King Malcolm existed, and his wife Margaret too; obviously they never did anything of the kind. King Evenus goes hand in hand with Princess Scotia, daughter of the pharaoh. He is completely made up, because when these people do not know how to fill the gaps, they invent. And books circulate; everyone reads books.

Medieval taxes had all kinds of local names. In France, a tax collected on various occasions—including, sometimes, marriage—could be called *coillage*. When the clerks who drew up legal documents translated that word into Latin, some wrote *culagium*. To speakers of Spanish, French, and Italian, *culagium* sounded like it came from the word for “ass.” Now imagine a sixteenth-century legal scholar discovering that a peasant had to pay a marriage tax with a name that seemed to mean something like “ass tax.” They could hardly contain themselves.

Another example begins with a tax to feed the lord’s horses. In Italy it was called *fodro*, a word related to the English “fodder,” meaning food for animals.
Centuries later, scholars had forgotten where the name came from. In Nizza Monferrato, an old legend told of peasants rebelling against a wicked lord. Then an eighteenth-century scholar named Giulio Cesare Cordara found records of a tax called *fodro*. He decided the word must be related to the Italian *fodero*, meaning “sheath”—the cover for a sword. From there he made quite a leap: the sheath must mean the vagina, and the tax must have replaced the lord's right to sleep with local brides. He even wrote a poem about it, *The Sheath, or the Ancient Lords of Nizza Monferrato's Right to Brides*.

Now take one more small step. Once everyone has convinced themselves that these rights existed in the past, they go looking for further proof, and when you go looking for further proof, you find it.

## 7. The Church and the first-night marriage tax

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00736–U-00784 -->

Here's an example: a document that countless scholars have cited as proof that the right of the first night existed in the Middle Ages, and that even the Church demanded it from its peasants. The starting point is a trial, a trial held before the Parlement of Paris, a royal court. It concerns the area under the bishop of Amiens. We are at the end of the fourteenth century. What's the background? In the Middle Ages, the Church, as it does today and perhaps even more than today—indeed, often more than today—makes an effort to remind Christians that sex within marriage is acceptable, but it has to be kept under control because its purpose is to have children. Consequently, one must not let oneself go too much in sex, even within marriage; indeed, learning to control desire is good and proper.

In some parts of medieval Europe, bishops begin telling people that it would be a fine thing if, when they marry, they went without sex for three days, so as to show that they know how to master their lust and that they are not marrying for that. In some areas, in one way or another, this becomes an obligation imposed by the Church. When you marry, you cannot sleep together for three days.

Then comes the late Middle Ages. Before the year 1000, the economy barely grows. Afterward, everyone starts making money, huge amounts of it circulate, and people become much more practical—and much more cynical. You pay for everything. In the late Middle Ages many people begin to say: all right, I'm getting married and I'm supposed to go without sex for three days, but what if instead I made an offering to the Church? And the Church says: splendid, make the offering. After a while there is nobody left who goes without sex for three days, but there are areas where it is normal for a person who marries to have to pay a certain tax to the Church. What do they call it? Various things, but mostly the tax on the first wedding night. Because that is precisely what I am paying for.

By the end of the fourteenth century—and this too is an old story—in the area under the bishop of Amiens people no longer pay it. The bishop is annoyed and brings a lawsuit to force people to continue paying this tax. The people protest; the matter goes to the royal court in Paris; a trial follows that lasts sixteen years, and in the end I no longer even remember what the court decides. Whatever the outcome, you can picture the trial record: huge bundles packed with legal arguments in Latin. When eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historians discover this trial, it is not as though they sit down and read the whole thing—you really cannot understand a thing—but one thing is clear: it speaks of a tax on the first night of marriage, and it is the Church that demands the tax on the first night of marriage from the peasants. And they take that as proof that the right of the first night existed.

## 8. Lords adopt the legend and marriage rituals blur it

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00785–U-00862 -->

And now perhaps the strangest development: between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, more or less everyone becomes convinced that in the bad old days the right of the first night existed. The lords hear these stories too. They still rule their lands, and will continue to do so until the French Revolution abolishes the feudal system. The lords are less powerful than before; the king has taken away a good many powers and sources of income from them. Nevertheless, the peasant still always has to deal with the lord and pays many taxes. The lords learn that this right of the first night existed in the past. And they think: wait a minute—maybe this right existed on my lands too. My ancestors—my ancestors certainly had the right. That leads to two possible reactions. A scholar writing in the early seventeenth century reports a conversation with Cardinal della Rovere, whose family held lands in Piedmont. He writes: “Such a custom formerly existed in Piedmont among pagans and gentiles, and the Most Illustrious Cardinal Hieronimo della Rovere told me that he himself had burned the privilege that his house possessed concerning it.” So he says he burned his family’s document claiming that right. But who knows what it really contained? A marriage tax, the *culagium*—who knows? The cardinal took it at face value; since he was a cardinal, he burned it. In our civilized times such things are no longer done.

Other lords aren't cardinals, and they are less troubled by the idea. Some lords are constantly fighting with their peasants, because it is all very well to say these are the rights, these are the obligations; people do not want to pay taxes, and so they quarrel continually and go to court. Sometimes a lord is summoned by a court to state what, in his view, his rights are. Beginning in the fifteenth century, a few cases have been identified, five in all as far as I know: one in the fifteenth century, three in the sixteenth, one at the end of the seventeenth, still under the Sun King, in various godforsaken places. A local lord, summoned by a court to declare what his rights and his peasants' obligations are, says: they pay me this, they pay me that, the lamb at Easter and the pig at Christmas, ten coins when they buy land, twenty coins when they move house, five coins when a daughter marries; and those five coins when a daughter marries are because I would have the right to sleep with her on the first night, but my ancestors agreed to accept money instead, and so now I... though I could always start demanding this again if my peasants do not behave themselves.

So the fantasy doesn't quite become reality, but it does become something people can imagine enforcing. There are five among the hundreds of thousands of lords who lived in Europe who quarrel so much with their peasants that they go and tell them: look, if you do not stop making me angry, I'll bring out the right of the first night.

To understand why people could believe these claims, we need to look at weddings themselves. In Europe at the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the modern period, marriage is a very public affair. It comes with customs, payments, and conflicts that people generally accept, even when they cause trouble. Here's an example. A girl from the village marries someone from another town, another village. We have already said that this annoys the lord and that a payment therefore has to be made. But it also annoys the other young men of the village. She is one of ours and one of those others has come to take her and is carrying her away. It is normal, when one of their female companions marries and goes to live in another village, for the young men of the village to have the road blocked when the wedding procession is about to leave. The road is blocked, all the young men of the village are there, and they tell the bridegroom: if you want to take her away, buy us a drink. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, the bridegroom knows the drill and gives in and buys everyone a drink. Then they remove the barrier and let him pass. One time in a hundred the bridegroom is someone who has already had a little to drink, becomes angry, they fight, and someone is killed.

The wedding banquet and the first wedding night invite the same kind of ritual conflict. The banquet: everyone comes. In the novelist Falcones's imagination, the lord happens to turn up by chance; he does not know that the peasant is getting married. Come on. Everyone knows about these things, and the lord is usually invited to the banquet, most certainly, because it is important to keep him happy; and he too needs to keep wealthy peasants happy, so he goes—he will later baptize their children. He goes to the banquet and is keen to go to the banquet and sit in the place of honor. Every now and again there is also some lord who quarrels with the peasants because they invite him but do not give him sufficiently good wine. He is supposed to drink better wine, and he has the right to drink the best wine at the banquet, not the wine they give second-class guests. And then, when the bride finally goes to bed with her husband, there are all sorts of customs. Generally all the male guests prepare the husband and the women prepare the wife. They undress them, put their nightshirts on them, play tricks on them, make a few rude jokes, and then finally shut them in the room. There are places where the lord says: I am the one who has the right to lead the bride to the bedroom, because I am the lord of this village and it must be seen.

So marriage comes with all sorts of public customs and things people owe to the wider community. And it is in this context that, if scholars believe there used to be a right of the first night, well, it does not take much for someone to begin believing it in earnest too.

## 9. Power, abuse, and Orwell's invented past

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00863–U-00913 -->

Let's wrap up. The *ius primae noctis* is an extraordinary fantasy born at the end of the Middle Ages. People believed in it so strongly that there was a risk someone might try to put it into practice. But there is no evidence that anyone ever did. The right itself never existed.

Does that mean a lord never sleeps with a village girl? No. The lord sleeps with the village girl, by fair means or foul. That kind of thing happens in every unequal society. But there's an enormous difference between a society where a rich man exploits a poor woman and one where the law supposedly gives him that right and everyone has to accept it. The second is sheer madness. Saying that lords slept with village girls is like saying that, in the nineteenth century, the owner of a textile mill or his son sometimes slept with a woman who worked there. There were a thousand reasons why this could happen, but nobody would have dreamed of calling it a right. To speak of it as a law means wanting to invent a past.

So I'll close with an extraordinary passage about inventing the past. It is taken from Orwell's novel *1984*. At one point the novel shows how, under Big Brother's regime, the past is reinvented for the people. People are taught that in the past there were evil creatures called capitalists, who oppressed everyone in every possible way. Orwell also mentions the supposed right of the first night, something that probably wouldn’t appear in a children’s textbook. Here are his exact words: It was the law by which every capitalist had the right to sleep with any woman working in one of his factories.

That is fantastic because Orwell has identified the mechanism exactly.
Something that can happen in reality, and is an act of violence and a result of unequal power, is imagined to have been a law. A society that could make such a thing legal feels utterly alien, completely barbaric by our standards. So when we rewrite the past in order to feel good about living in a much better world, the right of the first night is perfect for furnishing that past. Thank you.
