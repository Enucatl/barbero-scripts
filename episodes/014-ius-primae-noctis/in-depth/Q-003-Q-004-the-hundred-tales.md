# Q-003 and Q-004: *The Hundred Tales*

## Research question

Barbero uses two stories from *Les Cent Nouvelles nouvelles* to illustrate that late medieval
comic tales openly connected sex and social power without representing *ius primae noctis* as a
recognized seigneurial right:

- Q-003, U-00205–U-00242: a count is outwitted by a maiden using a half-removed garment.
- Q-004, U-00265–U-00275: Franciscan friars invent a sexual tithe and are burned in their
  monastery when the husbands discover it.

Earlier research identified the tales but lacked dependable page-level wording. The supplied
English edition resolves their plots and exposes several discrepancies in Barbero's retelling.

## Edition examined

- Title: *The Hundred Tales (Les Cent Nouvelles Nouvelles)*
- Original compiler: anonymous Burgundian compiler, formerly attributed to Antoine de la Sale
- Translator: Rossell Hope Robbins
- Illustrator: Alexander Dobkin
- Publisher: Bonanza Books, New York; originally published by Crown Publishers, New York
- Copyright year: 1960
- Library of Congress number: 60-15389
- Internet Archive identifier: `hundredtaleslesc00robb`
- Extent: complete translation of all one hundred tales
- Digital witness: searchable PDF with OCR and page images

The reported pagination map is front matter pp. i–xxvi at PDF pages 1–30, main text pp. 1–379 at
PDF pages 31–409, and appendix and bibliography pp. 381–390 at PDF pages 411–420. Consequently,
PDF page numbers in the main text are thirty greater than printed page numbers.

This is an English translation. It can establish plot, setting, character identity, and Robbins's
wording, but it cannot establish the precise Middle French vocabulary or syntax.

## Q-003: Tale XXIV, “The Boot”

### Identities and setting

The pursuer is Count Waleran, Count of Saint-Pol, called “The Handsome.” He is lord of the village
of Vrelenchem in the castellany of Lille. A doctor and follower acts as his procurer or
go-between. The object of the count's pursuit is described as a young and chaste maiden.

The encounter takes place while the maiden is cutting grass in an isolated clearing in a wood.

### Proposition and answer

The doctor offers presents, distinction, and advancement for the maiden's family. Robbins renders
her response as follows:

> The doctor did as he said. He went to the lovely girl and greeted her very civilly. No less
> modest and dutiful than beauteous, she politely returned his greeting. In short, after striking
> up an acquaintanceship, the good procurer made a long oration about the presents and distinction
> his lord offered her; in fact, if she liked, she would be the means of enriching and elevating
> her whole family. The chaste maiden knew what he was driving at. Her reply was just like herself,
> that is to say, gracious and dignified: In regard to his lordship, the count, she was, saving his
> honor, one who wanted to obey him, respect him, and serve him in all things. But what he would
> seek of her was against her virtue, which she held as dear as her life. She did not know him, and
> she would do no more for him than a devil for the damned!

Locator: printed p. 93, PDF p. 123. No OCR uncertainty was reported.

### The count's pressure

> “Now, there, missy,” then said our lordly gallant, who was all eager, “you're a little too high
> and mighty. So you can't be won without a siege! Well, then, look to your defenses, for the
> battle's beginning. Before you get away from me, you'll have to make amends on my terms and
> wishes for all the inconvenience and trouble I've suffered and endured for love of you.”

Locator: printed p. 94, PDF p. 124. This supports Barbero's paraphrase that she cannot escape, but
it is not a formal threat of death.

### The boot trick

> “Since I must obey you,” said she, “I ask that you do me the honor not to dirty me with your dirty
> riding boots, which are covered with mud and dung, and then I'll satisfy you as to the rest.”
> “And how can I get them off?” asked milord. “I'll take them off for you very readily, if it please
> you,” she replied. “For, on my word, I couldn't bring myself to make you happy while you're
> wearing those filthy riding boots.” “Boots don't make much difference,” said the count.
> “Nevertheless, if that's what you want, they'll be taken off.” Then he released his grip on her
> and, sitting down on the grass, extended his leg. The winsome lass took off his spurs and then
> tugged at one of his riding boots, which were extremely tight-fitting and very difficult to get
> on or off. She purposely pulled it at a wrong angle so that it stuck about halfway off. Then she
> ran away as fast as her legs could carry her, encouraged and sustained by resolute determination
> to escape the gallant count, and didn't stop running till she reached her father's house.

Locator: printed p. 95, PDF p. 125. No OCR uncertainty was reported.

The garment in Robbins's translation is unambiguously a tight-fitting riding boot. Barbero's
trousers or breeches are therefore a material alteration of the mechanism, although the half-off
garment and resulting escape are preserved.

### Marriage and reward

> To hear him, the girl who'd tricked him wouldn't be long for this world, so much he wanted to do
> her harm. No matter what he felt like doing right then and no matter how angry he was for a time,
> after he had cooled off a little, however, all his rage turned into cordial good will. And this
> must have been true, since, at his insistence and at considerable cost and expense, he arranged a
> wealthy and favorable marriage for her as a reward only for her frankness and purity, which he
> knew through personal experience, by the rebuff which has just been narrated.

Locator: printed pp. 95–96, PDF pp. 125–126.

### Assessment

Barbero accurately preserves the proposition, the maiden's defense of her honor, the isolated
encounter, the trick with a garment stuck halfway off, the escape, and the favorable marriage
arranged at the count's expense. He omits that the intermediary is a doctor and changes the riding
boot into trousers or breeches.

Q-003 is therefore `resolved` and `confirmed-in-substance`, with high confidence. It remains a
`paraphrase` and `source_replacement: not-applicable` because Barbero summarizes a long narrative
rather than translating one clean passage.

## Q-004: Tale XXXII, “The Women Who Paid Tithes”

### Setting and claimed tithe

Robbins places the story in Hostalrich, Catalonia, not Girona. The perpetrators are banished
Franciscan friars of the Order of the Observance. They do not invoke papal authorization. They
represent the tithe as a spiritual obligation owed in return for the Holy Sacrament:

> They made known to all the women in the town that, without exception, they were obligated before
> God to pay tithe on all their possessions. “Just as you render to the lord of the manor a tenth
> part of such and such, and to your parish and curé a tithe of this and that other, so to us friars
> must you pay a tenth of the number of times you have intercourse with your husbands. We will take
> no other tithe from you; as you know, we have no money at all, and we ask for none, since the
> temporal and transitory goods of this world are nothing to us. We seek and ask only spiritual
> rewards. The tithes which we demand of you, and which you owe us, are not temporal blessings;
> they are in return for the Holy Sacrament you receive, something divine and spiritual. No one has
> the right to receive this tithe except us alone, friars of the Order of Observants.”

Locator: printed pp. 136 and 138, PDF pp. 166 and 168. No OCR uncertainty was reported.

The discontinuous printed pages reported for this passage should be checked against the PDF before
publication, but the supplied extraction is clear about both the one-in-ten obligation and its
claimed sacramental basis.

### Discovery and scale

A wife explains the supposed obligation to her husband:

> “At least you know very well,” she continued, “that I have to pay the tithe.” “What tithe?”
> “Indeed, it's been going on for a mighty long time,” she said, “a tenth of our nights together.
> You get off easy, for I've got to pay it for both of us.”

She says that some sixteen or twenty women pay Brother Eustace and that Brothers Bartholomew,
James, Anthony, and every other friar have their own allotments. Some women prepay and receive
credit for future encounters. Locator: printed p. 139, PDF p. 169.

### Burning of the monastery

> After many diverse opinions were expressed, the last and final decision they took was to go and
> set fire to the monastery and burn both friars and establishment together. They went down into
> the town and came to the monastery. They removed thence the Blessed Sacrament and all the
> reliquaries and bore them to the parish church. Then, without further inquiry, they set fire in
> several places, and never left until everything was consumed by the flames—friars, monastery,
> church, dormitory, and the rest of the buildings, of which there were quite a number. Thus the
> poor Franciscans paid a very high price for the unusual tithe which they imposed. God Himself
> could do nothing about it, and indeed had His house burned down.

The supplied result labels this printed p. 174 and PDF p. 174. That cannot be correct under the
edition's reported thirty-page offset and would place the end of Tale XXXII far outside the tale's
surrounding pages. The consistent locator is printed p. 144, PDF p. 174. This inferred correction
must be checked against the page image during human review.

### Assessment

Barbero accurately preserves the Franciscan order, the analogy with ordinary tithes, the sexual
tithe of one encounter in ten, its distribution among multiple friars, its discovery, and the
burning of both friars and monastery. He introduces two unsupported details:

- The story is set in Hostalrich, not Girona.
- The friars appeal to sacramental and spiritual authority, not a grant from the pope.

Q-004 is therefore `resolved` and `confirmed-in-substance`, with high confidence. It remains a
`paraphrase` and `source_replacement: not-applicable` because the lecture compresses an extended
narrative.

## Evidence limits and editorial consequence

The Robbins translation establishes that the two tales and their main narrative sequences are
correctly identified. It also supports accuracy review for the boot/trousers, Hostalrich/Girona,
and sacramental/papal discrepancies.

It does not recover the Middle French text. Accordingly, both ledger entries retain
`original_language: frm` and a null `original_text`; their `translation` fields record exact
excerpts from Robbins and disclose the translation dependency. Neither result should be treated as
evidence for the precise medieval vocabulary. Both remain `human_reviewed: false` pending a check
against the supplied PDF.
