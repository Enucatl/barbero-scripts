# Q-007 and Q-012: Boureau's *The Lord's First Night*

## Research scope

This pass examined the English translation of Alain Boureau's monograph for the two remaining
deferred quotation targets:

- Q-007: Barbero's composite account of marriage customs reported in the Canaries, Tenerife, and
  Cuba.
- Q-012: the report that Cardinal Girolamo della Rovere burned a family parchment granting a
  supposed first-night right.

The Boureau pass substantially narrowed both searches. It identified the Gómara–Montaigne source
chain and Bonifacio Vannozzi's work and page, but did not itself mention Cadamosto or reproduce
Vannozzi's Della Rovere anecdote. A subsequent check of Cadamosto's voyage narrative recovered the
Tenerife report, and a subsequent examination of Vannozzi volume 2, page 253 recovered the Della
Rovere passage. Both entries are therefore resolved.

## Q-012: Bonifacio Vannozzi and Cardinal della Rovere

### What Boureau establishes

In chapter 1, Boureau discusses Edward Westermarck's late-nineteenth-century catalogue of alleged
first-night customs. He writes:

> The first volume of Westermarck's History is in fact aimed at refuting the notion of sexual
> promiscuity. Chapter 5—which is preceded by a chapter on sexual incontinence before marriage and
> followed by one on exchange practices within the institution of marriage—is dedicated to the jus
> primae noctis, for Westermarck merely a residual, dubious, and limited form of sexual life. He
> rejects the reality of a medieval droit de cuissage, accepting only a limited amount of evidence
> in rapidly listed instances from Brazil, the Caribbean, Senegal, Herodotus' Libya, Morocco,
> Kurdistan, Cambodia, and Malabar. When he speaks of Europe, he repeats the anecdote of King
> Evenus, mentions Bonifacio Vannozzi in the seventeenth century, and adds a few notes regarding the
> Celtic and Albanian frontier lands. His choice not only displays the persistence of an
> uncritical use of unverified sources on the part of a modern ethnologist, but also reveals a
> specific orientation...

Locator: printed p. 25, PDF p. 39, chapter 1, “The Idol of Origins: Ethnographic
Interpretations of the Droit de Cuissage.”

Boureau's chronological bibliography supplies the primary reference:

> 1610 Bonifacio Vannozzi. Della suppellettile degli avvertimenti politici, morali e christiani.
> Bologna: eredi di G. Rossi, 1609–13. Vol. 2 (1610), p. 253.

Locator: printed p. 279, PDF p. 293.

### What Boureau does not establish

Boureau does not name Cardinal Girolamo della Rovere in the relevant discussion, quote Vannozzi,
translate the alleged conversation, or describe the burning of a parchment. His text identifies
Vannozzi as one of Westermarck's uncritically used European references and provides the original
work and page.

The submitted Boureau analysis also supplied details about Vannozzi's personal conversation with
Cardinal Girolamo della Rovere and the cardinal throwing an ancient grant into the fire. Those
details were not present in Boureau and could not initially be certified from that PDF alone. They
were subsequently confirmed in the supplied scan of Vannozzi, volume 2, page 253; see
`Q-012-vannozzi-della-rovere.md`.

### Editorial decision

Q-012 is `resolved` and `confirmed`, with high confidence. Vannozzi explicitly says that Cardinal
Hieronimo della Rovere told him he had personally burned the privilege held by his family. The
surrounding paragraph explicitly construes the privilege as sexual access to brides. This confirms
the reported conversation but not the historical existence or exercise of the alleged charter.

## Q-007: Gómara, Montaigne, Cuba, and the missing Cadamosto claim

### Montaigne's social hierarchy

Boureau quotes the 1588 addition to Montaigne's *Essais*, book 1, chapter 23, using Jacob Zeitlin's
English translation:

> And in another place, if it is a merchant who marries, all the merchants invited to the wedding
> go to bed with the bride before him; and the greater their number, the greater is her honour and
> her reputation for strength and capacity; and if an officer marry, it is the same; the same if it
> is a nobleman, and so of all others except labourers or men of mean condition, for then it is the
> lord's privilege; and yet a severe loyalty during marriage is strictly enjoined.

Locator: Boureau, printed p. 21, PDF p. 35. Note 30, at printed p. 245 / PDF p. 259, identifies
Pierre Villey and V.-L. Saulnier's French edition and Jacob Zeitlin's 1934 English translation.

This is a close match for Barbero's hierarchy of merchants, nobles, and a lord according to the
husband's social status. The immediate source is Montaigne, not a generalized Portuguese travel
report.

### Gómara's Cuban account

Boureau traces Montaigne's addition to Francisco López de Gómara's account of Cuba through Martin
Fumée's French translation. His analysis explains:

> The fragment that Montaigne picks up is part of a short chapter on the island of Cuba, which
> Cortés visited in 1511 in the first stage of his American conquests. Cortés himself tells López de
> Gómara that his information was secondhand because by the time of his arrival in Cuba, the island
> had lost all its Indian inhabitants to syphilis and alcohol or deportation to New Spain. Cortés's
> informants told him that the original Cubans were in all ways like the natives of Hispaniola
> (Santo Domingo), except that they spoke a different language and went about naked. The only
> detail of their mores that the voyager noted was precisely this episode of savage nuptials.
> Fumée's French translation, which closely follows the Spanish original, presents a social and
> sexual hierarchy quite different from the one Montaigne presents. In Fumée only the tribal chiefs
> (caciques) and merchants practice communal clan sex; “citizens, bourgeois, and workers [citadins,
> bourgeois et laboureurs]” are excluded. Furthermore, it is “the lord or some priest [le Seigneur,
> ou quelque prestre]” who deflowers young women. Montaigne westernizes the hierarchy: In López de
> Gómara the Cuban custom made a special case of castes external to local life (chiefs and
> merchants); Montaigne separates the three groups from the rest of the population.

Locator: printed pp. 22–23, PDF pp. 36–37. Note 33, printed p. 245 / PDF p. 259, identifies Gómara's
1552 edition and Fumée's first French translation of 1569.

The chronological bibliography gives:

- Francisco López de Gómara, *La istoria de las Indias y conquista de México* (Zaragoza: A.
  Millan, 1552), with a modern Madrid edition at p. 74.
- Martin Fumée, trans., *Histoire générale des Indes Occidentales et terres neuves* (Paris: M.
  Sonnius, 1569), p. 122a.
- Michel de Montaigne, *Les essais*, book 1, essay 23; the relevant text first appears in the 1588
  version.

Locator: printed p. 278, PDF p. 292.

### Boureau's interpretation

Boureau does not treat these accounts as transparent descriptions of an actual institution.
Gómara's account participates in a representation of Indigenous peoples as savage, while
Montaigne substantially reshapes its social hierarchy for his meditation on custom. The
transmission history itself demonstrates adaptation rather than independent confirmation.

### Cadamosto's Tenerife report

Boureau does not mention Cadamosto, but G. R. Crone's critical English edition of the voyage
narrative does. Chapters VII–VIII describe the Canaries and then narrow the discussion to Tenerife.
Cadamosto reports:

> Their women are not held in common: but it is lawful for each to take as many as he wishes. They
> do not marry virgins, unless they have first lain one night with the lord, which is held to be a
> great honour.

Locator: Alvise Cadamosto, *The Voyages of Cadamosto and Other Documents on Western Africa in the
Second Half of the Fifteenth Century*, trans. and ed. G. R. Crone (Hakluyt Society, 1937), chapter
VIII, printed p. 13.

The passage is genuine, but Barbero changes its evidentiary status. Cadamosto immediately explains
that this information came through the inhabitants of the four Christian-controlled islands,
raiding expeditions, captives, and released Christian prisoners. At the end of the Canaries
section, he states that he personally visited Gomera and El Hierro and passed Palma without
landing. He does not claim to have visited Tenerife.

The editor also notes that *Le Canarien* and Zurara preserve earlier accounts of the archipelago.
Cadamosto was a Venetian sailing under Portuguese auspices, not a Portuguese discoverer of the
Canaries. His report belongs to a mid-fifteenth-century colonial information network rather than
direct observation on Tenerife.

Laura Sabina González Carracedo's 2023 study independently reads the passage as a representation
of Tenerife and places it within the colonial gendering of Indigenous Canary Islanders, slave
raiding, and European classifications of sexuality.

### Editorial decision

Q-007 is `resolved`, `composite`, and high-confidence. All three transmission components are now
identified:

1. Cadamosto's secondhand Tenerife report.
2. Gómara's secondhand Cuban report through Cortés.
3. Montaigne's transformation of the Cuban report into a hierarchy of merchants, officers,
   nobles, and lords.

`source_replacement` is `not-applicable`: Barbero gives an extended composite summary, and no
single source passage can replace it without erasing the document boundaries.
