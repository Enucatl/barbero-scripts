# The ius primae noctis: A Middle Ages not to believe in — faithful English translation

## 1. The modern image of the right of the first night

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00146 -->

Well, good evening. Tonight's subject may be the best-known of the three we're covering in this series. I think we all have in mind this image that we associate with the Middle Ages, the *ius primae noctis*. I know, I just contradicted myself: we all know the image, but let's define it anyway. The right of the lord of the village to have the virginity of all the girls in the village, or at least all those who are not noblewomen, all the female serfs and peasant women, especially when they marry: to sleep with them before the husband, on the first wedding night. This image is extremely widespread in the way people perceive the Middle Ages today, so much so that we continually come across it whenever there is a historical novel or a film set in the Middle Ages. Maybe “whenever” is too strong, but we run into it constantly. Let me give you two examples—both will probably sound familiar.

The first comes from historical fiction. Back in 2006, I believe, a Spanish author named Ildefonso Falcones publishes a historical novel that becomes a huge success. The novel is called *The Cathedral of the Sea*. It is a grand story set in the Middle Ages, in fourteenth-century Catalonia, a story of struggle for freedom and against oppression, and it opens with a great scene entirely centered on the *ius primae noctis*. Like any serialized story, a historical novel needs a powerful opening—something that grabs you immediately. A dead body is even better, but whatever it is, it has to hit hard. *The Cathedral of the Sea* opens with this extremely violent scene of the *ius primae noctis*. There is a peasant wedding. There is the peasant who is to be married, and they are prosperous peasants, people who work, who are doing well, respectable people. There's a wedding banquet in the countryside. Then, out of nowhere, the lord turns up on horseback, accompanied by his henchmen. Everyone is very frightened that the lord has arrived there, but obviously they can do nothing. They invite him to sit down, and the lord sits at the table with the other guests and begins to drink. He notices that the bride is a beautiful woman and wants her to be the one who pours his wine. He forces her to come over, touches her, and then, when he has drunk his fill, the lord jumps up, takes the bride by the wrist, calls the bridegroom, and tells him: “In exercise of the right that belongs to me as your lord, I have decided to lie with your wife on her first night.” He drags the woman inside, then comes out after a while buttoning his trousers and says to the husband: “Now it is your turn. […] Do your duty as a good Christian husband!” And nobody has lifted a finger. They are all dismayed, horrified by what is happening, but not one of those peasants lifts a finger, because the lord has the right to do what he is doing. Then this *ius primae noctis*, so violent that it is actually rape, will have enormous consequences. The whole novel starts from there, which is simply to say that the *ius primae noctis* is a formidable narrative device. It sets the whole plot in motion: there is the villain, there are the good people who have suffered the abuse, there is the girl who loses her mind after this violence, and after a while the husband leaves the village and goes to live in the city, where at least there is freedom and no lords who have to be obeyed.

Here's one more example—probably another unnecessary one, but never mind: Mel Gibson's *Braveheart*, a movie many of us have seen. A great big medieval film, the story of the rebellion of the Scots under the leadership of the heroic William Wallace against the wicked English rulers. Here too, the *ius primae noctis* comes into play to move the plot forward. It is a most curious scene. The king of England—wicked, naturally—is talking with his barons, and then he says: the problem with Scotland is that it is full of Scots, but I have found the way to put an end to the Scots. The time has come to restore an old tradition. Let us grant the *ius primae noctis*. From now on our nobles will have the right to sleep with Scottish girls before they marry, and in this way the idea is that this will dilute the bloodline of those rebels. Notice the difference. In Falcones's novel the author has rather courageously tried to imagine a world where the *ius primae noctis* exists as something that one cannot avoid accepting—it is an obligation, it is a law, and nobody rebels. In *Braveheart*, the perspective is different—and you'll see later why that matters. The king says: let us restore an old custom. And of course the consequence, when the English begin to demand the *ius primae noctis*, is that the Scots finally rise in revolt.

This is a connection that we find extremely often when the *ius primae noctis* is discussed. There are lots of Italian towns or villages whose tradition, whose legend, includes a distant past in which an evil feudal lord oppresses the inhabitants by demanding the *ius primae noctis*, until naturally a girl braver than the others, or a husband braver than the others, rebels and puts an end to the tyranny. It's a long list, and an internet search turns up even more. How many places have this story in their memory, in their tradition? Fiuggi; Sant'Agata di Puglia; Roccascalegna in Abruzzo; several villages in Liguria, Montalto Ligure, Onzo; several villages in Piedmont, Nizza Monferrato, Rocca Grimalda; even Ivrea, where the Ivrea Carnival—you know, the carnival where they throw oranges—is said today to originate in an episode in which an evil tyrant demands the *ius primae noctis* until a girl cleverer than the others rebels: Violetta, the beautiful miller's daughter.

A quick aside. To imagine how old this story is, you need only consider that in the Middle Ages they have no idea what the name Violetta even is. The name Violetta becomes fashionable in the nineteenth century with *La traviata*. Before that there is nobody called Violetta. End of aside—I don't want to influence your judgment.

So we've established our first point: in today's culture, both in local folklore and in the culture of historical novelists and screenwriters—and therefore also of all of us who watch those films and read those novels—the *ius primae noctis* is a fairly regular presence whenever the Middle Ages are discussed. The whole point of this series is to say: all right, let's check. Let's see what testimony exists in the Middle Ages for the existence of the *ius primae noctis*. Let's go look for it.

## 2. Searching medieval stories for the alleged right

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00147–U-00280 -->

Let me quickly suggest two places to look. We have all this testimony, all these sources handed down from the Middle Ages. Where should the *ius primae noctis* show up?

The first place is literature, in the tales, in that realistic literature which depicts real situations. Tales of the kind written by Boccaccio are enormously successful in the Middle Ages; people love them and write lots of them. We Italians know Boccaccio's tales well, but similar literary genres exist in other countries too. There is an immense production of these texts, which have the following features: they are generally very realistic, they represent real situations of the period and real characters, and they are very explicit. Sex is discussed a great deal, and without anyone having any difficulty about it. Sex is discussed to tell stories of successful tricks, to show how the poor manage to resist the abuses of the rich. Sex is discussed to show how much cleverness counts and how useful it is even in those situations.

Alongside Boccaccio's tales, there's another genre worth pausing over. In France, even before Boccaccio, as early as the thirteenth century, there is a literary genre called the *fabliaux*. They are short poems, short poems in verse, because that is the taste of the time, usually very funny, which rather like our tales recount pranks, jokes, tricks, strange episodes; and there too sex is discussed all the time, I repeat, realistically. There is the village priest who pursues the shoemaker's wife, and everyone knows that in the village the priest is the man who has studied, the one who speaks best, the one with the most money, and therefore, well, he is successful with the female parishioners. There is the passing student given a bed for the night by the miller who, that night, sets his eye on the miller's daughter, and so forth. Here too there is extreme freedom, including freedom of language, in describing these situations.

You'd think that if any source should tell us about the *ius primae noctis*, surely it will be in this medieval storytelling. What a subject, what a narrative device the *ius primae noctis* would be! It is never found. There is not a single medieval tale, not a single medieval joke or story about sex—and there are countless numbers of them—that ever speaks of the *ius primae noctis*. Does that mean they never show a village lord setting his eye on the girls? Of course not, because the village lord certainly does set his eye on the girls.

So how do they handle it? Here's an example, a fifteenth-century French tale. In the fifteenth century a collection of tales comes out in France whose structure borrows from the *Decameron*: there are one hundred of them, and they are called *The Hundred New Tales*, *Les Cent Nouvelles nouvelles*. Tale 24 follows a count who learns that in one of his villages there is a beautiful girl. The count is a good man, but, well, well, he does not pass up opportunities. So he goes to take a look. The girl really is pretty, and she is his subject. So what does the count do? He sends her a go-between, sends one of his servants to tell her: look, the count likes you; if you are clever, if you agree, your fortune is made, yours and your family's. The girl answers: I am a subject of the count; I am ready to obey any order of his except where my honor is concerned. But if they mean to ask me for something contrary to my honor, which I value more highly than my own life—I do not even know the count. The servant goes to the count and reports this answer. The count is disappointed and, since by now he has got the idea into his head, he wanders around the girl's village. Eventually he finds her alone. The girl is alone, cutting grass near the wood. The count arrives and begins to court her somewhat brusquely. The girl does not want to agree, and the count becomes very forward and tells her: listen, keep still, because in any case you are not getting away from me. At this point the girl—and the whole point of the tale, naturally, lies in the girl's device for escaping this embarrassing situation—says she will agree, but asks him not to soil her with his mud-covered riding boots. She offers to remove them herself. The boots are extremely tight and difficult to take off. Once the count sits down and extends his leg, she removes his spurs and deliberately pulls one boot at the wrong angle, leaving it stuck halfway off. Then she runs to safety while he is unable to pursue her. [N-001] The count thinks about it for a while, begins to laugh, decides that the girl has shown spirit and, since he is a good man, in fact draws a line under the matter. Indeed, at the first opportunity he arranges a grand marriage with another nobleman for this girl and gives her a large dowry to reward her for her spirit. If ever there were a tale where there might have been a little hint that all he had to do was say, I have the right, or the *ius primae*—no, nothing.

There are medieval tales in which, every now and again, this idea of sexual performance as a kind of tax, a kind of obligation, does appear. There are a few tales, but within a completely different framework from the *ius primae noctis* as we imagine it. Here's one example. We are still in this fifteenth-century French collection. It is a tale set in Hostalrich in Catalonia, where Observant Franciscans establish themselves and build their friary. [N-002] They begin to do what friars always do, that is, talk with people, hear penitents' confessions, give good advice; and, as always happens, many people go to the Franciscans to confess. Many women too, including many pretty women, and somehow or other, after a while, the Franciscans begin to—well, there are all these beautiful penitents and the Franciscans begin to feel desire. I am still telling this fifteenth-century tale. As you can see, in the Middle Ages they have no difficulty telling stories; they are not afraid of offending anyone. Then one of the Franciscans has a brilliant idea. When a beautiful penitent comes to confess to him, he falsely claims that the Observant friars are owed a spiritual tax in return for the blessing: one out of every ten encounters between wives and their husbands. [N-002] The woman, completely convinced, pays the tax. After a while the other Franciscans discover the trick, and all the women learn that the tax on nights of love has to be paid; they are all thoroughly convinced and, with great zeal to save their souls, come to the friary to pay the tax. The tale ends badly: the husbands find out, take it rather badly, and burn down the friary with all the friars inside, and that is how it ends.

It's a joke, of course. It's a story. It shows us that these people think about sex and know that there is a connection between sex and power, that sex is a complicated game, a complex social situation, that many things happen in the sexual sphere. But the *ius primae noctis* as we know it? Less than zero. So let's try another test.

## 3. Peasant obligations and the documentary silence

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00281–U-00352 -->

The *ius primae noctis* is supposed to be one of a peasant's obligations to his lord. So do we know what those obligations are? Do we know, in particular, which obligations weigh most heavily, which are irritating, which people rebelled against? We certainly do. We have a vast body of records on medieval peasants' complaints against all the obligations they are forced to bear under lordship. All kinds of conflicts show up in those records. Eventually, the peasants, who in the meantime have made money and are tired of having certain obligations, are tired of having to go—say—every night there has to be a man standing guard on the castle tower, so all the families in turn have to send a man. It's a serious nuisance.

Then there are the heavier financial burdens, all the village lands belong to the lord. The peasants have the right to work them in perpetuity, but they pay rent. The peasants can also sell them among themselves, but they always belong to the lord, and they have to pay the lord a certain tax when they buy or sell his lands. And in many lordships the lord says: when my man dies, if he has children, yes, they inherit the lands that are mine; but if he has no sons, I take the lands back. I do not allow one of my peasants to decide for himself, make a will, and leave the land perhaps to a woman, who might then marry somebody from another village, and nobody knows what happens within my lordship. No wills.

Eventually the peasants get tired of all this, and very often—indeed, almost everywhere; there is barely a village in Europe where sooner or later in the Middle Ages this does not happen—the peasants sit down at a table with the lord, put money on the table, and say: we would like to abolish this obligation, that one, that one, and that one; in exchange, we will pay. And that's what happens. They draw up a handsome parchment and everything is written down. If the lord refuses, the peasants may go to the king and tell him: we are not doing well under our lord. Why not? Because he demands this, that, and the other. This one and this one were already paid by our grandfathers, fair enough, but this other thing he is demanding is new, an abuse. We do not want to do this. The king intervenes and judges. The lawyers become involved, the peasants pay a lawyer, and the legal briefs arrive. In the archives we have enormous bundles containing lists of all the obligations of these peasants who cannot bear any more and are prepared to do anything to eliminate them. If the lawsuit fails, the peasants may rebel. They rebel and perhaps even kill the lord, or, more often, the lord runs away. Then after a while an agreement is reached again; perhaps somebody has been killed, but in the end peace is made and everything is written down once more. I've mentioned only a few of the many obligations weighing on medieval peasants, and people complain about every one of them and pay to redeem every one of them.

You already see where this is going: the *ius primae noctis* is never mentioned. There is not one list of peasant grievances that refers to this obligation. These people, who are willing to pay a large amount of money to be rid of the nuisance of having to do one shift of work every week in the castle ditches, never so much as mention the *ius primae noctis*.

Now for a methodological point. An argument from silence must be handled carefully. It is not enough to say: this thing is never discussed, and therefore it does not exist. That's a basic rule of the profession. Still, there are cases where, if something is never discussed even though it would have had to be discussed if it had existed—well, there are cases where, frankly, the argument from silence becomes hard to dismiss. Until the fifteenth century, and therefore for almost the whole of the Middle Ages, there is no testimony speaking of anything resembling the *ius primae noctis*. Although sometimes we find something that helps us understand how what, at this point, I feel able to call this legend later arises. Sometimes we find some clue that helps us understand how people begin to embroider upon this fantasy.

## 4. The thirteenth-century evidence from Verson

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00353–U-00419 -->

Let me give you the earliest known case. It is a document from 1247. We are in France.

 It is a list of the customary obligations of the peasants in a village. Since that village belongs half to a lay lord and half to the monks of a monastery, the lord and the monks keep arguing—that piece is mine, this one is yours—so eventually they draw up a formal list of all the obligations. Among the obligations of the peasants of this village of Verson, in thirteenth-century France, there is the following obligation. When a peasant marries off his daughter, if she marries a young man from another village, he has to pay a tax. There's nothing unusual about this. It is a very common clause in the lives of medieval peasants, And if you'll bear with me, I'll explain why. Follow the logic. The peasant works in the village and works the lord's land, and pays him rent. When his daughter marries, perhaps he gives her part of that land as a dowry; the son-in-law will continue to work that land and pay the lord the rent, so there is no problem. But if the son-in-law is from another village, who can assure the lord that he will manage to compel him to pay the rent, that he really will work, that he will come to live there? Nobody. It's another village under another lord. So the lord of Verson, like the lords of countless other medieval villages, is not pleased if his peasants' daughters marry a young man from another village. And so he says: if you insist, you have to pay for my consent.

This tax, then, is extremely widespread. But we are telling the specific story of the village of Verson in 1247. There is this list in Latin enumerating all these obligations.

Then a monk from the abbey, apparently with time on his hands, writes a little rhyming poem in French that lists those same obligations: he translates this list of obligations into French and into verse, and adds something of his own. When he reaches the tax that peasants have to pay when their daughter marries a young man from another village, the monk says: today three coins are paid, but the peasants are happy with that, because once it was much worse. There was a time when so much had to be paid that the peasants sometimes could not manage it, and there were fathers who, rather than having to give the lord a head of livestock or a vineyard or an income, chose the alternative recalled by the poem: “Long ago it happened that the peasant took his daughter by the hand and delivered her to his lord.”

This is the very first mention, in a medieval text among the millions of medieval texts and documents, of something that vaguely resembles the *ius primae noctis*. Why only vaguely? Because it is not spoken of as a right, and it is spoken of as something the father does so as not to have to pay the heavy tax. But the crucial point is that already in this very first mention—as we will see, it will happen regularly afterward—the *ius primae noctis*, or something resembling it, forgive me, something resembling it, is discussed by saying: in the bad old days. And this is the key to understanding how, at the end of the Middle Ages, people begin to fantasize about what happens in the bad old days.

## 5. The legend spreads in Europe

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00420–U-00598 -->

By the end of the Middle Ages, many villages and towns that had been created centuries earlier by groups of peasants who came to an agreement, left their village, said goodbye to their lord, founded a town of their own, and went to live freely in that town. This happens constantly in the Middle Ages. In some regions it is more common than in others. For a mix of reasons, it's especially common in Piedmont, for example, where several towns, from Cuneo to Alessandria and Cherasco, came into being through this mechanism.

Cuneo gives us a good example. Three hundred years have passed since the town was founded. At the end of the fifteenth century a learned man from Cuneo, the lawyer Rebaccini, decides to write Cuneo's history and tells how Cuneo came into being: how our ancestors, long ago, decided to abandon their lords, leave their villages, and go to live in a new town. And the lawyer Rebaccini says they left because it was burdensome to live under the lords' rule, and he begins to list a whole series of impositions that the lords inflicted on their peasants: they did not allow them to make wills; they forced them to pay whenever someone had to sell land; they took the tithe, took the *taglia*; on every contract they took a share; they forced them to perform unpaid labor services. The lawyer Rebaccini says: they forced them to work without discretion—because if they had done it with discretion, but instead, no. And notice that all these complaints correspond exactly to what documents from three hundred years earlier really tell us: these obligations and this burden existed in the lordships. Rebaccini goes on and on about how bad those old days were, how tyrannical those feudal lords were, and then at the very end, when everything is truly finished, finally: “subordinating reason to lust and sensuality, they deflowered their subjects' daughters and likewise their brides, persuading the subjects that this was their ancient right and a privilege granted to them.” As with Rebaccini in Cuneo, so too in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in several Italian and French towns, there is some good citizen who sets about telling the history of the town and begins with the time when, three hundred years earlier, four hundred years earlier, their great-great-grandfathers rebelled against the wicked lords. And by now this idea starts creeping into many of their accounts. Those lords really were wicked, and they even claimed this. If someone did not pay, they took the girls' virginity.

In other words, by the time we reach the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries—and therefore between the end of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance—we suddenly find ourselves facing a Europe in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* has entered circulation and is beginning to resurface. And it always, always resurfaces with this characteristic: it speaks of an extremely distant past. In the bad old days these things happened.

No—that's not quite right. It is not always the bad old days among us that people talk about. There is another way in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* circulates in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe. Not among us in the bad old days, but now among the savages.

Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe—here's the quick context—is the Europe of the great geographical discoveries. During the fifteenth century the Portuguese venture out into the Atlantic; they cross over, go down below the Cape of Good Hope, and discover new lands everywhere, and then, of course, at the end of the fifteenth century Columbus sets out and reaches America, and then in the early sixteenth century the *conquistadores* go. A whole series of new worlds comes into view. Each time navigators arrive in a new place, they publish reports describing what that place is like. These reports by Portuguese and Spanish navigators and conquerors all resemble one another. The inhabitants of these islands are savages. You cannot imagine how savage they are. They go naked, they have no morality, they do the worst things. Just imagine that among them, when someone marries, the village chief has the right to sleep with the girl; and sometimes not just the village chief: if a merchant marries, all the merchants go; if a nobleman marries, all the noblemen; if instead he is a commoner, the master goes, naturally. When the Portuguese discover the Canaries in the mid-fifteenth century, they tell this story. When Alvise Cadamosto goes to Tenerife, he says that this happens in Tenerife. When Cortés goes to Mexico he tells—or rather, earlier still, in Cuba—he tells that this used to happen in Cuba.

Why do they tell these stories? Because we are reducing these savage peoples to slavery, if not exterminating them. Because, supposedly, we're bringing them reason and the true faith, and consequently it is necessary to show that they are in great need of them: here we come to bring them civilization. These savages are truly beneath the human level; they do inconceivable things. Of course, no Spaniard or Portuguese imagines that such things happen in his own world, They would never present such behavior at home as a shocking curiosity. But when savages are discussed, anything goes.

But things are never quite that simple. These books appear, a great many appear, people believe them, and there is also someone who protests. Cortés's report saying that these wicked things happened in Cuba is published by Francisco López de Gómara in the *General History of the West Indies and the New Lands Discovered*, and someone protests, because in that strange Europe, after all, there is also someone who says: but none of this is true; we are wronging these Indians, they must be defended, what we are doing to them is shameful. Bartolomé de las Casas, a Dominican friar and bishop, accuses Gómara of calumniating Indigenous peoples. The crown later bans the book, but we cannot say that Las Casas personally caused the ban. [N-005] In the meantime, however, it has been translated into French, Montaigne reads it, everyone reads it, and in short these stories continue to circulate.

So let's take stock. In fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe the story of the *ius primae noctis* circulates widely. There are two possibilities: did it happen among us in the bad old days, or does it happen now among the savages? Among the savages, among the barbarians—because everyone, after all, can have whichever barbarian they want.

In England between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the England of the great Queen Elizabeth and of Shakespeare—Shakespeare... there is a country that good English people do not consider barbarous and savage; it is supposed to be highly civilized, but it has become horribly barbarized. It has sunk into an abyss of wickedness, and it is Italy, naturally. It is Italy where, on the one hand, the pope rules—and for good English Protestants papism is the worst thing in the world—and at the same time in Italy there is Machiavelli, the cynic, the immoral man. There: for the English in the early seventeenth century, Italy is the most immoral country in the world. In 1619 a play by two successful authors, Beaumont and Fletcher, is staged in London. It is entitled *The Custom of the Country*. And it tells of a country, a town so barbaric that in that town the governor demands the *ius primae noctis* over all the women who marry there, until naturally a couple more energetic than the others rebels, et cetera, et cetera. Where is that town? Italy, naturally.

In short, for intellectuals, writers, and the wider public of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the *ius primae noctis* has become a formidable device for describing all those worlds we do not like, which we consider barbaric, whether the distant medieval past or the present of savages or Italians, depending on the case. That is not all. At this point something else happens that helps consolidate the legend that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* really existed.

## 6. Jurists, marriage taxes, and false etymologies

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00599–U-00735 -->

The sixteenth century belongs to the jurists. They begin taking a close look at the obligations of peasants, the customs of lordships, the feudal customs, as people begin to call them in that period. In France, for example, these jurists are in the king's service, and the king is very happy to cut the lords' power down a little. In the culture of these jurists, the lords' power begins to seem like an antiquated relic, something that should be eliminated. Remember: in 1789, after two and a half centuries of reasoning of this kind, finally, with the French Revolution, the first thing they do is abolish the feudal regime. All this begins in the sixteenth century. The sixteenth-century jurists investigate and discover that in many lordships a tax is paid on marriage.

These sixteenth-century jurists are educated men, but they also know how to have fun. They too like slightly obscene tales. It is the century of Aretino; it is the century of Rabelais. At the universities there is much joking and amusement, and all these jurists were students before they became jurists, and, in short, they also know that very often, when a peasant pays a tax, it is because there was once a service in kind and later the peasant redeemed it. We have seen this mechanism.

Picture one of these jurists at a desk piled high with law books. He's extremely learned, but he is also ready to believe anything. He has to explain, because jurists of the period have to explain why this tax is paid. There is a tax on marriages. When the peasant marries off his daughter, he has to pay. Could it be that there was once a service in kind that has now been redeemed? And of course it must be so. Wherever they find a marriage tax, the jurists imagine that the *ius primae noctis* existed before it. Soon entire treatises present this as an established fact. By now it is known that this happened in the past, and the historians too, by now, read the jurists—and if the jurists do not know, well then historians invent when a piece is missing.

There is a book that has enormous influence. Hector Boece's *Scotorum Historia* is first printed in 1527. [N-007] Boece is a learned Renaissance humanist, but here is a sense of how reliable his *History of Scotland* is. According to him Scotland was founded by a princess named Scotia who was the daughter of the pharaoh, the one from Moses, the one in the Bible. In this work, whose credibility you can appreciate, Boece says: among us in Scotland peasants pay a marriage tax. This tax was introduced by the good King Malcolm in the eleventh century, on the advice of his wife, the saintly Queen Margaret, to replace an earlier tax—no, not a tax, an earlier obligation, a wicked, filthy obligation that I do not even dare tell you what it was—which had been introduced so many centuries earlier, in the days of the barbarians, the Celts, by King Evenus of Scotland: the obligation whereby, indeed, the girls with the lord, et cetera, et cetera, et cetera. At this point, your knees give way, because—no, for heaven's sake—King Malcolm existed, and his wife Margaret too; obviously they never did anything of the kind. King Evenus goes hand in hand with Princess Scotia, daughter of the pharaoh. He is completely made up, because when these people do not know how to fill the gaps, they invent. And books circulate; everyone reads books.

Medieval taxes had all kinds of local names. In France, a tax collected on various occasions—including, sometimes, marriage—could be called *coillage*. When notaries translated that word into Latin, some wrote *culagium*. To speakers of Spanish, French, and Italian, *culagium* sounded like it came from the word for “ass.” Now imagine a sixteenth-century jurist discovering that a peasant had to pay a marriage tax with a name that seemed to mean something like “ass tax.” They could hardly contain themselves.

Here's another example. Medieval peasants sometimes paid a levy to feed the lord's horses—basically a fodder tax. In Italy, it was called *fodro*, a word related to the English “fodder.” Centuries later, scholars had forgotten where the name came from. In Nizza Monferrato, an old legend told of peasants rebelling against a wicked lord. Then an eighteenth-century scholar named Giulio Cesare Cordara found records of a tax called *fodro*. He decided the word must be related to the Italian *fodero*, meaning “sheath.” From there he made quite a leap: the sheath must mean the vagina, and the tax must have replaced the lord's right to sleep with local brides. He even wrote a poem about it, *The Sheath, or the Ancient Lords of Nizza Monferrato's Right to Brides*.

Now take one more small step. Once everyone has convinced themselves that these rights existed in the past, they go looking for further proof, and when you go looking for further proof, you find it.

## 7. The Church and the first-night marriage tax

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00736–U-00784 -->

Here's an example: a document that countless scholars have cited as proof that not only does the *ius primae noctis* exist in the Middle Ages, but even the Church demands it from its peasants. The starting point is a trial, a trial held before the Parlement of Paris concerning the diocese of Amiens. We are at the end of the fourteenth century. What's the background? In the Middle Ages, the Church, as it does today and perhaps even more than today—indeed, often more than today—makes an effort to remind Christians that sex within marriage is acceptable, but it has to be kept under control because it is intended exclusively for procreation. Consequently, one must not let oneself go too much in sex, even within marriage; indeed, learning to control desire is good and proper.

In some medieval dioceses, bishops begin telling to their faithful that it would be a fine thing if, when they marry, they remained chaste for three days, so as to show that they know how to master their lust and that they are not marrying for that. In some dioceses, in one way or another, this becomes an obligation imposed by the Church. When you marry, for three days you cannot consummate the marriage.

Then comes the late Middle Ages. Before the year 1000, the economy is stagnant. Afterward, everyone starts making money, huge amounts of it circulate, and people become much more practical—and much more cynical. You pay for everything. In the late Middle Ages many people begin to say: all right, I'm getting married and I'm supposed to observe the three days of chastity, but what if instead I made an offering to the Church? And the Church says: splendid, make the offering. After a while there is nobody left who observes the three days of chastity, but there are dioceses where it is normal for a person who marries to have to pay a certain tax to the Church. What do they call it? Various things, but mostly the tax on the first wedding night. Because that is precisely what I am paying for.

By the end of the fourteenth century—and this too is an old story—in the diocese of Amiens people no longer pay it. The bishop is annoyed and brings a lawsuit to force people to continue paying this tax. The people protest; the matter goes to the Parlement of Paris; a trial ensues that lasts sixteen years, and in the end I no longer even remember what the Parlement of Paris decides. Whatever the outcome, you can picture the trial record: bundles this thick, packed with legal arguments in Latin. When eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historians discover this trial, it is not as though they sit down and read the whole thing—you really cannot understand a thing—but one thing is clear: it speaks of a tax on the first night of marriage, and it is the Church that demands the tax on the first night of marriage from the peasants. It is proof that the *ius primae noctis* existed.

## 8. Lords adopt the legend and marriage rituals blur it

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00785–U-00862 -->

One last thing, and maybe the strangest: between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, more or less everyone becomes convinced that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* existed. The lords too hear this, because lordship still exists. Until the feudal regime is abolished with the French Revolution, lordship exists. The lords are less powerful than before; the king has taken away a good many powers and revenues from them. Nevertheless, the peasant still always has to deal with the lord and pays many taxes. The lords learn that this *ius primae noctis* existed in the past. And they think: wait a minute—maybe my lordship had it too. My ancestors—my ancestors certainly had the right. That leads to two possible reactions. A scholar from the beginning of the seventeenth century tells us this story. He writes: “Such a custom formerly existed in Piedmont among pagans and gentiles, and the Most Illustrious Cardinal Hieronimo della Rovere told me that he himself had burned the privilege that his house possessed concerning it.” Who knows what that parchment really contained? A marriage tax, the *culagium*—who knows? The cardinal took it at face value; since he was a cardinal, he burned it. In our civilized times such things are no longer done.

Other lords aren't cardinals, and they have fewer scruples. Some lords are constantly fighting with their peasants, because it is all very well to say these are the rights, these are the obligations; people do not want to pay taxes, and so they quarrel continually and go to court. Sometimes a lord is summoned by a court to state what, in his view, his rights are. Beginning in the fifteenth century, a few cases have been identified, five in all as far as I know: one in the fifteenth century, three in the sixteenth, one at the end of the seventeenth, still under the Sun King, in various godforsaken places. A local lord, summoned by a court to declare what his rights and his peasants' obligations are, says: they pay me this, they pay me that, the lamb at Easter and the pig at Christmas, ten coins when they buy land, twenty coins when they move house, five coins when a daughter marries; and those five coins when a daughter marries are because I would have the right to sleep with her on the first night, but my ancestors agreed to commute this right, and so now I... though I could always start demanding this again if my peasants do not behave themselves.

So the fantasy doesn't quite become reality, but it does become something people can imagine enforcing. There are five among the hundreds of thousands of lords who lived in Europe who quarrel so much with their peasants that they go and tell them: look, if you do not stop making me angry, I'll bring out the *ius primae noctis*.

We're almost done, but we still have five minutes, so this is worth mentioning. Marriage in Europe at the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the modern period is not a very private matter. On the contrary, it is a very public matter, and one that is also at the center of many conflicts that people accept fairly readily, even though they are conflicts and can create problems. Here's an example. A girl from the village marries someone from another town, another village. We have already said that this annoys the lord and that a payment therefore has to be made. But it also annoys the other young men of the village. She is one of ours and one of those others has come to take her and is carrying her away. It is normal, when one of their female companions marries and goes to live in another village, for the young men of the village to have the road blocked when the wedding procession is about to leave. The road is blocked, all the young men of the village are there, and they tell the bridegroom: if you want to take her away, buy us a drink. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, the bridegroom knows the drill and gives in and buys everyone a drink. Then they remove the barrier and let him pass. One time in a hundred the bridegroom is someone who has already had a little to drink, becomes angry, they fight, and someone is killed.

The wedding banquet and the first wedding night invite the same kind of ritual conflict. The banquet: everyone comes. In the novelist Falcones's imagination, the lord happens to turn up by chance; he does not know that the peasant is getting married. Come on. Everyone knows about these things, and the lord is usually invited to the banquet, most certainly, because it is important to keep him well disposed; and he too needs to keep wealthy peasants well disposed, so he goes—he will later baptize their children. He goes to the banquet and is keen to go to the banquet and sit in the place of honor. Every now and again there is also some lord who quarrels with the peasants because they invite him but do not give him sufficiently good wine. He is supposed to drink better wine, and he has the right to drink the best wine at the banquet, not the wine they give second-class guests. And then, when the bride finally goes to bed with her husband, there is an endless variety of possible rituals. Generally all the male guests prepare the husband and the women prepare the wife. They undress them, put their nightshirts on them, play a few jokes, make a few quips, a few obscenities, and then finally shut them in the room. There are lordships where the lord says: I am the one who has the right to escort the bride to the bedroom, because I am the lord of this village and it must be seen.

In other words, marriage sits inside a whole world of public rituals in which obligations—yes, obligations toward the community—can be woven around marriage. And it is in this context that, if scholars believe there used to be a *ius primae noctis*, well, it does not take much for someone to begin believing it in earnest too.

## 9. Power, abuse, and Orwell's invented past

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00863–U-00913 -->

Let's wrap up. The *ius primae noctis* is an extraordinary fantasy born at the end of the Middle Ages. People believe in it so strongly that someone nearly tries to put it into practice, but there is no indication that anyone ever does. The right itself never exists.

Does that mean a lord never sleeps with a village girl? No. The lord sleeps with the village girl, by fair means or foul. That kind of thing happens in every unequal society. But there's an enormous difference between a society where a rich man exploits a poor woman and one where the law supposedly gives him that right and everyone has to accept it. The second is sheer madness. Saying that lords sleep with village girls is like saying that, in the nineteenth century, the owner of the spinning mill or his son sometimes sleeps with a female worker. That's the comparison. There are a thousand reasons why this can happen, but obviously no one dreams of saying it is a right. To speak of it as a law means wanting to invent a past.

So I'll close with an extraordinary passage about inventing the past. It is taken from Orwell's novel *1984*. At one point the novel shows how, under Big Brother's regime, the past is reinvented for the people. In the past, the people are taught, there are evil creatures called capitalists, and the capitalists oppress the people in every way, with every kind of abuse and oppression. I quote verbatim: there is also something called the *ius primae noctis*, which probably ought not to be mentioned in schoolbooks for children. It was the law by which every capitalist had the right to sleep with any woman working in one of his factories.

That is fantastic because Orwell has identified the mechanism exactly. Something that can happen in reality, and is an act of violence and an effect of social relations, is imagined to have been a law. A society that could make such a thing legal feels utterly alien, completely barbaric by our standards. So when we rewrite the past in order to feel good about living in a much better world, the *ius primae noctis* is perfect for furnishing that past. Thank you.
