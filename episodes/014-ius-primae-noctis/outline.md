# Lo ius primae noctis — structural outline

## Overview

Barbero tests the familiar image of a legally enforceable medieval “right of the first night.” He
moves from modern fiction and folklore to two bodies of medieval evidence—sexual comic literature
and records of peasant obligations—where the alleged right is absent. He then traces how late
medieval and early modern chronicles, travel writing, jurists, false etymologies, and retrospective
lordly claims made the story credible. The conclusion distinguishes real sexual abuse under unequal
power from a legal right and uses Orwell to explain why invented laws make the past appear alien.

## 1. The modern image and its narrative uses

**Transcript:** U-00001–U-00146  
**Original audio:** 00:49–09:26

Barbero defines the alleged right and shows how strongly it inhabits modern images of the Middle
Ages. He retells the opening of Ildefonso Falcones’s *La cattedrale del mare* and the Scottish plot
device in *Braveheart*, then surveys Italian local legends in which a bride or husband ends a
feudal tyrant’s claim. The examples establish the myth’s usefulness as an engine of oppression,
rebellion, and liberation before the lecture turns to medieval evidence.

- Examples: Falcones, *Braveheart*, Fiuggi, Sant’Agata di Puglia, Roccascalegna, Ligurian and
  Piedmontese traditions, and Ivrea’s Violetta.
- Aside: the name Violetta is anachronistic for a medieval heroine.
- Quotations/provenance: Q-001 (Falcones scene); Q-002 (*Braveheart* royal speech).
- Claims: C-001 (modern prevalence and narrative pattern); C-002 (local folklore traditions).

## 2. The absence from medieval sexual storytelling

**Transcript:** U-00147–U-00280  
**Original audio:** 09:26–18:53

The first evidentiary test is realistic and sexually explicit medieval storytelling: Boccaccio’s
novelle and French fabliaux. Despite abundant stories about clerical, aristocratic, and household
sexual pursuit, Barbero says the alleged legal right never appears. He illustrates the different
social logic with two tales from *Les Cent Nouvelles nouvelles*: a count outwitted by a peasant
woman and friars who invent a sexual “tithe.”

- Narrative steps: genres and evidentiary value; negative finding; tale 24; the Girona friars.
- Quotations/provenance: Q-003 (the count and peasant woman); Q-004 (the friars’ tithe).
- Claims: C-003 (absence from medieval novelle/fabliaux); C-004 (collection, date, and examples).

## 3. Peasant obligations and documentary silence

**Transcript:** U-00281–U-00352  
**Original audio:** 18:54–24:10

The second test is the extensive documentary record of obligations peasants contested, redeemed,
litigated, and sometimes rebelled against. Barbero lists guard duty, labor, rents, alienation fees,
and inheritance restrictions. He argues that the total absence of the alleged sexual obligation is
probative because these records preserve far less serious grievances in exhaustive detail, while
acknowledging the methodological limits of arguments from silence.

- Claims: C-005 (range and documentation of peasant obligations); C-006 (probative silence and its
  methodological qualification).

## 4. Verson and the earliest resemblance

**Transcript:** U-00353–U-00419  
**Original audio:** 24:10–28:36

A 1247 record from Verson concerns a fee when a peasant’s daughter marries outside the village.
Barbero explains the landholding rationale, then describes a rhymed vernacular complaint in which
the monk says that in harsher former times fathers unable to pay offered daughters to the lord.
This is presented as the earliest passage remotely resembling the later legend, but as an alleged
substitute for an onerous fee rather than an enforceable right.

- Quotation/provenance: Q-005 (the Verson complaint).
- Claims: C-007 (document, fee, and poem); C-008 (its limited resemblance and retrospective frame).

## 5. The legend enters circulation

**Transcript:** U-00420–U-00598  
**Original audio:** 28:37–38:58

Late medieval foundation chronicles portray distant feudal times as tyrannical. Barbero focuses on
Rebaccini’s Cuneo chronicle and its statement about lords deflowering subjects’ daughters and
brides. He then follows the same othering mechanism into Portuguese and Spanish travel accounts,
Las Casas’s objection to Gómara, and English anti-Italian theater. The alleged custom is located
either in a barbaric past or among supposedly barbaric contemporaries.

- Quotations/provenance: Q-006 (Rebaccini); Q-007 (travel-account reports); Q-008 (Las Casas on
  Gómara); Q-009 (Beaumont and Fletcher’s Italian setting).
- Claims: C-009 (late-medieval genesis in civic chronicles); C-010 (colonial othering and dissent);
  C-011 (early modern theatrical circulation).

## 6. Jurists, taxes, and false etymologies

**Transcript:** U-00599–U-00735  
**Original audio:** 38:59–47:37

Sixteenth-century jurists reasoned backwards from marriage payments to a supposedly commuted
sexual service. Hector Boece supplied a Scottish pseudo-history involving Evenus, Malcolm, and
Margaret; Barbero illustrates its continuing influence through a modern reader citing Du Cange.
The names *coillage/culagium* and *fodro* then encouraged sexual folk etymologies, including
Giulio Cesare Cordara’s poem about Nizza Monferrato. Once the premise was accepted, ambiguous
documents were repeatedly converted into proofs.

- Quotations/provenance: Q-010 (Boece); Q-011 (Du Cange’s Scottish entry).
- Claims: C-012 (juristic backward inference); C-013 (Boece’s Scottish construction); C-014
  (coillage/culagium and fodro etymologies).

## 7. The Amiens church tax

**Transcript:** U-00736–U-00784  
**Original audio:** 47:38–50:52

Barbero describes a late-fourteenth-century dispute in Amiens over a church payment associated
with the first three nights of marital continence. Later readers reduced a long legal record to a
“first-night” fee demanded by the Church and treated it as proof of the alleged sexual right.

- Claims: C-015 (continence rule, commutation, Amiens litigation, and later misreading).

## 8. Retrospective lordly claims and public marriage ritual

**Transcript:** U-00785–U-00862  
**Original audio:** 50:52–57:23

After the legend became common knowledge, some lords interpreted old marriage dues as evidence of
an ancestral sexual right. Barbero recounts Cardinal Gerolamo della Rovere’s reported destruction
of a charter and five known late cases in which lords used such claims against peasants. He then
places those claims within public wedding customs: payments for marrying out, roadblocks, banquet
precedence, and the ceremonial escort of the bride.

- Quotations/provenance: Q-012 (Della Rovere anecdote); Q-013 (composite lordly declarations).
- Claims: C-016 (late retrospective claims); C-017 (collective wedding rites and obligations).

## 9. Abuse, legal right, and the invented past

**Transcript:** U-00863–U-00913  
**Original audio:** 57:24–60:36

The conclusion states that the legal right never existed, while explicitly refusing to deny real
sexual exploitation by powerful men. The decisive distinction is between abuse produced by social
inequality and a law to which everyone must submit. Barbero closes with a passage from Orwell’s
*1984*, where the regime teaches that capitalists once possessed a version of the right, as a model
of how invented legal horrors furnish a gratifyingly barbaric past.

- Modern analogy: factory owners and workers in the nineteenth century.
- Quotation/provenance: Q-014 (Orwell, *1984*).
- Claims: C-018 (abuse/right distinction and myth-making function).
