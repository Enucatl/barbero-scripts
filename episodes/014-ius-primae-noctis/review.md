**Priority findings**

1. **Restore the Du Cange reader anecdote after Boece.**

   At [final line 112](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:112), “books circulate; everyone reads books” now leads directly to tax names. In the [Italian, U-00681–00689](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/transcript.it.md:2727), Barbero describes a reader responding to his newspaper article by citing Du Cange’s dictionary as evidence that King Evenus really instituted the right. The preceding story has just exposed Evenus as invented.

   This is the most consequential removal. It demonstrates how an invented story acquires authority and reaches the present. It also supplies personal experience, surprise, and a punchline. Q-011 supports the transmission in substance. Keep the event anchored to Barbero’s experience; the exact “this morning” and “three or four days ago” timing can be shortened. A brief exchange with the reader would recover its value without restoring every word.

2. **Restore the original degree of uncertainty in the conclusion.**

   [Final line 157](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:157) says someone “nearly tries to put it into practice.” The [Italian, U-00866](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/transcript.it.md:3467), says there was almost a risk someone would want to do so. The English implies an actual near-attempt that the source does not establish. Suggested wording: “People believed in it so strongly that there was a risk someone might try to put it into practice.”

3. **Recover the performance of the two medieval tales.**

   The count’s escape scene at [line 36](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:36) keeps the boot mechanism but summarizes the negotiation and escape. The friars’ scene at [line 39](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:39) compresses the invented tithe into one explanatory sentence. In the Italian, these are exchanges between characters, with a setup and comic payoff.

   Restore a short request to remove the dirty boots and some direct speech in the friars’ exchange. Especially useful is the original progression from ordinary tithes to the invented sexual tithe: the woman already pays shares to lord and parish, so the friar claims one encounter in ten. This makes the deception easier to grasp as well as more entertaining. Keep the corrected boot, Hostalrich setting, and omission of papal authorization. Those changes have source support; the loss of dialogue is an independent editorial choice.

4. **Clarify whose conclusion the listener is hearing.**

   The Amiens episode ends at [line 133](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:133): “It is proof that the ius primae noctis existed.” This reproduces the Italian’s irony, but its meaning depends on performance. Make the mistaken inference explicit: “And they take that as proof that the right existed.” Similarly, the colonial reports at lines 87–89 benefit from a clear spoken attribution when the narrator switches into the conquerors’ claims.

   In chapter 6, “the missing evidence begins to manufacture itself” (line 109) gives a useful structural signal but is abstract. A more concrete transition would be: “Now the story begins to produce its own apparent evidence.” The surrounding explanation should keep the agents visible: jurists reinterpret taxes, historians repeat them, and later readers mistake repetition for confirmation.

5. **Give Verson and the wedding rituals a clear role in the investigation.**

   [Line 57](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:57) says there is no testimony resembling the right until the fifteenth century, followed by “the earliest known case” in 1247. The qualification eventually arrives, but a listener first hears a contradiction. Introduce Verson as an earlier hint about how the story developed. Preserve the distinction between a current legal entitlement and a story about a bargain in an indefinite past.

   At [line 145](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:145), “we still have five minutes” makes the marriage customs sound optional, after “One last thing” has already signalled an ending. Their function is substantive: public wedding obligations made later sexual interpretations easier to imagine. Introduce that function directly, then retain the roadblock, banquet, and bedroom escort. The callback to Falcones is particularly valuable.

6. **Fix small but definite departures and quotation problems.**

   At [line 14](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:14), Violetta becomes a “miller’s daughter.” The source says “La bella mugnaia” ([U-00132](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/transcript.it.md:531)); it does not identify her as a daughter. Use wording faithful to that designation or establish the relationship from a source.

   At [line 161](/export/docker/barbero-scripts/episodes/014-ius-primae-noctis/script.en.md:161), “I quote verbatim” introduces a sentence that has been translated back from Italian and put into the present tense. Its modal wording also differs from Orwell’s original. The following sentence is the recovered exact English. Either use the original English for the full quoted passage or move the quotation introduction to the exact sentence. The passage can be checked in [Orwell, Part One, chapter 7](https://www.gutenberg.net.au/ebooks01/0100021h.html). Preserve the quotation’s original tense.

**Spoken English and information hierarchy**

The existing sequence works: a familiar modern image; two places where evidence should appear; the records’ silence; an early retrospective hint; later stories; taxes interpreted through those stories; lords adopting the claims; the distinction between abuse and law. Keep that sequence. Make its transitions audible because the listener cannot consult chapter headings.

The later section can be remembered through three processes: people tell stories about a barbaric past or foreign peoples; scholars interpret ordinary documents through those stories; interested lords adopt the resulting claims. Names, dates, and etymologies should support those processes. Explain “jurists” once as legal scholars, “service in kind” as an obligation performed instead of paying money, and the Parlement of Paris as a court. Translate or briefly explain the unglossed taglia at line 81.

Several phrases still sound like an insufficiently edited transcript. Useful local fixes include:

- Line 49: “Eventually, the peasants, who … are tired of having to go—say—every night …” never cleanly completes its setup. Give the guard-duty example its own sentence.
- Line 65: repair “peasants, And”; shorten the repeated statement that these are obligations of this village.
- Line 79: “By the end of the Middle Ages, many villages and towns that had been created …” is a sentence fragment. It needs a main clause, such as “there were many towns founded centuries earlier by peasants who had left their lords.”
- Line 81: “work without discretion” does not clearly convey the intended excessive demands in contemporary English. “Work without restraint” is easier to hear.
- Line 95: remove the duplicated “Shakespeare—Shakespeare.”
- Line 112: “your knees give way” translates an Italian idiom literally and suggests a physical event. Use an expression of exasperation suited to the narrator’s tone.
- Line 129: “begin telling to their faithful” should be “begin telling the faithful.”
- Line 133: “bundles this thick” depends on a gesture. “Huge bundles of legal arguments in Latin” carries the image in audio.

The historical present works in the acted scenes. It becomes awkward when applied indiscriminately to background facts, claims about a distant past, and quoted text: “Back in 2006 … publishes,” “in the Middle Ages they have no idea,” and the changed Orwell introduction are examples. Preserve scene immediacy while allowing ordinary past tense where it makes chronology and quotation clearer.

**Existing research qualifications relevant to the review**

These are inherited source claims or retained editorial decisions, rather than omissions newly introduced by translation:

- The Verson research distinguishes the 1247 compilation from a poem dated approximately 1270 (`claims.yaml`, C-007; `quotes.yaml`, Q-005). Lines 63–69 do not make that distinction audible.
- The Cadamosto research says he did not visit Tenerife (`claims.yaml`, C-010), whereas line 87 still says he goes there. “Cadamosto reports a story about Tenerife” would preserve the example without implying observation.
- Line 95 retains the traditional Beaumont-and-Fletcher attribution and a definite 1619 performance. The repository’s C-011 and [Folger’s record](https://emed.folger.edu/cc) identify Fletcher and Massinger and date the first performance approximately 1619–1620.
- The Amiens “sixteen years” is qualified in C-015. CC-006 was explicitly rejected with a retain recommendation, so this is a previously retained simplification, not a missed accepted change. The review does not reverse that decision.

The strongest material to preserve is the contrast between Falcones and Braveheart, both medieval tales, the archival argument with its caution about silence, the recurring “bad old days,” the false etymologies, the wedding callback to the opening novel, and Orwell’s ending. The recommended changes recover Barbero’s performance and clarify his reasoning while keeping this structure intact.
