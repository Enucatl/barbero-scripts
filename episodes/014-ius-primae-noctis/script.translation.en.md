# The ius primae noctis: A Middle Ages not to believe in — faithful English translation

## 1. The modern image of the right of the first night

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00146 -->

Well, good evening. So tonight's subject is perhaps the best known of all those we shall touch on here over these three evenings. I think we all have in mind this image that we associate with the Middle Ages, the *ius primae noctis*. And so—I contradict myself, we all have it in mind, but let us say what it is anyway. The right of the lord of the village to have the virginity of all the girls in the village, or at least all those who were not noblewomen, all the female serfs and peasant women, especially when they married: to sleep with them before the husband, on the first wedding night. This image is extremely widespread in the way people perceive the Middle Ages today, so much so that we continually come across it whenever there is a historical novel or a film set in the Middle Ages. Perhaps “whenever” is saying too much, but we come across it all the time. Let me give you a couple of examples, to recall things that will already be familiar to many of you. I could give you a great many; let us do two. One from historical fiction. A few years ago, in 2006, I believe, a historical novel came out and enjoyed immense success, by a Spanish author named Ildefonso Falcones. The novel is called *The Cathedral of the Sea*. It is a grand story set in the Middle Ages, in fourteenth-century Catalonia, a story of struggle for freedom and against oppression, and it opens with a great scene entirely centered on the *ius primae noctis*. You know that a historical novel, like every serialized novel, has to begin with a powerful scene. There has to be something that catches the reader immediately; if there is a dead body, so much the better, but in any event something very powerful. *The Cathedral of the Sea* opens with this extremely violent scene of the *ius primae noctis*. There is a peasant wedding. There is the peasant who is to be married, and they are prosperous peasants, people who work, who are doing well, respectable people. There is the wedding banquet in the countryside, and at a certain point, suddenly, the lord turns up on horseback, accompanied by his henchmen. Everyone is very frightened that the lord has arrived there, but obviously they can do nothing. They invite him to sit down, and the lord sits at the table with the other guests and begins to drink. He notices that the bride is a beautiful woman and wants her to be the one who pours his wine. He forces her to come over, touches her, and then, when he has drunk his fill, the lord jumps up, takes the bride by the wrist, calls the bridegroom, and tells him: “In exercise of the right that belongs to me as your lord, I have decided to lie with your wife on her first night.” After that he drags the woman inside, then comes out after a while buttoning his trousers and says to the husband: “Now it is your turn. […] Do your duty as a good Christian husband!”. And nobody has lifted a finger. They are all dismayed, horrified by what is happening, but not one of those peasants lifts a finger, because the lord has the right to do what he is doing. Then this *ius primae noctis*, so violent that it is actually rape, will have enormous consequences. The whole novel starts from there, which is simply to say that the *ius primae noctis* is a formidable narrative device. It sets a whole machine in motion: there is the villain, there are the good people who have suffered the abuse, there is the girl who loses her mind after this violence, and after a while the husband leaves the village and goes to live in the city, where at least there is freedom and no lords who have to be obeyed. Let me give you one more example; this one too may be superfluous, but never mind. A film many of us have seen, Mel Gibson's *Braveheart*. A great big medieval film, the story of the rebellion of the Scots under the leadership of the heroic William Wallace against the wicked English rulers. There too, at a certain point, the *ius primae noctis* comes into play to move the plot forward. It is a most curious scene. The king of England—wicked, naturally—is talking with his barons, and at a certain point he says: the problem with Scotland is that it is full of Scots, but I have found the way to put an end to the Scots. The time has come to restore an old tradition. Let us grant the *ius primae noctis*. From now on our nobles will have the right to sleep with Scottish girls before they marry, and in this way the implication is that the race of these rebels will be mongrelized. Notice that while in Falcones's novel the author has rather courageously tried to imagine a world where the *ius primae noctis* exists as something that one cannot avoid accepting—it is an obligation, it is a law, and nobody rebels—in the film *Braveheart*, and you will understand later why it is important to stress this aspect, the perspective is a little different. The king says: let us restore an old custom. And of course the consequence, when the English begin to demand the *ius primae noctis*, is that the Scots finally rise in revolt. This is a connection that we find extremely often when the *ius primae noctis* is discussed. There are lots of Italian towns or villages whose tradition, whose legend, includes a distant past in which an evil feudal lord oppressed the inhabitants by demanding the *ius primae noctis*, until naturally a girl braver than the others, or a husband braver than the others, rebelled and put an end to the tyranny. It is a long list; by looking on the Internet one could add still more. How many places have this story in their memory, in their tradition? Fiuggi; Sant'Agata di Puglia; Roccascalegna in Abruzzo; several villages in Liguria, Montalto Ligure, Onzo; several villages in Piedmont, Nizza Monferrato, Rocca Grimalda; even Ivrea, where the Ivrea Carnival—you know, the carnival where they throw oranges—is said today to have originated in an episode in which an evil tyrant demanded the *ius primae noctis* until a girl cleverer than the others rebelled: Violetta, the beautiful miller's daughter. Let me open a small parenthesis. To imagine how old this story is, you need only consider that in the Middle Ages they had no idea what the name Violetta even was. The name Violetta became fashionable in the nineteenth century with *La traviata*. Before that there was nobody called Violetta. Parenthesis closed, because I do not want to influence your judgment. So it seems to me that we have established our first point: in today's culture, both in local folklore and in the culture of historical novelists and screenwriters—and therefore also of all of us who watch those films and read those novels—the *ius primae noctis* is a fairly regular presence whenever the Middle Ages are discussed. As you know, the wager behind these evenings of ours is to say: well, let us check. Let us see what testimony exists in the Middle Ages for the existence of the *ius primae noctis*. Let us go looking for it.

## 2. Searching medieval stories for the alleged right

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00147–U-00280 -->

Now I shall quickly suggest two possible paths. Let us look through the testimony, through the sources that have come down to us from the Middle Ages: where is the *ius primae noctis* discussed, where can we look for it? One first idea is to look in literature, in the tales, in that realistic literature which depicts real situations. Tales of the kind written by Boccaccio were enormously successful in the Middle Ages; people loved them and wrote lots of them. We Italians know Boccaccio's tales well, but similar literary genres exist in other countries too. There is an immense production of these texts, which have the following features: they are generally very realistic, they represent real situations of the period and real characters, and they are very explicit. Sex is discussed a great deal, and without anyone having any difficulty about it. Sex is discussed to tell stories of successful tricks, to show how the poor manage to resist the abuses of the rich. Sex is discussed to show how much cleverness counts and how useful it is even in those situations. There are Boccaccio's tales, but there is another genre—let me pause on this for a second. In France, even before Boccaccio, as early as the thirteenth century, there is a literary genre called the *fabliaux*. They are short poems, short poems in verse, because that was the taste of the time, usually very funny, which rather like our tales recount pranks, jokes, tricks, strange episodes; and there too sex is discussed all the time, I repeat, realistically. There is the village priest who pursues the shoemaker's wife, and everyone knows that in the village the priest is the man who has studied, the one who speaks best, the one with the most money, and therefore, well, he is successful with the female parishioners. There is the passing student given a bed for the night by the miller who, that night, sets his eye on the miller's daughter, and so forth. Here too there is extreme freedom, including freedom of language, in describing these situations. One would say: if there is one place where some author ought to tell us about the *ius primae noctis*, surely it will be in this medieval storytelling. What a subject, what a narrative device the *ius primae noctis* would be! It is never found. There is not a single medieval tale, not a single medieval joke or story about sex—and there are countless numbers of them—that ever speaks of the *ius primae noctis*. Do they not speak of the village lord perhaps setting his eye on the girls? Of course they do, because the village lord certainly does set his eye on the girls. How do they speak about it? Let me give you an example, a fifteenth-century French tale. In the fifteenth century a collection of tales comes out in France which is somewhat copied from the *Decameron* in its structure: there are one hundred of them, and they are called *The Hundred New Tales*, *Les Cent Nouvelles nouvelles*. Tale 24 tells of a count who at a certain point learns that in one of his villages there is a beautiful girl. The count is a good man, but, well, well, he does not pass up opportunities. So he goes to take a look. The girl really is pretty, and she is his subject. At this point, what does the count do? He sends her a go-between, sends one of his servants to tell her: look, the count likes you; if you are clever, if you agree, your fortune is made, yours and your family's. The girl answers: I am a subject of the count; I am ready to obey any order of his except where my honor is concerned. But if they mean to ask me for something contrary to my honor, which I value more highly than my own life—I do not even know the count. The servant goes to the count and reports this answer. The count is disappointed and, since by now he has got the idea into his head, he wanders around the girl's village. At last he finds her alone. The girl is alone, cutting grass near the wood. The count arrives and begins to court her somewhat brusquely. The girl does not want to agree, and the count becomes very forward and tells her: listen, keep still, because in any case you are not getting away from me. At this point the girl—and the whole point of the tale, naturally, lies in the girl's device for escaping this embarrassing situation—at this point the girl tells him: all right, given the situation, I agree, but listen, I ask you just one thing: take off your trousers, because they hurt me. The count had been prepared simply to unbutton his trousers. Riding trousers, very tight, made of leather, very difficult to pull off—he certainly was not going to take them off. But he says: all right, come on, if you want me to take them off, you take them off, because I cannot manage it on my own. He lies down, and the girl begins pulling at these very tight trousers. When she has got them halfway off, she jumps up and says: I am leaving. The count jumps to his feet and falls flat on the ground because he cannot move with his trousers caught that way. The girl gets to safety. The count thinks about it for a while, begins to laugh, decides that the girl has shown spirit and, since he is a good man, in fact draws a line under the matter. Indeed, at the first opportunity he arranges a grand marriage with another nobleman for this girl and gives her a large dowry to reward her for her spirit. With the best will in the world, if ever there was a tale where there might have been a little hint that all he had to do was say, I have the right, or the *ius primae*—no, nothing. There are medieval tales in which, every now and again, this idea of sexual performance as a kind of tax, a kind of obligation, does appear. There are a few tales, but within a completely different framework from the *ius primae noctis* as we imagine it. I shall tell you one as an example. We are still in this fifteenth-century French collection. It is a tale about what happened when the Franciscans first established themselves in Catalonia, in the city of Girona. They built their friary and began to do what friars always do, that is, talk with people, hear penitents' confessions, give good advice; and, as always happens, many people go to the Franciscans to confess. Many women too, including many pretty women, and somehow or other, after a while, the Franciscans begin to—well, there are all these beautiful penitents and the Franciscans begin to feel desire. I am still telling this fifteenth-century tale. As you can see, in the Middle Ages they have no difficulty telling stories; they are not afraid of offending anyone. At a certain point one of the Franciscans has a brilliant idea. When the beautiful penitent comes to confess to him, the Franciscan tells her: my dear lady, you must know, however, that we Franciscans have rights too; the pope gave them to us. You too must pay your share. I am entirely ready. What must I do? And he says: you know that the tithe must be paid, yes? Certainly: you pay the tithe to the lord, you pay the tithe to the parish priest, and we Franciscans too are entitled to a tithe—a tithe of all the nights of love between you and your husband. So you should know, my dear, that from now on you must keep count and come and pay the tithe. The parishioner, completely convinced, pays the tithe. After a while the other Franciscans discover the trick, and all the female parishioners learn that the tithe on nights of love has to be paid; they are all thoroughly convinced and, with great zeal to save their souls, come to the friary to pay the tithe. Then, well, the tale ends badly, in the sense that the husbands find out, take it rather badly, and burn down the friary with all the friars inside, and that is how it ends. It is a joke, as you understand; it is a story. It shows us that these people thought about sex and knew that there was a connection between sex and power, that sex is a complicated game, a complex social situation, that many things happen in the sexual sphere. But with the best will in the world, the *ius primae noctis* as we know it: less than zero. Let us try another check.

## 3. Peasant obligations and the documentary silence

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00281–U-00352 -->

The *ius primae noctis* is known as one of the peasant's obligations toward his lord. Are we informed about what peasants' obligations toward their lords generally were? Do we know, in particular, which obligations weighed most heavily, which were irritating, which people rebelled against? We certainly do. We have an immense body of documentation on medieval peasants' complaints against all the obligations they were forced to bear under lordship. Because all sorts of things happened. For example, at a certain point it begins to happen fairly often that the peasants, who in the meantime have made money and are tired of having certain obligations, are tired of having to go—what do I know—every night there has to be a man standing guard on the castle tower, so all the families in turn have to send a man. It is a considerable nuisance. Or, to speak of things that are still more demanding because they affect money, all the village lands belong to the lord. The peasants have the right to work them in perpetuity, but they pay rent. The peasants can also sell them among themselves, but they always belong to the lord, and they have to pay the lord a certain tax when they buy or sell his lands. And in many lordships the lord says: when my man dies, if he has children, yes, they inherit the lands that are mine; but if he has no sons, I take the lands back. I do not allow one of my peasants to decide for himself, make a will, and leave the land perhaps to a woman, who might then marry somebody from another village, and nobody knows what happens within my lordship. No wills. After a while the peasants are annoyed by all this, and very often—indeed, almost everywhere; there is scarcely a village in Europe where sooner or later in the Middle Ages this does not happen—the peasants sit down at a table with the lord, put money on the table, and say: we would like to abolish this obligation, that one, that one, and that one; in exchange, we will pay. And it is done. A fine parchment is drawn up and everything is written down. If the lord does not agree, it happens that the peasants go to the king and tell him: we are not doing well under our lord. Why not? Because he demands this, that, and the other. This one and this one were already paid by our grandfathers, fair enough, but this other thing he is demanding is new, an abuse. We do not want to do this. The king intervenes and judges. The lawyers become involved, the peasants pay a lawyer, and the legal briefs arrive. In the archives we have enormous bundles containing lists of all the obligations of these peasants who cannot bear any more and are prepared to do anything to eliminate them. And even if the trial does not go well, it happens that the peasants rebel. They rebel and perhaps even kill the lord, or, more often, the lord runs away. Then after a while an agreement is reached again; perhaps somebody has been killed, but in the end peace is made and everything is written down once more. The list of obligations weighing on medieval peasants—I have just mentioned a few by way of example—these obligations were extremely numerous, and people complained about every one of them and paid to redeem every one of them. You have already understood: the *ius primae noctis* is never mentioned. There is not one list of peasant grievances that refers to this obligation. These people, who are willing to pay a large amount of money to be rid of the nuisance of having to do one shift of work every week in the castle ditches, never so much as mention the *ius primae noctis*. At that point, let us make a methodological observation. An argument from silence must be handled carefully. It is not enough to say: this thing is never discussed, and therefore it did not exist. That, let us say, is a general rule of the profession. It is also true, however, that there are cases where, if something is never discussed even though it would have had to be discussed if it had existed—well, there are cases where, frankly, the argument from silence puts you in some difficulty. Until the fifteenth century, and therefore for almost the whole of the Middle Ages, there is no testimony speaking of anything resembling the *ius primae noctis*. Although sometimes we find something that helps us understand how what, at this point, I feel able to call this legend later arose. Sometimes we find some clue that helps us understand how people began to embroider upon this fantasy.

## 4. The thirteenth-century evidence from Verson

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00353–U-00419 -->

Let me cite the earliest case that has been found. It is a document from 1247. We are in France. It is a list of the customary obligations of the peasants in a village. Since that village belongs half to a lay lord and half to the monks of a monastery, the lord and the monks often quarrel: that piece belongs to me, the other belongs to you. And so at a certain point they draw up a fine written list of all the obligations. Among the obligations of the peasants of this village of Verson, in thirteenth-century France, there is the following obligation. When a peasant marries off his daughter, if she marries a young man from another village, he has to pay a tax. This is nothing strange and nothing new. It is a very common clause in the lives of medieval peasants, and if it does not bore you too much I shall also explain why this clause exists. Follow me. The peasant works in the village and works the lord's land, and pays him rent. When his daughter marries, perhaps he gives her part of that land as a dowry; the son-in-law will continue to work that land and pay the lord the rent, so there is no problem. But if the son-in-law is from another village, who can assure the lord that he will manage to compel him to pay the rent, that he really will work, that he will come to live there? Nobody can assure him of that. It is another village; there is another lord. So the lord of Verson, like the lords of countless other medieval villages, is not pleased if his peasants' daughters marry a young man from another village. And so he says: if you truly want to do it, you must pay me; I want payment for giving my consent. This tax, then, is extremely widespread. But we are telling the specific story of the village of Verson in 1247. There is this list in Latin enumerating all these obligations. At a certain point a monk of that abbey, amusing himself when he had little to do, wrote a little rhyming poem in French that lists those same obligations: he translates this list of obligations into French and into verse, and at a certain point he adds something. When he reaches the tax that peasants have to pay when their daughter marries a young man from another village, the monk says: today three *sous* are paid, but the peasants are happy with that, because once it was much worse. There was a time when so much had to be paid that the peasants sometimes could not manage it, and there were fathers who, rather than having to give the lord a head of livestock or a vineyard or an income, chose the alternative recalled by the poem: “Long ago it happened that the peasant took his daughter by the hand and delivered her to his lord.” This is the very first mention, in a medieval text among the millions of medieval texts and documents, of something that vaguely resembles the *ius primae noctis*. Why do I say vaguely? Notice: because it is not spoken of as a right, and it is spoken of as something the father does so as not to have to pay the heavy tax. But above all, the important thing is that already in this very first mention—as we shall see, it will happen regularly afterward—the *ius primae noctis*, or something resembling it, forgive me, something resembling it, is discussed by saying: in the bad old days. And this is the key to understanding how, at the end of the Middle Ages, people began to fantasize about what happened in the bad old days.

## 5. The legend spreads in Europe

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00420–U-00598 -->

At the end of the Middle Ages, there are many places, villages, or even towns that had been created centuries earlier by groups of peasants who came to an agreement, left their village, said goodbye to the lord, founded a town of their own, and went to live freely in that town. It is an extremely common phenomenon in the Middle Ages. In some regions it is more common than in others. For various incidental reasons it is very common in Piedmont, for example, where several towns, from Cuneo to Alessandria and Cherasco, came into being through this mechanism. In Cuneo itself, at the end of the fifteenth century, this is what happens. Three hundred years have passed since the town was founded. At the end of the fifteenth century a learned man from Cuneo, the lawyer Rebaccini, decides to write Cuneo's history and tells how Cuneo came into being: how our ancestors, long ago, decided to abandon their lords, leave their villages, and go to live in a new town. And the lawyer Rebaccini says they left because it was burdensome to live under the lords' rule, and he begins to list a whole series of impositions that the lords inflicted on their peasants: they did not allow them to make wills; they forced them to pay whenever someone had to sell land; they took the tithe, took the *taglia*; on every contract they took a share; they forced them to perform unpaid labor services. The lawyer Rebaccini says: they forced them to work without discretion—because if they had done it with discretion, but instead, no. And notice that all these complaints correspond exactly to what documents from three hundred years earlier really tell us: these obligations and this burden existed in the lordships. The lawyer Rebaccini tells and tells how bad those old days were, how tyrannical those feudal lords were, and then at the very end, when everything is truly finished, finally: “subordinating reason to lust and sensuality, they deflowered their subjects' daughters and likewise their brides, persuading the subjects that this was their ancient right and a privilege granted to them.” As with Rebaccini in Cuneo, so too in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in several Italian and French towns, there is some good citizen who sets about telling the history of the town and begins with the time when, three hundred years earlier, four hundred years earlier, their great-great-grandfathers rebelled against the wicked lords. And with many of them this thing now enters circulation, does it not? Those lords really were wicked, and they even claimed this. If someone did not pay, they took the girls' virginity. In other words, once we reach the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries—and therefore between the end of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance—we suddenly find ourselves facing a Europe in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* has entered circulation and is beginning to resurface. And it always, always resurfaces with this characteristic: it speaks of an extremely distant past. In the bad old days these things happened. No, that is not true. It is not always the bad old days among us that people talk about. There is another way in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* circulates in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe. Not among us in the bad old days, but now among the savages. Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe—let us provide a little context quickly—is the Europe of the great geographical discoveries. During the fifteenth century the Portuguese ventured out into the Atlantic; they crossed over, went down below the Cape of Good Hope, discovered new lands everywhere, and then, of course, at the end of the fifteenth century Columbus sets out and reaches America, and then in the early sixteenth century the *conquistadores* go. A whole array of new worlds are discovered. Each time navigators arrive in a new place, they publish reports describing what that place is like. These reports by Portuguese and Spanish navigators and conquerors all resemble one another. The inhabitants of these islands are savages. You cannot imagine how savage they are. They go naked, they have no morality, they do the worst things. Just imagine that among them, when someone marries, the village chief has the right to sleep with the girl; and sometimes not just the village chief: if a merchant marries, all the merchants go; if a nobleman marries, all the noblemen; if instead he is a commoner, the master goes, naturally. When the Portuguese discover the Canaries in the mid-fifteenth century, they tell this story. When Alvise Cadamosto goes to Tenerife, he says that this happens in Tenerife. When Cortés goes to Mexico he tells—or rather, earlier still, in Cuba—he tells that this used to happen in Cuba. Is it clear why they tell these stories? Because we are reducing these savage peoples to slavery, if not exterminating them. Because, God willing, we are bringing them reason and the true faith, and consequently it is necessary to show that they are in great need of them: here we come to bring them civilization. These savages are truly beneath the human level; they do inconceivable things. It goes without saying that no Spaniard or Portuguese ever imagined that such things happened in his own world, because otherwise it would not occur to him to present them as an example of strangeness. But when savages are discussed, anything goes. Let us also say one thing, because matters are always more complex and the world is not made entirely one way or the other. These books appear, a great many appear, people believe them, and there is also someone who protests. Cortés's report saying that these wicked things happened in Cuba is published by Francisco López de Gómara in the *General History of the West Indies and the New Lands Discovered*, and someone protests, because in that strange Europe, after all, there is also someone who says: but none of this is true; we are wronging these Indians, they must be defended, what we are doing to them is shameful. Las Casas, the Jesuit, speaking precisely of this book, says: this book denigrates those peoples, none of it is true, and he goes so far as to have the king ban it because it slanders the Indians. In the meantime, however, it has been translated into French, Montaigne reads it, everyone reads it, and in short these stories continue to circulate. So let us take stock again. In fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe the story of the *ius primae noctis* circulates widely. There are two possibilities: did it happen among us in the bad old days, or does it happen now among the savages? Among the savages, among the barbarians—because everyone, after all, can have whichever barbarian they want. In England between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the England of the great Queen Elizabeth and of Shakespeare—Shakespeare... there is a country that good English people do not consider barbarous and savage; indeed, it ought to be a very civilized country, but it has become horribly barbarized. It is a country in an abyss of wickedness, and it is Italy, naturally. It is Italy where, on the one hand, the pope rules—and for good English Protestants papism is the worst thing in the world—and at the same time in Italy there is Machiavelli, the cynic, the immoral man. There: for the English in the early seventeenth century, Italy is the most immoral country in the world. In 1619 a play by two successful authors, Beaumont and Fletcher, is staged in London. It is entitled *The Custom of the Country*. And it tells of a country, a town so barbaric that in that town the governor demands the *ius primae noctis* over all the women who marry there, until naturally a couple more energetic than the others rebels, et cetera, et cetera. Where is that town? Why, in Italy, naturally. In substance, for intellectuals and writers, but also for the public of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the *ius primae noctis* has become a formidable device for describing all those worlds we do not like, which we consider barbaric, whether the distant medieval past or the present of savages or Italians, depending on the case, precisely. That is not all. At this point something else happens that helps consolidate the legend that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* really existed.

## 6. Jurists, marriage taxes, and false etymologies

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00599–U-00735 -->

The sixteenth century is an age of jurists. It is an age of jurists who begin, in particular, to analyze the obligations of peasants, the customs of lordships, the feudal customs, as people begin to call them in that period. In France, for example, these jurists are in the king's service, and the king would be very happy to cut the lords' power down a little. In the culture of these jurists, the lords' power begins to seem like an antiquated relic, something that ought to be eliminated. Let us remember that in 1789, after two and a half centuries of reasoning of this kind, finally, with the French Revolution, the first thing they do is abolish the feudal regime. All this begins in the sixteenth century. The sixteenth-century jurists investigate and discover that in many lordships a tax is paid on marriage. Now, the sixteenth-century jurists are cultured people, how shall I put it, but also fond of enjoying themselves. They too like slightly obscene tales. It is the century of Aretino; it is the century of Rabelais. At the universities there is much joking and amusement, and all these jurists were students before they became jurists, and, in short, they also know that very often, when a peasant pays a tax, it is because there was once a service in kind and later the peasant redeemed it. We have seen this mechanism. When the jurist—imagine the jurist at his desk piled high with law codes—he is extremely learned, but he is also ready to believe anything. He has to explain, because jurists of the period have to explain why this tax is paid. There is a tax on marriages. When the peasant marries off his daughter, he has to pay. Could it be that there was once a service in kind that has now been redeemed? And of course it must be so. Wherever they find a marriage tax, the jurists imagine that the *ius primae noctis* existed before it. And entire treatises appear in which this information is dispensed as though it were now an established fact. By now it is known that this happened in the past, and the historians too, by now, read the jurists—and if the jurists do not know, well then historians invent when a piece is missing. There is a book that has enormous influence. It comes out in 1526; it is a history of Scotland by an author named Boece, Hector Boece. To give you an idea how reliable Boece's *History of Scotland* is—even though he is a Renaissance intellectual, a learned humanist—to give you an idea how reliable it is, I shall tell you that according to him Scotland was founded by a princess named Scotia who was the daughter of the pharaoh, the one from Moses, the one in the Bible. In this work, whose credibility you can appreciate, Boece says: among us in Scotland peasants pay a marriage tax. This tax was introduced by the good King Malcolm in the eleventh century, on the advice of his wife, the saintly Queen Margaret, to replace an earlier tax—no, not a tax, an earlier obligation, a wicked, filthy obligation that I do not even dare tell you what it was—which had been introduced so many centuries earlier, in the days of the barbarians, the Celts, by King Evenus of Scotland: the obligation whereby, indeed, the girls with the lord, et cetera, et cetera, et cetera. With the best will in the world, your knees really buckle, because—no, for heaven's sake—King Malcolm existed, and his wife Margaret too; obviously they never did anything of the kind. King Evenus goes hand in hand with Princess Scotia, daughter of the pharaoh. He is completely made up, because when these people do not know how to fill the gaps, they invent. And books circulate; everyone reads books. Ah, I was forgetting, let me tell you this. I have related two or three of these things, though not the business about Boece and Scotland. I discussed the *ius primae noctis* in an article that appeared in *La Stampa* three or four days ago, in which I said: look, the *ius primae noctis* is a legend, and so on. This morning I open my mail and a very courteous gentleman has written to me, saying: dear professor, I read your article; the *ius primae noctis* certainly may be a legend, but I understand that in some cases it really existed. Du Cange's historical dictionary says that in Scotland King Evenus introduced the *ius*. I read this in my mail this morning, there. So, as you can see, things circulate. Jurists believe everything. It must be said that now and again the situation also helps them. The taxes that peasants paid in the Middle Ages had many names. There were many, for example, whose name began with the idea of the *colletta*. We all know what *colletta* means: collecting money. So in many cases, when the lord made the rounds and said it was time to pay, that thing was called the *colletta*. *Colletta* in Tuscan, but in the various languages of Europe it took different names. In French it was often called *coillage*, for example. In the common language, a tax paid on various occasions was called *coillage*. Sometimes even the marriage tax was called *coillage*. But of course the people called it *coillage*, while in Latin documents the notary made an effort to translate this word from the spoken language into Latin. And some notaries—perhaps precisely because they too had tremendous fun making fools of the peasants and their lords, those coarse ignoramuses—some notaries, when they had to transcribe the word *coillage* in Latin, wrote *culagium*. Imagine the sixteenth-century jurists when they discover that a peasant, when his daughter marries, has to pay the lord a tax called *culagium*. It is the end of the world. But that is not all. Another example. In the Middle Ages a name for taxes is universally widespread, a word no longer used today and one that many of you may never have heard: *fodro*. The word *fodro*: specialists in medieval history know it very well, whereas it no longer exists in spoken language today. In fact it is a word with a very clear origin. It is the same word as English *fodder*, meaning forage, food for animals. A tax is paid to feed the king's or lord's horses: *fodro*. But after a while the scholars of the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries no longer know the origin of this word. The *fodro* is paid—what can it possibly be? At Nizza Monferrato, then called Nizza della Paglia, in Piedmont, the *fodro* is paid. In Nizza Monferrato the local legend says that the peasants founded the town by rebelling against a wicked lord. In the eighteenth century a scholar arrives, Abbot Giulio Cesare Cordara, who puts the two things together: at Nizza Monferrato there was the wicked lord, and a tax was paid called the *fodro*. *Fodro*—what can this possibly be? *Fodero*, *fodero*—there, that is where *fodero* comes from: a sheath. But what is this sheath? It must be an image, a metaphor: the sheath, the scabbard, the vagina. There, that is what these poor peasants had to pay. And at the end of the eighteenth century Abbot Cordara writes a poem: *The Sheath, or the Ius over Brides of the Ancient Lords of Nizza Monferrato*. One little step further. Once everyone has convinced themselves that these rights existed in the past, they go looking for further proof, and when you go looking for further proof, you find it.

## 7. The Church and the first-night marriage tax

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00736–U-00784 -->

An example—an example of a document that countless scholars have cited as proof that not only did the *ius primae noctis* exist in the Middle Ages, but even the Church demanded it from its peasants. The starting point is a trial, a trial held before the Parlement of Paris concerning the diocese of Amiens. We are at the end of the fourteenth century. What is the premise? In the Middle Ages, the Church, as it does today and perhaps even more than today—indeed, often more than today—made an effort to remind Christians that sex within marriage is all very well, but it has to be kept under control because it is intended exclusively for procreation. Consequently, one must not let oneself go too much in sex, even within marriage; indeed, it is good and right to learn to keep lust under control. It happens in some medieval dioceses that bishops begin explaining to their faithful that it would be a fine thing if, when they marry, they remained chaste for three days, so as to show that they know how to master their lust and that they are not marrying for that. In some dioceses, in one way or another, this becomes an obligation imposed by the Church. When you marry, for three days you cannot consummate the marriage. After that, the late Middle Ages—the early Middle Ages, before the year 1000, is a stagnant era. Not afterward: afterward everyone makes money, endless amounts of money circulate, and people also become very brisk and very cynical. You pay for everything. In the late Middle Ages many people begin to say: yes, all right, I am getting married, I ought to observe the three days of chastity, but what if instead I made an offering to the Church? And the Church says, splendid, make the offering. After a while there is nobody left who observes the three days of chastity, but there are dioceses where it is normal for a person who marries to have to pay a certain tax to the Church. What do they call it? Various things, but mostly the tax on the first wedding night. Because that is precisely what I am paying for. At the end of the fourteenth century—this too is an old story—in the diocese of Amiens people no longer pay it. The bishop is annoyed and brings a lawsuit to force people to continue paying this tax. The people protest; the matter goes to the Parlement of Paris; a trial ensues that lasts sixteen years, and in the end I no longer even remember what the Parlement of Paris decides. But anyway, in any event, this trial—you can imagine it—consists of bundles this thick, in Latin, of legal arguments in Latin. When eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historians discover this trial, it is not as though they sit down and read the whole thing—you really cannot understand a thing—but one thing is clear: it speaks of a tax on the first night of marriage, and it was the Church that demanded the tax on the first night of marriage from the peasants. It is proof that the *ius primae noctis* existed.

## 8. Lords adopt the legend and marriage rituals blur it

One last thing, the most curious if you like: between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, more or less everyone becomes convinced that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* existed. The lords too hear this, because lordship still exists. Until the feudal regime is abolished with the French Revolution, lordship exists. The lords are less powerful than before; the king has taken away a good many powers and revenues from them. Nevertheless, the peasant still always has to deal with the lord and pays many taxes. The lords learn that this *ius primae noctis* existed in the past. That is to say: just a moment, perhaps it existed in my lordship too. My ancestors—my ancestors certainly had the right. So there are two possible reactions. A scholar from the beginning of the seventeenth century tells us this story. He writes: “Such a custom formerly existed in Piedmont among pagans and gentiles, and the Most Illustrious Cardinal Hieronimo della Rovere told me that he himself had burned the privilege that his house possessed concerning it.” Who knows what was in that parchment? A marriage tax, the *culagium*; who knows what was there. The cardinal took it at face value; since he was a cardinal, he burned it. In our civilized times such things are no longer done. But there are also lords who are not cardinals and who have fewer scruples. There are lords who perhaps quarrel often with their peasants—indeed, lords quarrel continually with peasants, because it is all very well to say these are the rights, these are the obligations; people do not want to pay taxes, and so they quarrel continually and go to court. Every now and then it happens that a lord is summoned by a court to state what, in his view, his rights are. Beginning in the fifteenth century, a few cases have been identified, five in all as far as I know: one in the fifteenth century, three in the sixteenth, one at the end of the seventeenth, still under the Sun King, in various godforsaken places. A local lord, summoned by a court to declare what his rights and his peasants' obligations were, said: they pay me this, they pay me that, the lamb at Easter and the pig at Christmas, ten *sous* when they buy land, twenty *sous* when they move house, five *sous* when a daughter marries; and those five *sous* when a daughter marries are because I would have the right to sleep with her on the first night, but my ancestors agreed to commute this right, and so now I... though I could always start demanding this again if my peasants do not behave themselves. So, as you can see, the fantasy becomes not exactly reality, but a kind of hypothesis of what is possible. There are five among the hundreds of thousands of lords who lived in Europe who quarreled so much with their peasants that they went and told them: look, if you do not stop making me angry, I shall bring out the *ius primae noctis*. Perhaps it would also be worth mentioning this—we are almost at the end, but we still have five minutes. Marriage in Europe at the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the modern period is not a very private matter. On the contrary, it is a very public matter, and one that is also at the center of many conflicts that people accept fairly readily, even though they are conflicts and can create problems. Let me give you an example of conflict. A girl from the village marries someone from another town, another village. We have already said that this annoys the lord and that a payment therefore has to be made. But it also annoys the other young men of the village. She is one of ours and one of those others has come to take her and is carrying her away. It is normal, when one of their female companions marries and goes to live in another village, for the young men of the village to have the road blocked when the wedding procession is about to leave. The road is blocked, all the young men of the village are there, and they tell the bridegroom: if you want to take her away, buy us a drink. And ninety-nine times out of a hundred the bridegroom gives in—it is understood—and buys everyone a drink. Then they remove the barrier and let him pass. One time in a hundred the bridegroom is someone who has already had a little to drink, becomes angry, they fight, and someone is killed. The wedding banquet—the wedding banquet and then the moment of the first wedding night are also moments of this kind. The banquet: everyone comes. In the novelist Falcones's imagination, the lord happens to turn up by chance; he did not know that the peasant was getting married. Just imagine. Everyone knew about these things, and the lord was usually invited to the banquet, most certainly, because it is important to keep him well disposed; and he too needs to keep wealthy peasants well disposed, so he goes—he will later baptize their children. He goes to the banquet and is keen to go to the banquet and sit in the place of honor. Every now and again there is also some lord who quarrels with the peasants because they invited him but did not give him sufficiently good wine. He was supposed to drink better wine, and he has the right to drink the best wine at the banquet, not the wine they give second-class guests. And then, when the bride finally goes to bed with her husband, there is an endless variety of possible rituals. Generally all the male guests prepare the husband and the women prepare the wife. They undress them, put their nightshirts on them, play a few jokes, make a few quips, a few obscenities, and then finally shut them in the room. There are lordships where the lord says: I am the one who has the right to escort the bride to the bedroom, because I am the lord of this village and it must be seen. In other words, there is a whole folkloric context in which obligations—yes, obligations toward the community—can be woven around marriage. And it is in this context that, if scholars believe there used to be a *ius primae noctis*, well, it does not take much for someone to begin believing it in earnest too. After that,

## 9. Power, abuse, and Orwell's invented past

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00863–U-00913 -->

let us conclude. The *ius primae noctis* is an extraordinary fantasy that the Middle Ages created, which arose at the end of the Middle Ages, and in which people believed so strongly that there was almost a risk that someone would want to put it into practice for real, although there is no indication that this ever really happened. In reality it is a fantasy; it never existed. Does that mean the lord did not sometimes sleep with the village girl? No, it does not mean that. The lord slept with the village girl, by fair means or foul. These are things that happen in every unequal society. But there is an immense difference between a society in which it happens that the rich man takes advantage and sleeps with the poor girl, and imagining instead—which is true madness—a society where this is a right to which everyone must resign themselves because the law says so. To say that the lord slept with the village girls is like saying that in the nineteenth century—that in the nineteenth century the owner of the spinning mill or his son sometimes slept with a female worker. It is as if the owner of the spinning mill sometimes slept with the female worker. There were a thousand reasons why this could happen, but obviously no one would have dreamed of saying it was a right. To speak of it as a law means wanting to invent a past. And so I would close with a passage that I find extraordinary, which speaks precisely of the invention of the past. It is taken from Orwell's novel *1984*. At one point in Orwell's novel it discusses how, under Big Brother's regime, the past was reinvented for the people. In the past, the people are taught, there were evil creatures called capitalists, and the capitalists oppressed the people in every way, with every kind of abuse and oppression. I quote verbatim: there was also something called the *ius primae noctis*, which probably ought not to be mentioned in schoolbooks for children. It was the law by which every capitalist had the right to sleep with any woman working in one of his factories. There. I find this fantastic because Orwell has identified the mechanism exactly. Something that can happen in reality, and is an act of violence and an effect of social relations, is imagined to have been a law. Because imagining a society in which such a thing could be a law means imagining a society truly alien and totally barbaric in relation to our values. And so, precisely, when we want to rewrite the past and imagine it, and be pleased that we live in a much better world than the past, the *ius primae noctis* is perfect for furnishing that past. Thank you.
