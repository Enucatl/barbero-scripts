## 1. The modern image of the right of the first night

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00146 -->

Well, good evening. So tonight's subject is perhaps the best known of all those we shall touch on here over these three evenings. I think we all have in mind this image that we associate with the Middle Ages, the *ius primae noctis*. And so—I contradict myself, we all have it in mind, but let us say what it is anyway. The right of the lord of the village to have the virginity of all the girls in the village, or at least all those who are not noblewomen, all the female serfs and peasant women, especially when they marry: to sleep with them before the husband, on the first wedding night. This image is extremely widespread in the way people perceive the Middle Ages today, so much so that we continually come across it whenever there is a historical novel or a film set in the Middle Ages. Perhaps “whenever” is saying too much, but we come across it all the time. Let me give you a couple of examples, to recall things that will already be familiar to many of you. I could give you a great many; let us do two. One from historical fiction. A few years ago, in 2006, I believe, a historical novel comes out and enjoys immense success, by a Spanish author named Ildefonso Falcones. The novel is called *The Cathedral of the Sea*. It is a grand story set in the Middle Ages, in fourteenth-century Catalonia, a story of struggle for freedom and against oppression, and it opens with a great scene entirely centered on the *ius primae noctis*. You know that a historical novel, like every serialized novel, has to begin with a powerful scene. There has to be something that catches the reader immediately; if there is a dead body, so much the better, but in any event something very powerful. *The Cathedral of the Sea* opens with this extremely violent scene of the *ius primae noctis*. There is a peasant wedding. There is the peasant who is to be married, and they are prosperous peasants, people who work, who are doing well, respectable people. There is the wedding banquet in the countryside, and at a certain point, suddenly, the lord turns up on horseback, accompanied by his henchmen. Everyone is very frightened that the lord has arrived there, but obviously they can do nothing. They invite him to sit down, and the lord sits at the table with the other guests and begins to drink. He notices that the bride is a beautiful woman and wants her to be the one who pours his wine. He forces her to come over, touches her, and then, when he has drunk his fill, the lord jumps up, takes the bride by the wrist, calls the bridegroom, and tells him: “In exercise of the right that belongs to me as your lord, I have decided to lie with your wife on her first night.” After that he drags the woman inside, then comes out after a while buttoning his trousers and says to the husband: “Now it is your turn. […] Do your duty as a good Christian husband!” And nobody has lifted a finger. They are all dismayed, horrified by what is happening, but not one of those peasants lifts a finger, because the lord has the right to do what he is doing. Then this *ius primae noctis*, so violent that it is actually rape, will have enormous consequences. The whole novel starts from there, which is simply to say that the *ius primae noctis* is a formidable narrative device. It sets a whole machine in motion: there is the villain, there are the good people who have suffered the abuse, there is the girl who loses her mind after this violence, and after a while the husband leaves the village and goes to live in the city, where at least there is freedom and no lords who have to be obeyed. Let me give you one more example; this one too may be superfluous, but never mind. A film many of us have seen, Mel Gibson's *Braveheart*. A great big medieval film, the story of the rebellion of the Scots under the leadership of the heroic William Wallace against the wicked English rulers. There too, at a certain point, the *ius primae noctis* comes into play to move the plot forward. It is a most curious scene. The king of England—wicked, naturally—is talking with his barons, and at a certain point he says: the problem with Scotland is that it is full of Scots, but I have found the way to put an end to the Scots. The time has come to restore an old tradition. Let us grant the *ius primae noctis*. From now on our nobles will have the right to sleep with Scottish girls before they marry, and in this way the implication is that the race of these rebels will be mongrelized. Notice that while in Falcones's novel the author has rather courageously tried to imagine a world where the *ius primae noctis* exists as something that one cannot avoid accepting—it is an obligation, it is a law, and nobody rebels—in the film *Braveheart*, and you will understand later why it is important to stress this aspect, the perspective is a little different. The king says: let us restore an old custom. And of course the consequence, when the English begin to demand the *ius primae noctis*, is that the Scots finally rise in revolt. This is a connection that we find extremely often when the *ius primae noctis* is discussed. There are lots of Italian towns or villages whose tradition, whose legend, includes a distant past in which an evil feudal lord oppresses the inhabitants by demanding the *ius primae noctis*, until naturally a girl braver than the others, or a husband braver than the others, rebels and puts an end to the tyranny. It is a long list; by looking on the Internet one could add still more. How many places have this story in their memory, in their tradition? Fiuggi; Sant'Agata di Puglia; Roccascalegna in Abruzzo; several villages in Liguria, Montalto Ligure, Onzo; several villages in Piedmont, Nizza Monferrato, Rocca Grimalda; even Ivrea, where the Ivrea Carnival—you know, the carnival where they throw oranges—is said today to originate in an episode in which an evil tyrant demands the *ius primae noctis* until a girl cleverer than the others rebels: Violetta, the beautiful miller's daughter. Let me open a small parenthesis. To imagine how old this story is, you need only consider that in the Middle Ages they have no idea what the name Violetta even is. The name Violetta becomes fashionable in the nineteenth century with *La traviata*. Before that there is nobody called Violetta. Parenthesis closed, because I do not want to influence your judgment. So it seems to me that we have established our first point: in today's culture, both in local folklore and in the culture of historical novelists and screenwriters—and therefore also of all of us who watch those films and read those novels—the *ius primae noctis* is a fairly regular presence whenever the Middle Ages are discussed. As you know, the wager behind these evenings of ours is to say: well, let us check. Let us see what testimony exists in the Middle Ages for the existence of the *ius primae noctis*. Let us go looking for it.
