## 5. The legend spreads in Europe

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00420–U-00598 -->

At the end of the Middle Ages, there are many places, villages, or even towns that had been created centuries earlier by groups of peasants who came to an agreement, left their village, said goodbye to the lord, founded a town of their own, and went to live freely in that town. It is an extremely common phenomenon in the Middle Ages. In some regions it is more common than in others. For various incidental reasons it is very common in Piedmont, for example, where several towns, from Cuneo to Alessandria and Cherasco, came into being through this mechanism. In Cuneo itself, at the end of the fifteenth century, this is what happens. Three hundred years have passed since the town was founded. At the end of the fifteenth century a learned man from Cuneo, the lawyer Rebaccini, decides to write Cuneo's history and tells how Cuneo came into being: how our ancestors, long ago, decided to abandon their lords, leave their villages, and go to live in a new town. And the lawyer Rebaccini says they left because it was burdensome to live under the lords' rule, and he begins to list a whole series of impositions that the lords inflicted on their peasants: they did not allow them to make wills; they forced them to pay whenever someone had to sell land; they took the tithe, took the *taglia*; on every contract they took a share; they forced them to perform unpaid labor services. The lawyer Rebaccini says: they forced them to work without discretion—because if they had done it with discretion, but instead, no. And notice that all these complaints correspond exactly to what documents from three hundred years earlier really tell us: these obligations and this burden existed in the lordships. The lawyer Rebaccini tells and tells how bad those old days were, how tyrannical those feudal lords were, and then at the very end, when everything is truly finished, finally: “subordinating reason to lust and sensuality, they deflowered their subjects' daughters and likewise their brides, persuading the subjects that this was their ancient right and a privilege granted to them.” As with Rebaccini in Cuneo, so too in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in several Italian and French towns, there is some good citizen who sets about telling the history of the town and begins with the time when, three hundred years earlier, four hundred years earlier, their great-great-grandfathers rebelled against the wicked lords. And with many of them this thing now enters circulation, does it not? Those lords really were wicked, and they even claimed this. If someone did not pay, they took the girls' virginity. In other words, once we reach the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries—and therefore between the end of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance—we suddenly find ourselves facing a Europe in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* has entered circulation and is beginning to resurface. And it always, always resurfaces with this characteristic: it speaks of an extremely distant past. In the bad old days these things happened. No, that is not true. It is not always the bad old days among us that people talk about. There is another way in which the tale of the *ius primae noctis* circulates in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe. Not among us in the bad old days, but now among the savages. Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe—let us provide a little context quickly—is the Europe of the great geographical discoveries. During the fifteenth century the Portuguese venture out into the Atlantic; they cross over, go down below the Cape of Good Hope, and discover new lands everywhere, and then, of course, at the end of the fifteenth century Columbus sets out and reaches America, and then in the early sixteenth century the *conquistadores* go. A whole array of new worlds are discovered. Each time navigators arrive in a new place, they publish reports describing what that place is like. These reports by Portuguese and Spanish navigators and conquerors all resemble one another. The inhabitants of these islands are savages. You cannot imagine how savage they are. They go naked, they have no morality, they do the worst things. Just imagine that among them, when someone marries, the village chief has the right to sleep with the girl; and sometimes not just the village chief: if a merchant marries, all the merchants go; if a nobleman marries, all the noblemen; if instead he is a commoner, the master goes, naturally. When the Portuguese discover the Canaries in the mid-fifteenth century, they tell this story. When Alvise Cadamosto goes to Tenerife, he says that this happens in Tenerife. When Cortés goes to Mexico he tells—or rather, earlier still, in Cuba—he tells that this used to happen in Cuba. Is it clear why they tell these stories? Because we are reducing these savage peoples to slavery, if not exterminating them. Because, God willing, we are bringing them reason and the true faith, and consequently it is necessary to show that they are in great need of them: here we come to bring them civilization. These savages are truly beneath the human level; they do inconceivable things. It goes without saying that no Spaniard or Portuguese imagines that such things happen in his own world, because otherwise it does not occur to him to present them as an example of strangeness. But when savages are discussed, anything goes. Let us also say one thing, because matters are always more complex and the world is not made entirely one way or the other. These books appear, a great many appear, people believe them, and there is also someone who protests. Cortés's report saying that these wicked things happened in Cuba is published by Francisco López de Gómara in the *General History of the West Indies and the New Lands Discovered*, and someone protests, because in that strange Europe, after all, there is also someone who says: but none of this is true; we are wronging these Indians, they must be defended, what we are doing to them is shameful. Bartolomé de las Casas, a Dominican friar and bishop, accuses Gómara of calumniating Indigenous peoples; the surviving evidence does not establish that Las Casas personally caused the crown to prohibit the book. [N-005] In the meantime, however, it has been translated into French, Montaigne reads it, everyone reads it, and in short these stories continue to circulate. So let us take stock again. In fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Europe the story of the *ius primae noctis* circulates widely. There are two possibilities: did it happen among us in the bad old days, or does it happen now among the savages? Among the savages, among the barbarians—because everyone, after all, can have whichever barbarian they want. In England between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the England of the great Queen Elizabeth and of Shakespeare—Shakespeare... there is a country that good English people do not consider barbarous and savage; indeed, it ought to be a very civilized country, but it has become horribly barbarized. It is a country in an abyss of wickedness, and it is Italy, naturally. It is Italy where, on the one hand, the pope rules—and for good English Protestants papism is the worst thing in the world—and at the same time in Italy there is Machiavelli, the cynic, the immoral man. There: for the English in the early seventeenth century, Italy is the most immoral country in the world. In 1619 a play by two successful authors, Beaumont and Fletcher, is staged in London. It is entitled *The Custom of the Country*. And it tells of a country, a town so barbaric that in that town the governor demands the *ius primae noctis* over all the women who marry there, until naturally a couple more energetic than the others rebels, et cetera, et cetera. Where is that town? Why, in Italy, naturally. In substance, for intellectuals and writers, but also for the public of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the *ius primae noctis* has become a formidable device for describing all those worlds we do not like, which we consider barbaric, whether the distant medieval past or the present of savages or Italians, depending on the case, precisely. That is not all. At this point something else happens that helps consolidate the legend that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* really existed.
