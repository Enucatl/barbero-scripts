## 4. The thirteenth-century evidence from Verson

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00353–U-00419 -->

Let me cite the earliest case that has been found. It is a document from 1247. We are in France. It is a list of the customary obligations of the peasants in a village. Since that village belongs half to a lay lord and half to the monks of a monastery, the lord and the monks often quarrel: that piece belongs to me, the other belongs to you. And so at a certain point they draw up a fine written list of all the obligations. Among the obligations of the peasants of this village of Verson, in thirteenth-century France, there is the following obligation. When a peasant marries off his daughter, if she marries a young man from another village, he has to pay a tax. This is nothing strange and nothing new. It is a very common clause in the lives of medieval peasants, and if it does not bore you too much I shall also explain why this clause exists. Follow me. The peasant works in the village and works the lord's land, and pays him rent. When his daughter marries, perhaps he gives her part of that land as a dowry; the son-in-law will continue to work that land and pay the lord the rent, so there is no problem. But if the son-in-law is from another village, who can assure the lord that he will manage to compel him to pay the rent, that he really will work, that he will come to live there? Nobody can assure him of that. It is another village; there is another lord. So the lord of Verson, like the lords of countless other medieval villages, is not pleased if his peasants' daughters marry a young man from another village. And so he says: if you truly want to do it, you must pay me; I want payment for giving my consent. This tax, then, is extremely widespread. But we are telling the specific story of the village of Verson in 1247. There is this list in Latin enumerating all these obligations. At a certain point a monk of that abbey, amusing himself when he has little to do, writes a little rhyming poem in French that lists those same obligations: he translates this list of obligations into French and into verse, and at a certain point he adds something. When he reaches the tax that peasants have to pay when their daughter marries a young man from another village, the monk says: today three *sous* are paid, but the peasants are happy with that, because once it was much worse. There was a time when so much had to be paid that the peasants sometimes could not manage it, and there were fathers who, rather than having to give the lord a head of livestock or a vineyard or an income, chose the alternative recalled by the poem: “Long ago it happened that the peasant took his daughter by the hand and delivered her to his lord.” This is the very first mention, in a medieval text among the millions of medieval texts and documents, of something that vaguely resembles the *ius primae noctis*. Why do I say vaguely? Notice: because it is not spoken of as a right, and it is spoken of as something the father does so as not to have to pay the heavy tax. But above all, the important thing is that already in this very first mention—as we shall see, it will happen regularly afterward—the *ius primae noctis*, or something resembling it, forgive me, something resembling it, is discussed by saying: in the bad old days. And this is the key to understanding how, at the end of the Middle Ages, people begin to fantasize about what happens in the bad old days.
