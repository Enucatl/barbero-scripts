## 7. The Church and the first-night marriage tax

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00736–U-00784 -->

An example—an example of a document that countless scholars have cited as proof that not only does the *ius primae noctis* exist in the Middle Ages, but even the Church demands it from its peasants. The starting point is a trial, a trial held before the Parlement of Paris concerning the diocese of Amiens. We are at the end of the fourteenth century. What is the premise? In the Middle Ages, the Church, as it does today and perhaps even more than today—indeed, often more than today—makes an effort to remind Christians that sex within marriage is all very well, but it has to be kept under control because it is intended exclusively for procreation. Consequently, one must not let oneself go too much in sex, even within marriage; indeed, it is good and right to learn to keep lust under control. It happens in some medieval dioceses that bishops begin explaining to their faithful that it would be a fine thing if, when they marry, they remained chaste for three days, so as to show that they know how to master their lust and that they are not marrying for that. In some dioceses, in one way or another, this becomes an obligation imposed by the Church. When you marry, for three days you cannot consummate the marriage. After that, the late Middle Ages—the early Middle Ages, before the year 1000, is a stagnant era. Not afterward: afterward everyone makes money, endless amounts of money circulate, and people also become very brisk and very cynical. You pay for everything. In the late Middle Ages many people begin to say: yes, all right, I am getting married, I ought to observe the three days of chastity, but what if instead I made an offering to the Church? And the Church says, splendid, make the offering. After a while there is nobody left who observes the three days of chastity, but there are dioceses where it is normal for a person who marries to have to pay a certain tax to the Church. What do they call it? Various things, but mostly the tax on the first wedding night. Because that is precisely what I am paying for. At the end of the fourteenth century—this too is an old story—in the diocese of Amiens people no longer pay it. The bishop is annoyed and brings a lawsuit to force people to continue paying this tax. The people protest; the matter goes to the Parlement of Paris; a trial ensues that lasts sixteen years, and in the end I no longer even remember what the Parlement of Paris decides. But anyway, in any event, this trial—you can imagine it—consists of bundles this thick, in Latin, of legal arguments in Latin. When eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historians discover this trial, it is not as though they sit down and read the whole thing—you really cannot understand a thing—but one thing is clear: it speaks of a tax on the first night of marriage, and it is the Church that demands the tax on the first night of marriage from the peasants. It is proof that the *ius primae noctis* existed.
