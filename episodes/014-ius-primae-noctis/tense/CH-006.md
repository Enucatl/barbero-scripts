## 6. Jurists, marriage taxes, and false etymologies

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00599–U-00735 -->

The sixteenth century is an age of jurists. It is an age of jurists who begin, in particular, to analyze the obligations of peasants, the customs of lordships, the feudal customs, as people begin to call them in that period. In France, for example, these jurists are in the king's service, and the king is very happy to cut the lords' power down a little. In the culture of these jurists, the lords' power begins to seem like an antiquated relic, something that ought to be eliminated. Let us remember that in 1789, after two and a half centuries of reasoning of this kind, finally, with the French Revolution, the first thing they do is abolish the feudal regime. All this begins in the sixteenth century. The sixteenth-century jurists investigate and discover that in many lordships a tax is paid on marriage. Now, the sixteenth-century jurists are cultured people, how shall I put it, but also fond of enjoying themselves. They too like slightly obscene tales. It is the century of Aretino; it is the century of Rabelais. At the universities there is much joking and amusement, and all these jurists were students before they became jurists, and, in short, they also know that very often, when a peasant pays a tax, it is because there was once a service in kind and later the peasant redeemed it. We have seen this mechanism. When the jurist—imagine the jurist at his desk piled high with law codes—he is extremely learned, but he is also ready to believe anything. He has to explain, because jurists of the period have to explain why this tax is paid. There is a tax on marriages. When the peasant marries off his daughter, he has to pay. Could it be that there was once a service in kind that has now been redeemed? And of course it must be so. Wherever they find a marriage tax, the jurists imagine that the *ius primae noctis* existed before it. And entire treatises appear in which this information is dispensed as though it were now an established fact. By now it is known that this happened in the past, and the historians too, by now, read the jurists—and if the jurists do not know, well then historians invent when a piece is missing. There is a book that has enormous influence. Hector Boece's *Scotorum Historia* is first printed in 1527. [N-007] To give you an idea how reliable Boece's *History of Scotland* is—even though he is a Renaissance intellectual, a learned humanist—to give you an idea how reliable it is, I shall tell you that according to him Scotland was founded by a princess named Scotia who was the daughter of the pharaoh, the one from Moses, the one in the Bible. In this work, whose credibility you can appreciate, Boece says: among us in Scotland peasants pay a marriage tax. This tax was introduced by the good King Malcolm in the eleventh century, on the advice of his wife, the saintly Queen Margaret, to replace an earlier tax—no, not a tax, an earlier obligation, a wicked, filthy obligation that I do not even dare tell you what it was—which had been introduced so many centuries earlier, in the days of the barbarians, the Celts, by King Evenus of Scotland: the obligation whereby, indeed, the girls with the lord, et cetera, et cetera, et cetera. With the best will in the world, your knees really buckle, because—no, for heaven's sake—King Malcolm existed, and his wife Margaret too; obviously they never did anything of the kind. King Evenus goes hand in hand with Princess Scotia, daughter of the pharaoh. He is completely made up, because when these people do not know how to fill the gaps, they invent. And books circulate; everyone reads books. Ah, I was forgetting, let me tell you this. I have related two or three of these things, though not the business about Boece and Scotland. I discussed the *ius primae noctis* in an article that appeared in *La Stampa* three or four days ago, in which I said: look, the *ius primae noctis* is a legend, and so on. This morning I open my mail and a very courteous gentleman has written to me, saying: dear professor, I read your article; the *ius primae noctis* certainly may be a legend, but I understand that in some cases it really existed. Du Cange's historical dictionary says that in Scotland King Evenus introduced the *ius*. I read this in my mail this morning, there. So, as you can see, things circulate. Jurists believe everything. It must be said that now and again the situation also helps them. The taxes that peasants pay in the Middle Ages have many names. There are many, for example, whose name begins with the idea of the *colletta*. We all know what *colletta* means: collecting money. So in many cases, when the lord makes the rounds and says it is time to pay, that thing is called the *colletta*. *Colletta* in Tuscan, but in the various languages of Europe it takes different names. In French it is often called *coillage*, for example. In the common language, a tax paid on various occasions is called *coillage*. Sometimes even the marriage tax is called *coillage*. But of course the people call it *coillage*, while in Latin documents the notary makes an effort to translate this word from the spoken language into Latin. And some notaries—perhaps precisely because they too have tremendous fun making fools of the peasants and their lords, those coarse ignoramuses—some notaries, when they have to transcribe the word *coillage* in Latin, write *culagium*. Imagine the sixteenth-century jurists when they discover that a peasant, when his daughter marries, has to pay the lord a tax called *culagium*. It is the end of the world. But that is not all. Another example. In the Middle Ages a name for taxes is universally widespread, a word no longer used today and one that many of you may never have heard: *fodro*. The word *fodro*: specialists in medieval history know it very well, whereas it no longer exists in spoken language today. In fact it is a word with a very clear origin. It is the same word as English *fodder*, meaning forage, food for animals. A tax is paid to feed the king's or lord's horses: *fodro*. But after a while the scholars of the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries no longer know the origin of this word. The *fodro* is paid—what can it possibly be? At Nizza Monferrato, then called Nizza della Paglia, in Piedmont, the *fodro* is paid. In Nizza Monferrato the local legend says that the peasants founded the town by rebelling against a wicked lord. In the eighteenth century a scholar arrives, Abbot Giulio Cesare Cordara, who puts the two things together: at Nizza Monferrato there is the wicked lord, and a tax is paid called the *fodro*. *Fodro*—what can this possibly be? *Fodero*, *fodero*—there, that is where *fodero* comes from: a sheath. But what is this sheath? It must be an image, a metaphor: the sheath, the scabbard, the vagina. There, that is what these poor peasants had to pay. And at the end of the eighteenth century Abbot Cordara writes a poem: *The Sheath, or the Ius over Brides of the Ancient Lords of Nizza Monferrato*. One little step further. Once everyone has convinced themselves that these rights existed in the past, they go looking for further proof, and when you go looking for further proof, you find it.
