## 3. Peasant obligations and the documentary silence

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00281–U-00352 -->

The *ius primae noctis* is known as one of the peasant's obligations toward his lord. Are we informed about what peasants' obligations toward their lords generally are? Do we know, in particular, which obligations weigh most heavily, which are irritating, which people rebelled against? We certainly do. We have an immense body of documentation on medieval peasants' complaints against all the obligations they are forced to bear under lordship. Because all sorts of things happen. For example, at a certain point it begins to happen fairly often that the peasants, who in the meantime have made money and are tired of having certain obligations, are tired of having to go—what do I know—every night there has to be a man standing guard on the castle tower, so all the families in turn have to send a man. It is a considerable nuisance. Or, to speak of things that are still more demanding because they affect money, all the village lands belong to the lord. The peasants have the right to work them in perpetuity, but they pay rent. The peasants can also sell them among themselves, but they always belong to the lord, and they have to pay the lord a certain tax when they buy or sell his lands. And in many lordships the lord says: when my man dies, if he has children, yes, they inherit the lands that are mine; but if he has no sons, I take the lands back. I do not allow one of my peasants to decide for himself, make a will, and leave the land perhaps to a woman, who might then marry somebody from another village, and nobody knows what happens within my lordship. No wills. After a while the peasants are annoyed by all this, and very often—indeed, almost everywhere; there is scarcely a village in Europe where sooner or later in the Middle Ages this does not happen—the peasants sit down at a table with the lord, put money on the table, and say: we would like to abolish this obligation, that one, that one, and that one; in exchange, we will pay. And it is done. A fine parchment is drawn up and everything is written down. If the lord does not agree, it happens that the peasants go to the king and tell him: we are not doing well under our lord. Why not? Because he demands this, that, and the other. This one and this one were already paid by our grandfathers, fair enough, but this other thing he is demanding is new, an abuse. We do not want to do this. The king intervenes and judges. The lawyers become involved, the peasants pay a lawyer, and the legal briefs arrive. In the archives we have enormous bundles containing lists of all the obligations of these peasants who cannot bear any more and are prepared to do anything to eliminate them. And even if the trial does not go well, it happens that the peasants rebel. They rebel and perhaps even kill the lord, or, more often, the lord runs away. Then after a while an agreement is reached again; perhaps somebody has been killed, but in the end peace is made and everything is written down once more. The list of obligations weighing on medieval peasants—I have just mentioned a few by way of example—these obligations are extremely numerous, and people complain about every one of them and pay to redeem every one of them. You have already understood: the *ius primae noctis* is never mentioned. There is not one list of peasant grievances that refers to this obligation. These people, who are willing to pay a large amount of money to be rid of the nuisance of having to do one shift of work every week in the castle ditches, never so much as mention the *ius primae noctis*. At that point, let us make a methodological observation. An argument from silence must be handled carefully. It is not enough to say: this thing is never discussed, and therefore it does not exist. That, let us say, is a general rule of the profession. It is also true, however, that there are cases where, if something is never discussed even though it would have had to be discussed if it had existed—well, there are cases where, frankly, the argument from silence puts you in some difficulty. Until the fifteenth century, and therefore for almost the whole of the Middle Ages, there is no testimony speaking of anything resembling the *ius primae noctis*. Although sometimes we find something that helps us understand how what, at this point, I feel able to call this legend later arises. Sometimes we find some clue that helps us understand how people begin to embroider upon this fantasy.
