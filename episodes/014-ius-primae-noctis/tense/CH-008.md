## 8. Lords adopt the legend and marriage rituals blur it

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00785–U-00862 -->

One last thing, the most curious if you like: between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, more or less everyone becomes convinced that in the bad old days the *ius primae noctis* existed. The lords too hear this, because lordship still exists. Until the feudal regime is abolished with the French Revolution, lordship exists. The lords are less powerful than before; the king has taken away a good many powers and revenues from them. Nevertheless, the peasant still always has to deal with the lord and pays many taxes. The lords learn that this *ius primae noctis* existed in the past. That is to say: just a moment, perhaps it existed in my lordship too. My ancestors—my ancestors certainly had the right. So there are two possible reactions. A scholar from the beginning of the seventeenth century tells us this story. He writes: “Such a custom formerly existed in Piedmont among pagans and gentiles, and the Most Illustrious Cardinal Hieronimo della Rovere told me that he himself had burned the privilege that his house possessed concerning it.” Who knows what was in that parchment? A marriage tax, the *culagium*; who knows what was there. The cardinal took it at face value; since he was a cardinal, he burned it. In our civilized times such things are no longer done. But there are also lords who are not cardinals and who have fewer scruples. There are lords who perhaps quarrel often with their peasants—indeed, lords quarrel continually with peasants, because it is all very well to say these are the rights, these are the obligations; people do not want to pay taxes, and so they quarrel continually and go to court. Every now and then it happens that a lord is summoned by a court to state what, in his view, his rights are. Beginning in the fifteenth century, a few cases have been identified, five in all as far as I know: one in the fifteenth century, three in the sixteenth, one at the end of the seventeenth, still under the Sun King, in various godforsaken places. A local lord, summoned by a court to declare what his rights and his peasants' obligations are, says: they pay me this, they pay me that, the lamb at Easter and the pig at Christmas, ten *sous* when they buy land, twenty *sous* when they move house, five *sous* when a daughter marries; and those five *sous* when a daughter marries are because I would have the right to sleep with her on the first night, but my ancestors agreed to commute this right, and so now I... though I could always start demanding this again if my peasants do not behave themselves. So, as you can see, the fantasy becomes not exactly reality, but a kind of hypothesis of what is possible. There are five among the hundreds of thousands of lords who lived in Europe who quarrel so much with their peasants that they go and tell them: look, if you do not stop making me angry, I'll bring out the *ius primae noctis*. Perhaps it would also be worth mentioning this—we are almost at the end, but we still have five minutes. Marriage in Europe at the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the modern period is not a very private matter. On the contrary, it is a very public matter, and one that is also at the center of many conflicts that people accept fairly readily, even though they are conflicts and can create problems. Let me give you an example of conflict. A girl from the village marries someone from another town, another village. We have already said that this annoys the lord and that a payment therefore has to be made. But it also annoys the other young men of the village. She is one of ours and one of those others has come to take her and is carrying her away. It is normal, when one of their female companions marries and goes to live in another village, for the young men of the village to have the road blocked when the wedding procession is about to leave. The road is blocked, all the young men of the village are there, and they tell the bridegroom: if you want to take her away, buy us a drink. And ninety-nine times out of a hundred the bridegroom gives in—it is understood—and buys everyone a drink. Then they remove the barrier and let him pass. One time in a hundred the bridegroom is someone who has already had a little to drink, becomes angry, they fight, and someone is killed. The wedding banquet—the wedding banquet and then the moment of the first wedding night are also moments of this kind. The banquet: everyone comes. In the novelist Falcones's imagination, the lord happens to turn up by chance; he does not know that the peasant is getting married. Just imagine. Everyone knows about these things, and the lord is usually invited to the banquet, most certainly, because it is important to keep him well disposed; and he too needs to keep wealthy peasants well disposed, so he goes—he will later baptize their children. He goes to the banquet and is keen to go to the banquet and sit in the place of honor. Every now and again there is also some lord who quarrels with the peasants because they invite him but do not give him sufficiently good wine. He is supposed to drink better wine, and he has the right to drink the best wine at the banquet, not the wine they give second-class guests. And then, when the bride finally goes to bed with her husband, there is an endless variety of possible rituals. Generally all the male guests prepare the husband and the women prepare the wife. They undress them, put their nightshirts on them, play a few jokes, make a few quips, a few obscenities, and then finally shut them in the room. There are lordships where the lord says: I am the one who has the right to escort the bride to the bedroom, because I am the lord of this village and it must be seen. In other words, there is a whole folkloric context in which obligations—yes, obligations toward the community—can be woven around marriage.
