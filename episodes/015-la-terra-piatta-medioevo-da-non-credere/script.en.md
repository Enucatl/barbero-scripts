# Unbelievable Middle Ages: Flat Earth

## 1. The modern myth of a flat medieval Earth

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. Tonight we're going to talk about another superstition—not a medieval superstition, of course, but one of our superstitions about the Middle Ages. We all more or less share the popular image of a dark, superstitious Middle Ages, backward compared with the splendor of antiquity. And part of that image is the idea that everyone then believed the Earth was flat. Everyone: not just people who did not concern themselves with such matters, but scholars too. We picture those scholars as terribly ignorant and superstitious, convinced that the Earth was flat, or at least afraid to say or teach anything else for fear of punishment by the Church.

This idea is so widespread that I probably do not need to prove how widespread it is. Most of us share it, or at least believed it until some point in our lives: in the Middle Ages, people imagined the Earth to be flat. Still, I will cite an example that seems conclusive, a work that certainly reflects popular culture: Walt Disney's *The Sword in the Stone*, a film we all remember from childhood. At one point in the cartoon, as you may recall, Merlin is teaching Wart. Merlin travels into the future and comes back, if I remember correctly, in sunglasses and Bermuda shorts. The exchange goes: “If the boy goes about saying the world is round, they'll take him for a lunatic. / The world is round? / Yes, yes, that's right. And it also goes around. / You mean it'll be round someday? / No, no, no. It's round now. Man will discover this in centuries to come.” So clearly those backward medieval people did not know the Earth was round.

Christopher Columbus is central to this picture. Without Columbus, the image of ignorant scholars who believed in a flat Earth would not be so fixed in our minds. We all know that Columbus had enormous trouble getting his project approved, securing money, and sailing with the three caravels. The version most of us know says the others did not believe the Earth was round, and therefore did not believe that by sailing from Palos he could reach the other side of the world. We picture that epoch-making scene—later we will see when the story arose—of Columbus before the scholars of Salamanca, those hopelessly ignorant men trying to obstruct him because they did not believe in a round Earth.

The theme is everywhere today. There are countless cartoons, gags, and jokes about Columbus and the flat Earth. Look on the internet and you will find them: in one cartoon the caravels reach the edge of the world—the Earth really is flat—start falling off, and all the sailors shout to Columbus, “So it was round after all!” I also remember a marvelous comic about Christopher Columbus by our own Altan, published a few decades ago. It is memorable, I must say. At one point Columbus has been traveling across the ocean for ages, no land is in sight, and he stands on deck drumming his fingers nervously and muttering, “It's flat, damn it, it's flat.”

As those of you who heard the previous episode or the one before that know, my job here is to spoil the fun. After presenting this wonderful picture of a mad world that believes the Earth is flat, I have to tell you that none of it is true. This case is so easy that, if it were all I had to discuss, we would finish in ten minutes. People in the Middle Ages knew perfectly well that the Earth was round.

How can I show you? If we could project images here, I could do it immediately. But this is an experiment you can try when you get home—tomorrow morning, perhaps. Find in a book or on the internet any image at all of a medieval emperor: the famous bronze statuette of Charlemagne on horseback, a miniature of Frederick Barbarossa, for example, or a relief of a Byzantine emperor. Every one of them is always holding, usually in the left hand, the symbol of his power over the world: a globe, sometimes topped by a cross to show that the world is to be ruled by the Christian religion. Every medieval emperor is represented holding a globe to signify his power over the world—which, as everyone knows, is a globe. But we can't show pictures here, and some of you may be thinking, “Well, I don't remember those images.” So let's look at some other evidence too.

## 2. Bede, John of Holywood, and the medieval spherical Earth

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

Medieval scholars concerned themselves with what the world and the universe were like. They read treatises and wrote treatises on the shape of the Earth—of course they did. Now, I hope this part isn't too dull, but since I have to demonstrate what I have just told you, namely that they knew perfectly well the Earth was round, let us cite a few sources.

In seventh-century England there is a monk named Bede. Bear in mind that the seventh century falls right in the middle of the phase of the Middle Ages that the English still call the Dark Ages. It is a time when the cultural level really is low: little is written, no great achievements have come down to us, and overall it is the moment of the Middle Ages' deepest cultural decline. Yet in that remote place called England, Bede writes a series of works. Someone might say, “Yes, Bede wrote them, but who knew about them?” No: Bede's works are copied and circulated, many manuscripts of them survive, and they are found in libraries everywhere. Translating from Latin, Bede says that we call the Earth a globe not because it has no irregularities—mountains and plains—but because, if we consider it as a whole, the circumference of the Earth forms a perfect globe.

Bede concerns himself with the Earth's shape on another occasion too. He writes a treatise on calculating the calendar—on times, days, and years. An aside: why do they concern themselves with this stuff? Because the medieval Church is intensely interested in calculating the calendar. They have to work out when Easter falls; the whole religious year, so to speak, depends on calculations. They have to know these things. Bede writes a handbook on calculating Easter, and at one point he says, in effect: you will have noticed that the days are longer in summer and shorter in winter. Why? The reason the days are not all the same length, Bede says, is the Earth's roundness, *rotunditas*. It is not for nothing that the Bible, and our ordinary language too, speak of the earthly orb. Indeed—and this is still Bede I am quoting, in the seventh century, right in the middle of the Dark Ages—it is an orb placed at the center of the universe. Well, we must forgive them here; they had not understood everything. For them, as for Ptolemy in antiquity, Earth stood at the center of the universe. But Bede says it is an orb.

When Bede says the Earth is round, he realizes there is a problem. Merely saying it is round is not enough, because “round” is an ambiguous word. Since I mentioned Altan earlier, allow me one more small detour into pop culture and comic strips. There is—or there was in my day—a comic strip set in the Middle Ages called *The Wizard of Id*. It has a medieval kingdom ruled by a nasty little king. In one strip the kingdom's scholars are debating the shape of the Earth: “It's round.” “No it isn't, it's flat.” They put the question to the king. The king goes away to think, then returns holding something we cannot see and announces, “I have decided: the Earth is round, and it is also flat.” Everyone asks how that can possibly be. The king replies, “I call it the pizza theory,” and reveals a pizza, which of course is both round and flat.

I made that detour to explain why Bede, saying in the seventh century that the Earth is round, realizes that this is not enough. Why not? Well, they did not know pizza in seventh-century England. Bede explains: “It is not round like a shield, but like a ball, equally round in every direction.” After Bede, treatises on the universe, astronomy, and the shape of the Earth continue to appear throughout the Middle Ages. Their authors clearly continue to have this little problem with language: merely saying the Earth is round may not be enough. So they make a real effort to explain it even to people who may not be very educated, using images rather than theorems. Let me quote a few passages from various medieval treatises on the Earth's shape: the Earth is round like a ball—that Bede had said too; if there were no obstacles, we could walk all the way around the world like a fly on an apple. Then some writers say, in effect, “Look, let's settle this problem of language. ‘Round’ will not do, but there is a word that cannot cause any doubt: sphere.”

The most important medieval treatise on the shape of the Earth is written in the thirteenth century by an English scholar later known as John of Holywood—Sacrobosco in Latin—and this treatise is called *De sphaera*, *On the Sphere*. Bear this in mind, just as with Bede, because someone might say, “Yes, he wrote it, but who knew about it?” Sacrobosco's treatise is enormously widespread: it is the standard textbook in medieval universities. As soon as printing is invented, they print Sacrobosco's *De sphaera*. It is one of the very first printed books, appearing in 1472. [N-001] Twenty-five printed editions appear within thirty years. In other words, when Columbus debates the doctors of Salamanca—but did Columbus really debate the doctors of Salamanca? We will see how true that is too—in any case, when Columbus goes looking for funding, every library in Europe already has a printed copy of Sacrobosco's treatise on the Earth, entitled *The Sphere*.

## 3. Ancient observations inherited by medieval scholars

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00355 -->

At this point, let's take a step back. I have been told that the first evening's talk about the year 1000 had too many flashbacks and became difficult to follow. All right, forgive me—but let us take a step back anyway. Why did at least the medieval scholars know perfectly well that the Earth was a sphere? Because the ancients already knew, and it is not true that ancient knowledge was lost. Many things were lost, yes, and many details, yes, but the substance could not disappear. The ancient Greeks knew perfectly well that the Earth was round. And how did they know? Let us think about that for a moment, because medieval authors are their heirs. They know the Earth is round because they follow the same lines of reasoning that Aristotle or Ptolemy had followed many centuries earlier.

And these are lines of reasoning, let me add in parentheses, that we too can follow, should the doubt ever occur to us. Today it is easy. Someone says, “I've seen the photographs. The astronauts took photographs from the Moon. The Earth is round.” Convenient. But how did people manage before that? The astonishing fact is that even then it was very easy to understand that the Earth is a sphere: one merely had to stop and think. Aristotle says—and this, in truth, is not an argument so much as an empirical observation—that sailors who travel south tell us that, as you go farther south, the constellations in the sky shift and rise, new stars appear that could not be seen before, and once you have traveled very far the constellations are entirely different. Aristotle says this can be explained only if the Earth is a sphere, because if the Earth were flat, the constellations would remain the same as you moved across it.

Perhaps you are not entirely convinced. After all, who personally knows a traveler who has gone that far south? Aristotle therefore gives another observation that really is conclusive, because anyone can make this very simple experiment from home. He writes: “In eclipses the outline is always curved.” At certain times the Earth's shadow falls across the Moon. The shadow cast by the Earth on the Moon is always circular. And there is only one solid that always casts a circular shadow, whatever its position: the Moon rises, climbs through the sky, and then sets, yet the Earth's shadow is always circular. Aristotle continues: “And, since it is the interposition of the earth that makes the eclipse, the form of this line is caused by the form of the earth's surface, which is therefore spherical.” There is no getting around it.

After Aristotle comes Ptolemy, antiquity's greatest geographer. The greatest geographer, even though he believes that Earth stands at the center of the universe, as Christians too will believe for many centuries. And even though—as we will see when we return to Christopher Columbus and discuss how large the Earth is and what its circumference measures—Ptolemy gets that part muddled. But I do not want to get ahead of myself. One thing Ptolemy states clearly: the Earth is round. Do you want proof? You are on a ship approaching the mainland, and there are mountains on the coast. First you see the mountain peaks. Then, as you draw closer, the mountains grow: you begin to see their bases, and only when you are very close do you see the beach as well. Ptolemy says this can happen only if we accept that, contrary to what we believe, the surface of the sea is slightly curved; otherwise there is no explaining it.

Good. But you will rightly say: all right, the ancients knew, and the medieval people knew too—we saw that earlier—but are we certain there is a direct passage from one to the other? Did no one ever question any of this? What does the Bible say?

## 4. Biblical imagery, early Christians, and Augustine

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00356–U-00542 -->

But what does the Bible say? Let us stop here for a moment, because it is perfectly true that the early Christians—not all the scholars of a Middle Ages that lasted a thousand years, but the early Christians—have some difficulty with this business of a spherical Earth. If you read the Bible, it does not say that the Earth is round; it says nothing clear on the matter. Yet there are passages which, with the best will in the world, do not fit very comfortably with the idea that the Earth is a sphere.

We should remember that the Bible is a collection of works written over several centuries in the Jewish world. It contains the whole historical and wisdom tradition of a small people who do not have a scientific cast of mind. The Jews are not the Greeks. Their way of thinking is allegorical, imaginative, and religious, and in their books—that is, in the Bible—they are not concerned with scientific truths. They speak a different kind of language.

Let me give you a few examples, so that we can see why the early Christians, who have learned at school that the Earth is round, find it a little difficult to reconcile that lesson with what they read in the Bible. Every so often, God forgive them, biblical authors speak of the four corners of the Earth. If you take that literally, you have a problem. Revelation 7:1, for example, describes four angels standing at the four corners of the Earth. Some early Christians say that this means the Earth has four corners. So what are the Greeks doing telling us it is a sphere? Where are the four corners on a sphere?

Above all, though, the image found most often in the Bible is that of heaven as a tent pitched over the Earth. Remember that the Jews are a people of shepherds, a seminomadic people; the tent is an extremely familiar image to them. Psalm 104 says that God stretches the heavens like a tent. The prophet Isaiah asks whether people have understood how the world was founded, then describes God sitting upon the vault covering the Earth; from up there, human beings look like ants. He stretches out heaven like a veil and spreads it like a tent to live in. The early Christians read these texts and reason as follows: if the Earth has four corners and heaven is like a tent, where do you pitch a tent? On a flat surface, with its four pegs at the four corners. And so some early Christians conclude that the Earth must be flat, with God placing heaven above it like a tent.

There is even an author from the first centuries of Christianity called Cosmas—Cosmas Indicopleustes—who writes about this in Greek and goes so far as to imagine it visually. He includes drawings, preserved in manuscripts of his works, of this heaven stretched like a tent above an Earth that is, by that point, flat.

I am talking about the early Christians, and it is no accident that I keep insisting on that phrase. Think about what is going on in their minds. Christians living through persecution are extremely hostile to the civilization around them. They are convinced that they possess a new truth and that all the old truths are rubbish to be thrown away. They are convinced that the Greek philosophers and scientists will burn in hell, and that everything those people believed is as false as their false gods. In this period, when Christianity is very aggressive and profoundly contemptuous of the whole ancient civilization, you can indeed find the occasional Christian author saying that the Earth is flat.

A brief aside, which you will forgive from the medievalist speaking to you: those very few flat-Earth authors are not medieval authors. They are ancient authors. We are still in antiquity—late antiquity, when Christianity is emerging. There are a few writers then who say they have read in the Bible that the Earth is flat. But that does not last at all. Within the Christian world itself, people who read the Bible literally and start arguing about the shape of the Earth eventually become embarrassing.

The Christian attitude of standing outside the world, hostile both to it and to the civilization in which Christians live, does not last very long either. Constantine is all it takes. Once Constantine arrives, the persecutions end, the empire begins to protect Christians, and Roman and Greek society gradually becomes Christian. That puts an end to this business of opposing classical civilization. The age of confrontation is over.

The most important Fathers of the Church then begin to worry. They worry because Christians who go around insisting that the Bible says the Earth is flat make themselves look ridiculous, and the Fathers have no desire to give the impression that Christians are a pack of ignorant illiterates. That may sound like strong language, but it is what they themselves say.

Let me quote one of them. I have already mentioned him in the past few days because he is one of the thinkers who most profoundly influences medieval thought: Saint Augustine, at the end of the fourth and beginning of the fifth century. Augustine faces precisely this problem. Some Christian starts arguing with scientists that the Earth cannot be round because the Bible says it has four corners. Augustine replies: I have read the Bible, and there is nothing precise there about the shape of the Earth. Clearly the Lord does not care about the Earth's shape. He created it and we live on it, but it is not the issue; it is not a truth of faith. It is ridiculous for Christians to start arguing about such matters. From the point of view of salvation, Augustine says, the shape of the Earth matters to no one. It is one of the many subjects we must leave to the specialists—and on which we must believe the specialists—because there is no dogma at stake.

Augustine says that pagans often possess certain knowledge of the Earth, the heavens, and the movement of the stars because that knowledge comes from reason and experience. If you will allow me, let us pause over that pair, because it remains central to medieval thought. How do you know things? There are two ways. One is experience: I have tried it; I have touched it with my own hands. The other is regarded as better still, because it is nobler: you do not need to perform the experiment, because you can reach the answer by thinking. That other route is what your reason tells you.

Augustine therefore warns: “It is disgraceful and dangerous for a nonbeliever to hear a Christian, supposedly explaining Scripture, talk nonsense on these subjects.” Augustine continues: we should do everything we can to avoid such an embarrassing situation—to prevent nonbelievers from seeing nothing but ignorance in Christians and laughing at us.

Augustine writes this in a book called *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, a work on the literal interpretation of Genesis in which he says that Genesis must not, in fact, be interpreted literally. It is made of images and metaphors; reading it literally makes no sense. In the very same work, when Augustine speaks about the Earth, he says, well, the Earth is a globe. Obviously it is a globe, with one face illuminated by the Sun and the other in darkness. And Augustine takes care to say that when the Bible describes the tent, it is a metaphor. You must not take it literally. If a pagan can establish something through reason or experience, and a Christian denies it while waving the Bible, the pagan will laugh not merely at that Christian but at Scripture. If reason and sensory experience establish a fact, Scripture cannot truly contradict it, so the interpreter must look for another meaning.

But accepting a spherical Earth raises a different question: if we live on this side of the globe, is there anyone on the other side? The ancients call such people the antipodes—those whose feet are opposite ours. Augustine makes the distinction explicit: “Even if it is supposed or scientifically demonstrated that the world is round and spherical, it does not follow that the other side is inhabited.” Scripture teaches that humanity descends from Adam, and Augustine cannot see how anyone could cross the enormous ocean. Thus he rejects inhabited antipodes, not the spherical Earth itself. This is the key distinction for what follows.

## 5. Explaining a spherical Earth to ordinary people

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00543–U-00693 -->

So, then—did everyone know that the Earth was round? Not so fast, because there is always a problem. There are people who concern themselves with these questions and reason about them—the scholars—and then there are the others, the ignorant. They may never once in their lives have worried about such problems, but the first time they hear that the Earth is round, they say, “How can that be? It does not look round to me. It looks flat.” Ignorant people exist, and in the Middle Ages this comes up repeatedly. We scholars know that the Earth is round, medieval writers say, but every now and then we must be patient with the ignorant and explain it to them.

In the twelfth century, three hundred years before Christopher Columbus, a philosopher and theologian called William of Conches writes a philosophical treatise with a chapter entitled “The Shape of the Earth.” He asks himself: we scholars know the Earth is round, but how are we to explain it to the ignorant? So he imagines a dialogue between a philosopher and a layman—between a learned theologian and a poor fellow who, not being a churchman, has never read a book. The layman cast as the fool who needs the truth explained to him is the Duke of Normandy, because evidently it is not only the poor: even dukes have other things to do in life than worry about the shape of the Earth. The theologian talks with the duke, who says he has doubts: is the Earth really round, as the scholars claim? He cannot see its roundness.

The theologian replies, rather brutally, that some people are like animals because they trust their sensations rather than reason. Let us say in passing that medieval scholars are in love with reason. They talk about reason constantly. They have this inexplicable confidence in human beings: they are convinced that if a person sits down and thinks, he can get to the bottom of anything—that reasoning is enough.

And so the philosopher says that certain people, being like animals and unable to use reason, declare the Earth flat because when they walk about they do not notice its roundness. All right, you do not notice it while walking around—but think, says this twelfth-century philosopher, and he offers a proof. These arguments and demonstrations are a little complicated, but I am going to inflict one more on you. If the Earth were flat, imagine a city at the extreme eastern edge. The moment the Sun rose, it would already be noon because the Sun would immediately be almost directly overhead. In that city, the interval from dawn to midday would last only an instant. Then you would have ages to wait before sunset, because the whole course of the Sun would still lie ahead. Yet wherever we are, the philosopher says, the time from sunrise to noon equals the time from noon to sunset. Therefore the Earth is not flat.

The treatise I have mentioned might make us imagine a sharp divide: scholars know the Earth is round, while everyone else is convinced it is flat. That is not quite right. Everyone else, quite reasonably—like many of us before this evening—has simply never worried about the question and does not think about it much. But in the Middle Ages there is a clear effort to do what they called “putting things into the vernacular”: making the most important ideas comprehensible even to the ignorant.

One famous medieval book is a collection of imaginary travels. It is not like Marco Polo's book: Marco Polo may embellish—indeed, embellishes a great deal—but he really does go to China. The book I am talking about is called *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville*, and the travels are entirely invented. Sir John Mandeville never existed. Yet the book describes an endless series of journeys around the world and explains what the world is like. It is, for a change, enormously successful. During the fourteenth century it is translated into every major language, and—at the risk of boring you—I shall even give you the number of printed editions. It is printed as soon as printing is invented, and by 1500 there are thirty-five editions.

An aside: do you see how useful that is? If you want to know which books were the most popular and widely read, you count the printed editions. That gives you hard proof that everybody was reading a book.

In *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville*, sailors voyaging across the seas and heading south eventually reach places where the Pole Star is no longer visible. We have heard this argument already; Aristotle uses it. But here it reappears in a medieval work, and above all in a book written for the general public—a bestseller, if you like. Indeed, I can quote it directly from a medieval vernacular version. In the English Cotton version: “For which cause men may well perceive, that the land and the sea be of round shape and form; for the part of the firmament sheweth in one country that sheweth not in another country. And men may well prove by experience and subtle compassment of wit.”

Notice that carefully: experience and “subtle compassment of wit.” Once again we have Saint Augustine's pair from a thousand years earlier. There are two ways of knowing things: experience—perform the experiment—and subtle investigation—think about it and work it out. And then Mandeville completes the sentence: “that if a man found passages by ships that would go to search the world, men might go by ship all about the world and above and beneath.” This is roughly 150 years before Christopher Columbus.

Naturally, Mandeville—especially in versions translated into the vernacular—uses somewhat rough, plain language that everyone can understand. He sees the Earth as round and yet makes a mistake that I do not know whether we make too—unconsciously, I suspect we do. Mandeville says there is an above and a below. If you turn and travel south, you go around the roundness of land and sea and descend beneath this country of ours. We are up above, here in our own country, and if we go south, we go underneath. But nothing happens when you go underneath. Everything still works perfectly well.

Mandeville says that it certainly seems strange, because ignorant people tell you, “But if you go underneath, you will fall.” He calls them “coarse people”—notice the phrase. Coarse people are people who are not subtle, who do not know how to reason. To coarse people it seems impossible to go beneath the Earth without falling away into the lower sky. But that cannot be, Mandeville says, because from their point of view we would otherwise fall from the Earth where we stand above them. In other words, it is not that we are above and they are below. From their point of view, we are the ones underneath. We do not fall upward, do we? And neither do they. It is a little complicated to explain, but the idea is clear enough. At this point—

## 6. Antipodes, the torrid zone, and the ocean

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00694–U-00811 -->

—we have entered a discussion that is no longer about a flat Earth, because, as you can see, there is not the slightest possible dispute over the Earth's roundness. But it is related all the same, and even if it were not, I feel like telling you about it, so I am going to do so anyway. Who is on the other side of the world? Is there anyone there, at the antipodes?

We speak of “the antipodes” as though it were a place, but the term is invented in antiquity to describe not a place but people. The antipodes are those who stand on the far side of our feet. Our feet are planted firmly on the ground; go down, down, down, down, emerge on the other side, and whom do you find? The antipodes, naturally standing with their feet in the air. But do the antipodes exist?

In antiquity and the Middle Ages, no one from Europe has been to the other side of the world, so experience is of no use. The only way to discover whether anyone could live there is to try reasoning it out. For centuries, in antiquity and the Middle Ages, scholars have tremendous fun trying to prove that people could live on the other side—or, on the contrary, that it is impossible and no one can be there. The arguments against their existence begin already in antiquity.

We have already heard one argument: the argument of coarse people, of people who cannot reason. There can be no one on the other side because they would fall off. Ignorant people are already making that argument in antiquity. Pliny the Elder discusses it in a passage of his *Natural History* in the first century. The Earth is round, Pliny says; everybody knows that. But when it comes to whether the antipodes exist—whether there are people on the other side—he says: “There is a great debate between learning and the common herd.” Notice his terms. Pliny is clearly a man of letters himself and is keen not to belong to the crowd. In his account, “people stand opposite one another, foot to foot,” while ordinary people ask “why those on the other side do not fall off—as if they would not wonder why we do not fall off.” In other words, it is as ridiculous to imagine them falling off as it would be to imagine us falling upward. Nobody falls off. No one has yet calculated the law of gravity, but people understand perfectly well that we all remain attached to Earth.

With the arrival of Christianity, however, the debate over human beings on the far side of the Earth becomes more intense, because there are new arguments to consider, and the idea that people might live there begins to seem more difficult. There are two reasons—more than one, at any rate.

Forgive me, I have raced ahead slightly: one of the arguments is already used by the ancients, so I shall deal with it quickly. The ancients ask what the world is like. Well, at the poles it is bitterly cold; then there is a temperate zone where, luckily for us, we live; and in the middle it is unbearably hot. At the equator there is torrid heat. Since no one has ever been there, the ancients eventually become convinced that the equator is so hot that no one can cross it. So far, so good.

Then the Christians arrive and say that all human beings descend from Adam. Adam is created in a particular place, in Palestine, and from there the human race spreads out. But how could people reach the other side of the world if the torrid zone lies in between, with scorching heat through which no one can pass? You see, we must always try to imagine these people, who have no way of knowing by experience and therefore have to reason from the evidence available to them. All human beings descend from Adam; crossing the equator is impossible because you would burn there; therefore no one has gone to the other side, and so no one can be living there.

Then there is a second reason. The world is covered partly by dry land and partly by water. I hope you are still following me. I know this is complicated, but this year I wagered that I could tell you about complicated things, and by now it is too late to change the subject, so you will have to forgive me.

Ancient and medieval philosophers reason as follows. The world consists of dry land and water—but in what proportion? For a very long time, they answer: little land and a great deal of water. Aristotle—and you know the influence Aristotle has throughout the Middle Ages—gives a ratio of one to ten: one tenth dry land and all the rest water. The emerged lands are small. Cicero, who is no superstitious medieval, says at one point that the lands are like tiny spots on the surface of the Earth, while all the rest is water. But then, even if we grant that there may be another tiny spot on the far side, how could anyone cross that entire ocean? The ancients already regard it as impossible.

Then the Christians arrive, and Christians read the Bible. The Bible says—as you all remember—that God first creates the waters and the Earth is entirely covered by them. Then God makes the waters withdraw so that dry land can emerge. But, ladies and gentlemen, if he makes the waters withdraw, it is still obvious that the greater part of the world is covered by water and the dry land is very small.

For all these reasons, ancient philosophers and medieval theologians say: we do not know for certain, but in my opinion there is nothing on the other side of the world. And then Christopher Columbus arrives.

## 7. Columbus, expert review, and erroneous distances

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00812–U-00929 -->

And with Columbus we come back to where we started, and begin to draw toward a conclusion as well. So what is Columbus's real problem? We have seen that the Earth is round, and everybody knows it. There is no need to explain to anyone that the Earth is round. What does Columbus come and say to Queen Isabella? What idea has he got into his head? Let us try to remember the context.

This is the late fifteenth century, an age when an enormous amount of money is made from the spice trade—truly enormous amounts. The spice trade can fund the budget of an entire state, and the spices come from the East. For centuries, Arab merchants have gone east to obtain them, then carried them on camelback as far as Egypt. There they are loaded onto ships, which cross a Mediterranean full of pirates and finally reach Venice or Genoa with their spices. The cost is terrifying.

Not long before Columbus, the Portuguese discover that instead of getting bogged down in the Mediterranean and waiting for the Red Sea caravan, they can sail around Africa. You sail around Africa, round the Cape of Good Hope, and reach India in less time. That means a much larger profit on every cargo of spices, and the Portuguese grow rich.

Columbus goes to the queen of Spain and tells her, “I have an idea that will make us a great deal of money, because I have worked out a much shorter route to the East.” Since the Earth is round, Columbus says, if you leave Spain and sail west, you go all the way around and arrive in the East—and that way we beat the Portuguese.

So the familiar story about the commission and the scholars of Salamanca is simply untrue, as we shall see. What we do know is that Columbus presents the queen with this project and all his calculations: how long it will take and how far the journey is. Since it involves spending a great deal of money, the queen does what would probably be done even today: she appoints a commission, a panel of specialists charged with assessing Columbus's proposal. They are not the scholars of the University of Salamanca. The experts meet several times and, after a few months, give an initial negative opinion. But Columbus has powerful recommendations and many influential friends, so the commission is told to investigate further: think about it a little longer. They think about it for several years. Sometimes they meet in Salamanca, sometimes in Córdoba, wherever the itinerant court happens to be.

And what does the commission say? It says that Columbus's project is extremely bold and that there would be difficulties. By now I hardly need tell you that nobody on the commission stands up and says the Earth is flat. Why would they? They do say: yes, he wants to go that way, but what about the torrid zone? Then again, perhaps it is not really so torrid after all; and even if it is, perhaps they can endure it. Never mind that. The real problem, the commission says, is that Columbus has supplied certain measurements. According to him the voyage will take only so long and can be made. But we have read the Greek scientists; we have read Eratosthenes, who calculated the circumference of the Earth, and Columbus's figures simply do not add up. In our view, he will not make it. The voyage is much longer than Columbus claims. We therefore advise against spending money on this scheme.

The tragedy, with hindsight, is that they are right. Columbus has got his calculations completely wrong. It is still unclear how much of that is bad faith and how much comes from believing in the project so fiercely, but scholars have reconstructed the way he calculates the distance from Spain to the Far East by sailing west.

Columbus tries everything. He reads Marco Polo and attempts to calculate how large China is according to Marco Polo—and he gives it every possible advantage. The larger China is, the farther east it extends and the sooner a westward voyage will reach it. Columbus considers all the existing calculations of the Earth's circumference, chooses the smallest, and treats it as correct. Then he cheats on the units: some measurements are expressed in nautical miles, and he pretends they are land miles, which are shorter.

In the end Columbus presents a calculation in which the distance to the Far East is measured from the Canaries—because, rather than start from Spain, he may as well start from the Canaries, which are already a little farther west and save him some distance. From the Canaries to the Far East, Columbus says, it is 4,500 kilometers. He gives the figure in miles, of course, but that is roughly what it amounts to: 4,500 kilometers. Do you know the real distance from the Canaries to the Far East? Brace yourselves: 22,000 kilometers.

In other words, Columbus sets out, and under normal circumstances they should all die of thirst after a month in the middle of the ocean. He is saved by running into America, which nobody knows is there. Because it lies halfway along the route, Columbus manages to reach land. So in the end he is the one who is right, obviously. And indeed, the chief lesson sixteenth-century scholars learn from the discovery of America is this: we were wrong. We thought there was very little dry land and that the globe as a whole was covered by water. It is not true. As one sixteenth-century treatise puts it, they discover that there is not as much ocean as they had believed.

## 8. Washington Irving invents the Salamanca legend

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00930–U-00984 -->

Very well, but if that is how things happen—and I have only five minutes left now, though let us still say a few things quickly—why have we all seen paintings or cartoons of Columbus, globe in hand, facing the scholars of Salamanca? Why do we all have this image in our heads of the utterly ignorant scholars of Salamanca insisting that the Earth is flat and that Columbus therefore cannot succeed? Who invents this nonsense?

Here we know the culprit—or rather, yes, there is a specific name that I am about to give you, but more generally we can blame the Americans. Americans simply have to believe that Columbus changed the history of the world, obviously, and therefore that he is a great hero who fought ignorance and superstition. This image of Columbus as the great hero who puts an end—as I have now repeated for three evenings—to the darkness of the Middle Ages and brings the light of knowledge is especially important to Americans.

At the beginning of the nineteenth century, an American writer named Washington Irving—very famous in America, less so here—writes a book called *A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus*. Washington Irving knows that learned men at Salamanca criticized Columbus and did not want to give him money. He knows nothing else.

So he describes this marvelous scene, page after extraordinary page, with an entire coalition of ignorant men trying to halt the advance of truth. They bring in the antipodes, the Bible's tent, and the prophet Isaiah, as though the scholars of the fifteenth century were still stuck, like certain Christians a thousand years earlier, arguing about those things.

Everyone in America reads the book, and this marvelous image of Columbus and the scholars of Salamanca is stamped forever on the collective memory. It can still be found in American schoolbooks. And the United States Capitol in Washington has the bronze Columbus Doors, on which Columbus is actually immortalized in bronze as he heroically debates the scholars of Salamanca. So the image takes root forever. Columbus represents enlightened modernity against the superstition and ignorance of the past. Let us finish—but first, one last little point.

## 9. Modern flat-earthers and the duty to verify

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00985–U-01035 -->

<!-- research: [Q-015] [Q-016] [C-015] [C-016] -->

All right, everyone knew the Earth was round. But then who wrote this passage? Try to guess when this demonstration was written. Listen: “Human beings require a surface on which to live that, in its general character, shall be LEVEL; and since the Omniscient Creator must have been perfectly acquainted with the requirements of His creatures, it follows that, being an All-wise Creator, He has met them thoroughly. This is a theological proof that the Earth is not a globe.”

Had I opened by attributing that passage to a doctor of Salamanca, I suspect everyone would have accepted it. It comes instead from a nineteenth-century American, William Carpenter, who in 1885 self-publishes in Baltimore—American publishing deserves credit for that restraint—a book called *One Hundred Proofs That the Earth Is Not a Globe*. Carpenter's 1885 pamphlet belongs to a nineteenth-century flat-Earth revival that had already developed around Samuel Rowbotham in England. [N-006] The hundred proofs are extraordinary: look the book up online—the whole thing is there—and read it. The first proof is simply this: well, if you rise in a balloon and look down, the Earth plainly appears flat.

And, as a little tailpiece to that very last point, William Carpenter is not alone. At the end of the nineteenth century, a Flat Earth Society is born in America, and it still exists. Go and look it up: there is a website, the Flat Earth Society. Its several thousand members are convinced that the Earth is flat and that we are made to believe it is round because they can make us believe anything they like. They made us believe we went to the Moon; that was all staged too.

In other words, today, perhaps for the first time, there are people who genuinely believe the Earth is flat. And they believe it because they are convinced that everything we are told is a deception—that whatever we are told must be assumed false, because there is a conspiracy to mislead us.

Now, as I was thinking about this, I said to myself: that sounds almost like what I have been doing over these evenings. Or rather, I hope that is not what I have done. I have not said, “Whatever anyone tells you, beware, because it is false.” But I hope I have told you this: whatever we are told, we should not believe it merely because someone has told us. And if it is something we care about, something we really want, so to speak, to believe, then it is not enough that someone has told us or that we have read it. Within the limits of our abilities, we must try to check it. Thank you.
