# The Flat Earth: An Unbelievable Middle Ages — corrected English script

## 1. The modern myth of a flat medieval Earth

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. Tonight we will talk about another superstition—not a medieval superstition, of course, but one of our superstitions about the Middle Ages. We all more or less share the popular image of a dark, superstitious Middle Ages, backward compared with the splendor of antiquity. And part of that image is the idea that everyone then believed the Earth was flat. Everyone: not just people who did not concern themselves with such matters, but scholars too. We picture those scholars as terribly ignorant and superstitious, convinced that the Earth was flat, or at least afraid to say or teach anything else for fear of punishment by the Church.

This idea is so widespread that I probably do not need to prove it. Most of us shared it, or believed it until some point in our lives. But I will cite an example that seems conclusive, a work that certainly reflects popular culture: Walt Disney's *The Sword in the Stone*, a film we all remember from childhood. Merlin teaches Wart, travels into the future, and comes back, if I remember correctly, in sunglasses and Bermuda shorts. The exchange goes: “If the boy goes about saying the world is round, they'll take him for a lunatic. / The world is round? / Yes, yes, that's right. And it also goes around. / You mean it'll be round someday? / No, no, no. It's round now. Man will discover this in centuries to come.” So clearly those backward medieval people did not know it.

Christopher Columbus is central to this picture. Without Columbus, the image of ignorant scholars who believed in a flat Earth would not be so fixed in our minds. We all know that Columbus had enormous trouble getting his project approved, securing money, and sailing with the three caravels. The version most of us know says the others did not believe the Earth was round, and therefore did not believe that by sailing from Palos he could reach the other side of the world. We picture that epoch-making scene—later we will see when the story arose—of Columbus before the scholars of Salamanca, those hopelessly ignorant men trying to obstruct him because they did not believe in a round Earth.

The theme is everywhere today. There are countless cartoons and jokes about Columbus and the flat Earth: the caravels reach the edge of the world, start falling off, and the sailors shout to Columbus, “So it was round after all!” I also remember a marvelous comic by our own Altan. Columbus has been at sea for ages, no land is in sight, and he stands on deck drumming his fingers nervously and muttering that the cursed thing is flat.

As those of you who attended yesterday or the day before know, my job here is to spoil the fun. After presenting this wonderful picture of a mad world that believes the Earth is flat, I have to tell you that none of it is true. This case is so easy that, if it were all I had to discuss, we would finish in ten minutes. People in the Middle Ages knew perfectly well that the Earth was round.

How can I show you? If we could project images here, I could do it immediately. You can try the experiment at home: find any image of a medieval emperor—the famous bronze statuette of Charlemagne on horseback, a miniature of Frederick Barbarossa, or a relief of a Byzantine emperor. They characteristically hold, usually in the left hand, the symbol of their power over the world: a globe, sometimes topped by a cross to show that the world is under the Christian religion. Medieval emperors are represented holding a globe because their power extends over the world, which everyone knows is a globe. But since we cannot show pictures, and some of you may not remember them, let us offer other evidence.

## 2. Bede, Sacrobosco, and the medieval spherical Earth

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

Medieval scholars cared about the structure of the world and the universe. They read and wrote treatises on the Earth's shape. I hope this part is not too dull, but since I must prove that they knew the Earth was round, let us cite some sources.

In seventh-century England there is a monk named Bede. This is the heart of the period the English still call the Dark Ages, a time of genuinely low cultural production and perhaps the deepest cultural decline of the Middle Ages. Yet in that remote place Bede writes many works. One might ask who knew them, but they were copied and circulated; many manuscripts survive, and they stood in libraries everywhere. Translating from Latin, Bede says that we call the Earth a globe not because mountains and plains make it perfectly even, but because, considered as a whole, its circumference has the form of a complete globe.

Bede returns to the Earth's shape in a treatise on calendars, days, and years. The medieval Church cares intensely about calendrical calculation because Easter must be dated and the religious year depends on it. Bede explains why summer days are longer and winter days shorter: the reason, he says, is the Earth's roundness, *rotunditas*. Scripture and ordinary language call it the earthly orb. It is an orb at the center of the universe. We must forgive that part: like Ptolemy, they do not understand everything. But Bede explicitly calls it an orb.

He also realizes that “round” is ambiguous. Since I mentioned Altan, allow me another comic-book detour. *The Wizard of Id* is set in a medieval kingdom ruled by a nasty little king. In one strip the scholars argue whether the Earth is round or flat. The king goes away to think, returns holding something out of sight, and announces that the Earth is both round and flat. Asked how that can be, he reveals a pizza and calls it the pizza theory.

Bede does not know pizza, but he sees the linguistic problem. He explains: “It is not round like a shield, but like a ball, equally round in every direction.” Later medieval treatises keep explaining the same point in images understandable to less educated readers: the Earth is round like a ball; without obstacles, one could walk around it like a fly around an apple. Eventually writers choose a word that removes all doubt: sphere.

The most important medieval textbook on the subject is written in the thirteenth century by the English scholar John of Sacrobosco and is called *De sphaera*, *On the Sphere*. Again, this is not an obscure private opinion. Sacrobosco's work is the standard astronomy text in medieval universities. Sacrobosco's *De sphaera* was first printed in 1472. [N-001] Twenty-five printed editions appear within thirty years. Thus when Columbus seeks funding—and we will ask how much he really debates the doctors of Salamanca—printed copies of a treatise called *The Sphere* are already in libraries across Europe.

## 3. Ancient observations inherited by medieval scholars

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00355 -->

Now let us step backward. I have been told that the first evening's talk about the year 1000 had too many flashbacks and became difficult to follow; forgive me, but we need another one. Why did medieval scholars know the Earth was a sphere? Because the ancients already knew, and the substance of ancient knowledge was not lost. Many details disappeared, but not everything important. Greek thinkers knew perfectly well that the Earth was round, and medieval authors inherited their reasoning.

We can repeat those arguments ourselves if we ever doubt the photographs taken from space. Aristotle reports an empirical observation from sailors: as one travels south, the constellations shift, new stars appear, and after a great distance the sky looks different. That makes sense only on a spherical Earth; on a flat surface, the same constellations would remain visible.

He gives a still more decisive observation available from home. Aristotle writes: “In eclipses the outline is always curved; and, since it is the interposition of the earth that makes the eclipse, the form of this line is caused by the form of the earth's surface, which is therefore spherical.” Ptolemy, antiquity's greatest geographer, is also explicit. Although he places Earth at the center of the universe and, as we will see with Columbus, mishandles its circumference, he observes what happens as a ship approaches mountainous land: first the peaks appear, then more of the mountains, and finally the shore. This occurs only if the sea's surface is slightly curved.

So antiquity knows and the Middle Ages knows. But is the transmission really continuous? Does no one ever challenge it? We must ask what the Bible says.

## 4. Biblical imagery, early Christians, and Augustine

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00356–U-00542 -->

The Bible says nothing clear about a spherical Earth, and some passages do not fit it easily if read literally. The Bible is a collection written over centuries within the Jewish world, the historical and wisdom tradition of a small people whose mentality is religious, imaginative, and allegorical rather than Greek and scientific. Its writers are not trying to state scientific truths.

This creates trouble for some early Christians who learned at school that Earth was round. Revelation 7:1 speaks of four angels at the four corners of the Earth. Taken literally, a sphere has no four corners. More often, heaven is pictured as a tent stretched above the Earth. The Hebrews are pastoral and seminomadic, so the image is familiar. Psalm 104 says God stretches the heavens like a tent; Isaiah describes God sitting above the vault of Earth, from where people look like ants, and spreading heaven like a veil or a tent. Some Christians reason that a tent is pitched on a flat surface with four pegs.

Cosmas Indicopleustes even develops and illustrates a flat Earth beneath a tent-like sky. But I keep saying “early Christians.” These few authors belong to late antiquity, not the thousand-year Middle Ages. Christians living under persecution can be fiercely hostile to the civilization around them, regarding pagan philosophy and science as refuse along with pagan gods. In that setting one can find Christian flat-Earth authors. It does not last. Once Constantine ends persecution and the Greco-Roman world becomes Christian, Christians no longer want to discard its whole intellectual inheritance. Literal cosmographers become embarrassing.

Saint Augustine is the crucial example. In *The Literal Meaning of Genesis* he warns: “It is disgraceful and dangerous for a nonbeliever to hear a Christian, supposedly explaining Scripture, talk nonsense on these subjects.” If a pagan can establish something through calculation or experience, and a Christian denies it while waving the Bible, the pagan will laugh not merely at that Christian but at Scripture. The Bible speaks in a way ordinary people can understand; it is not a scientific manual. If reason and sensory experience establish a fact, Scripture cannot truly contradict it, so the interpreter must look for another meaning.

Augustine makes the distinction explicit: “Even if it is supposed or scientifically demonstrated that the world is round and spherical, it does not follow that the other side is inhabited.” Scripture teaches that humanity descends from Adam, and Augustine cannot see how anyone could cross the enormous ocean. Thus he rejects inhabited antipodes, not the spherical Earth itself. This is the key distinction for what follows.

## 5. Explaining a spherical Earth to ordinary people

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00543–U-00693 -->

Perhaps scholars know all this, but what about ordinary medieval people? Most people never consider the problem, just as most people today do not independently investigate it. Yet medieval writers make a clear effort to “popularize” important knowledge.

One famous example is *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville*, a book of imaginary travel. Unlike Marco Polo, who really went to China even if he embellishes heavily, Sir John Mandeville never existed. Still, the work describes endless journeys and the shape of the world. It is a medieval bestseller, translated into all the major languages during the fourteenth century and printed repeatedly as soon as printing appears—thirty-five editions before 1500. Counting early editions gives us rough evidence of what people read.

Mandeville says sailors traveling south eventually lose sight of the Pole Star. This is Aristotle's old argument, now presented in a popular vernacular book. In the English Cotton version: “For which cause men may well perceive, that the land and the sea be of round shape and form; for the part of the firmament sheweth in one country that sheweth not in another country. And men may well prove by experience and subtle compassment of wit, that if a man found passages by ships that would go to search the world, men might go by ship all about the world and above and beneath.” As with Augustine a thousand years earlier, there are two routes to knowledge: experiment and reasoning. This is roughly 150 years before Columbus.

The vernacular versions use deliberately plain language. Mandeville imagines us as “above” and people across the globe as “below.” Traveling south means going down around the roundness of land and sea. But nothing happens when you go underneath. Ignorant people think you would fall away into the lower sky. Mandeville answers that this cannot happen, because otherwise we would fall from the Earth from their point of view. We are not truly above while they are below: to them, we are underneath. The explanation is awkward, but the concept is clear.

## 6. Antipodes, the torrid zone, and the ocean

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00694–U-00811 -->

We have left the flat-Earth question, since the Earth's roundness is not seriously disputed, but the related debate is too interesting to omit. Who, if anyone, lives on the other side? “Antipodes” originally means people, those whose feet are opposite ours. In antiquity and the Middle Ages no one from Europe had traveled there, so experience could not answer. Scholars reasoned for centuries both for and against their existence.

The crudest objection is that they would fall off. Pliny the Elder discusses this in the first century. The Earth's roundness, he says, is known; antipodes produce a conflict between learned people and the common crowd. The crowd asks why people on the other side do not fall into the sky. Pliny answers: “There is a great debate between learning and the common herd: people stand opposite one another, foot to foot, while ordinary people ask why those on the other side do not fall off—as if they would not wonder why we do not fall off.” No one has calculated gravity yet, but people understand that everyone remains attached to Earth.

Christianity intensifies the question. Ancient geography already divides the world into freezing polar zones, a habitable temperate zone, and a lethally hot equatorial zone. If the torrid zone cannot be crossed, Christians add, how can descendants of Adam—created in a definite region—reach the other side? Their reasoning uses the evidence available to them: common descent plus an impassable equator appears to exclude a second population.

Water supplies another obstacle. Ancient and medieval philosophers ask how much of the globe is land. For a long time they answer: little land and much water. Aristotle gives a ratio of one to ten; Cicero calls the lands tiny spots on Earth's surface. Even if another spot lies across the globe, how could anyone cross the immense ocean? Christians also read Genesis as saying that water first covers everything before God draws it back to reveal land. Thus ancient philosophers and medieval theologians do not deny the sphere; they say that, as far as they can reason, the far side is probably unreachable and uninhabited. Then Columbus arrives.

## 7. Columbus, expert review, and erroneous distances

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00812–U-00929 -->

What is Columbus's real problem? Everyone knows the Earth is round. In the late fifteenth century the spice trade produces immense profits, even state-scale revenues. Arab merchants bring spices west by caravan to Egypt; ships then cross the pirate-filled Mediterranean to Venice or Genoa. The route is ruinously expensive. The Portuguese discover that ships can go around Africa, round the Cape of Good Hope, and reach India faster, making still greater profits.

Columbus offers Isabella a shorter route: because Earth is round, sail west from Spain and arrive in the East, beating the Portuguese. The story of Salamanca is false in its familiar form. Columbus presents calculations of time and distance, and the queen, before spending heavily, appoints experts to assess them. This is not simply the faculty of Salamanca. The commission meets several times, sometimes at Salamanca and sometimes with the moving court at Córdoba. It initially reports against the plan, then, because Columbus has influential patrons, reconsiders for years.

No commissioner needs to object that Earth is flat. The torrid-zone question may arise, but the decisive problem is distance. Columbus supplies measurements that do not agree with ancient calculations of the Earth's circumference. The experts conclude the journey is much longer than he claims and advise against funding it. With hindsight, they are right.

Columbus's reconstruction mixes conviction and perhaps bad faith. He reads Marco Polo and makes China as wide as possible, pushing its eastern coast farther east and therefore closer by a westward route. Among estimates of Earth's circumference he selects the smallest. He treats miles from one system as the shorter miles of another. Starting from the Canaries, already west of Spain, he calculates only about 4,500 kilometers to East Asia. The actual distance is about 22,000 kilometers. Ordinarily the crews would die of thirst in mid-ocean. They survive because America, whose existence they do not know, lies in the way.

Columbus therefore succeeds, but for the wrong numerical reasons. The great lesson sixteenth-century geographers draw from America is that they were wrong about the balance of land and water: there is not as much ocean as they had believed.

## 8. Washington Irving invents the Salamanca legend

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00930–U-00984 -->

If events unfold this way, why do we all know paintings and cartoons of Columbus, globe in hand, facing the scholars of Salamanca? Who invents the nonsense? We know the culprit—or, speaking more broadly, let us blame the Americans. America needs Columbus to be a world-changing hero battling ignorance and superstition, the enlightened modern man ending medieval darkness.

In the early nineteenth century the famous American writer Washington Irving publishes *A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus*. Irving knows that learned men connected with Salamanca criticized Columbus and opposed funding. From that seed he creates pages of magnificent drama: a coalition of ignorance tries to halt the advance of truth, invoking the antipodes, Isaiah, and the Bible's tent as if fifteenth-century experts still argued like a few Christians a thousand years earlier.

The book is read everywhere in America, and its picture of Columbus before the scholars of Salamanca enters collective memory. It survives in schoolbooks and is cast in bronze on the Columbus Doors of the United States Capitol. Columbus becomes enlightened modernity confronting the superstition and ignorance of the past. One final point will close the story.

## 9. Modern flat-earthers and the duty to verify

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00985–U-01035 -->

<!-- research: [Q-015] [Q-016] [C-015] [C-016] -->

So everyone knew the Earth was round. Then who writes the following demonstration? “Human beings require a surface on which to live that, in its general character, shall be LEVEL; and since the Omniscient Creator must have been perfectly acquainted with the requirements of His creatures, it follows that, being an All-wise Creator, He has met them thoroughly. This is a theological proof that the Earth is not a globe.”

Had I opened by attributing that passage to a doctor of Salamanca, I suspect everyone would have accepted it. It comes instead from a nineteenth-century American, William Carpenter, who in 1885 self-publishes in Baltimore—American publishing deserves credit for that restraint—a book called *One Hundred Proofs That the Earth Is Not a Globe*. Carpenter's 1885 pamphlet belongs to a nineteenth-century flat-Earth revival that had already developed around Samuel Rowbotham in England. [N-006] The hundred proofs are extraordinary and available online. One says that if you rise in a balloon and look down, the Earth plainly appears flat.

A Flat Earth Society, Barbero says, still exists in America. Its thousands of adherents believe Earth is flat and that authorities deceive us about it, just as they supposedly fabricated the Moon landing. Perhaps for the first time, people believe in a flat Earth because they believe everything they are told forms part of a conspiracy.

Thinking about this, I worry that I may seem to have done the same thing during these evenings. I hope I have not told you that anything you hear is likely false. What I hope I have said is that we should not believe a claim merely because someone tells us or because we read it. If it matters to us and we truly want to believe it, then within the limits of our abilities we must try to check it. Thank you.
