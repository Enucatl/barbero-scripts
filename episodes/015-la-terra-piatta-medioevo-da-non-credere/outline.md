# Episode outline

## Overview

Barbero dismantles the modern myth that educated medieval Europeans thought the Earth was flat. He moves from popular culture to medieval and ancient learned texts, explains the narrower debate over the antipodes, returns to Columbus's badly underestimated westward distance, traces the Salamanca legend to Washington Irving, and closes with the genuinely modern flat-Earth revival and a plea to verify received claims.

## 1. The modern myth of a flat medieval Earth

- Transcript: U-00001–U-00123; original audio: 00:54–07:20.
- Argument: the flat-medieval-Earth story is a modern superstition, reinforced by Disney, cartoons, and the heroic Columbus legend.
- Steps/examples: *The Sword in the Stone*; Altan's Columbus; imperial globes and the globus cruciger.
- Digression: Barbero casts himself as the series' spoilsport.
- Targets: Q-001, Q-017; C-001–C-002.

## 2. Bede, John of Holywood, and the medieval spherical Earth

- Transcript: U-00124–U-00274; original audio: 07:30–15:49.
- Argument: widely copied and taught medieval works explicitly describe a spherical Earth.
- Steps/examples: Bede's globe and ball language; the Wizard of Id pizza joke; Sacrobosco's *De sphaera* and print history.
- Digression: the ambiguity of “round” illustrated with a pizza.
- Targets: Q-002–Q-004; C-003–C-004.

## 3. Ancient observations inherited by medieval scholars

- Transcript: U-00275–U-00355; original audio: 15:58–20:26.
- Argument: medieval scholars inherit ancient demonstrations based on observation and reasoning.
- Steps/examples: changing stars with latitude; Earth's curved eclipse shadow; mountains appearing progressively over the horizon.
- Digression: photographs from the Moon make the modern answer too easy.
- Targets: Q-005–Q-006; C-005.

## 4. Biblical imagery, early Christians, and Augustine

- Transcript: U-00356–U-00542; original audio: 20:34–31:10.
- Argument: some late-antique Christians read biblical imagery cosmographically, but Augustine warns Christians not to oppose demonstrated natural knowledge with a rash scriptural reading.
- Steps/examples: Revelation's four corners; Psalms and Isaiah's tent; Cosmas; Constantine and Christian accommodation to classical learning; Augustine's method.
- Digression: biblical shepherd imagery and the difference between literary and scientific language.
- Targets: Q-007–Q-010; C-006–C-007.

## 5. Explaining a spherical Earth to ordinary people

- Transcript: U-00543–U-00693; original audio: 31:17–39:56.
- Argument: vernacular and popular medieval works also explain sphericity and possible circumnavigation.
- Steps/examples: John Mandeville's pole-star observation, round world, sailing around it, and reciprocal “down.”
- Digression: print counts as a rough measure of popularity.
- Targets: Q-011; C-008–C-009.

## 6. Antipodes, the torrid zone, and the ocean

- Transcript: U-00694–U-00811; original audio: 40:14–47:16.
- Argument: learned disagreement concerned habitation and access, not Earth's shape.
- Steps/examples: Pliny against the falling-off objection; impassable torrid zone; Adamic descent; land/water estimates.
- Digression: Barbero acknowledges leaving the nominal flat-Earth topic.
- Targets: Q-012; C-010–C-011.

## 7. Columbus, expert review, and erroneous distances

- Transcript: U-00812–U-00929; original audio: 47:26–54:03.
- Argument: experts objected to Columbus's distance estimates, not to a spherical Earth, and their objection was substantially correct.
- Steps/examples: spice routes; a royal expert commission; Columbus's unit choices and expanded Asia; America saves the voyage.
- Digression: expert project review compared to modern public spending.
- Targets: Q-013; C-012–C-013.

## 8. Washington Irving invents the Salamanca legend

- Transcript: U-00930–U-00984; original audio: 54:09–56:44.
- Argument: Irving's 1828 romantic biography dramatized Columbus confronting scriptural obscurantists and fixed the scene in American memory.
- Steps/examples: Irving's narrative; schoolbooks; the Columbus Doors at the U.S. Capitol.
- Digression: mock collective blame assigned to Americans.
- Targets: Q-014; C-014.

## 9. Modern flat-earthers and the duty to verify

- Transcript: U-00985–U-01035; original audio: 56:53–60:25.
- Argument: explicit modern flat-Earth polemic appears in the nineteenth century; the lesson is active verification, not blanket distrust.
- Steps/examples: Carpenter's theological and balloon proofs; organized flat-Earth belief; closing epistemic appeal.
- Digression: Barbero asks whether myth-busting itself could encourage conspiracism.
- Targets: Q-015–Q-016; C-015–C-016.
