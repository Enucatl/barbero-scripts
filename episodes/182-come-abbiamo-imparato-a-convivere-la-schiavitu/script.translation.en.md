# Come abbiamo imparato a convivere: la schiavitù — faithful English translation

## 1. Slavery as a historical norm and the distinction between slaveholding and slave societies

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00093 -->

So, slavery. Slavery is an enormous subject, because in fact almost all human societies in the past knew slavery, until fairly recent times. In Europe it was only with the Enlightenment in the eighteenth century that someone began to think something that neither the Greeks, however clever they were, nor the Romans, nor Christians had thought: namely, that there was something intrinsically wrong about slavery, something intrinsically unjust and indeed shameful. Before then, no one had ever thought it. And even so, we have to reach the second half of the nineteenth century before slavery finally disappears from the West as well, from the United States, from South America. So let us say that the period in which slavery ceased to be a problem is roughly a hundred or a hundred and fifty years old, no more than that. Before then, for millennia, slavery had always been present everywhere.

Everywhere means even in places and periods where perhaps we would not expect it. Because, well, I think everybody knows that the ancient Romans had slaves. But the fact that there were slaves even in the Florence of Lorenzo the Magnificent, for example, is not part of our mental furniture, so to speak. And yet there were, because no one had ever formally abolished slavery. In Renaissance Italy, I will not say the cities were full of slaves, but yes, some of them really were full. The homes of the rich, of princes, but also of merchants: it was completely normal to find male and female slaves in the home of a wealthy person in a Renaissance Italian city.

Why? Because the Christian world was in a permanent state of war with the Muslim world, and the Mediterranean was a battlefield. When Muslim ships were boarded, the people aboard them were captured: not only the crew, but civilians, merchants, and passengers. The Muslims naturally did the same. And those people were captured and sold. To whom? To whoever was pleased to buy himself a male slave—or, even more often, a female slave—to keep in the house. Or people were captured during raids carried out by Christian galleys along the coasts of North Africa, raids entirely identical to those carried out by the Barbary pirates along our coasts. Except that, since memory is always selective, we remember perfectly well that the Italian coasts in the Renaissance were continually attacked by Barbary pirates. We tend not to think about the fact that our people were doing the same thing on the other side.

But precisely because slaves were in fact everywhere, historians have been led to draw a very sharp distinction. In our terminology, we say that there are societies with slaves and slave societies. What does that mean? Slave societies are societies actually founded on slavery. They are societies that could not function if there were no slaves, or at any rate would have to function in a very different way. Ancient Greece, the Roman Empire up to a certain period, Georgia, Alabama, Virginia, and Texas before the American Civil War, the Caribbean—Haiti, Santo Domingo, Jamaica under French or English planters—those were slave societies in which slavery was an indispensable part of the system.

Renaissance Italy was not the same thing. You did not see slaves everywhere; of course, you knew that rich people had one or two at home, but if they had not had them, almost nothing would have changed. So we make this distinction. And since today I have to choose, I will concentrate on slave societies: that is, societies in which slaves are omnipresent, extremely numerous, and, so to speak, indispensable to the operation of the system. I will tell you, if I can manage it in an hour, four stories. Obviously, they do not amount to a history of slave societies. But placed together and connected to one another, they may allow us to gain a clearer idea of what it meant to live in a world based on slavery.

## 2. Rome in 61 CE and the murder of Pedanius Secundus

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00094–U-00175 -->

The first story is told by Tacitus. So we are in the Roman Empire, in AD 61, during the reign of Nero. Tacitus relates that in that year a very serious crime took place in Rome: the prefect of the city, Pedanius Secundus, was murdered in his own home by one of his slaves. The prefect of the city was what we might call the minister of the interior, all right? So he was an extremely high-ranking official and politician, one of the most important men in Rome. Tacitus tells us that he was killed in his bed by one of his own slaves.

Why did this slave kill his master? Tacitus is not all that interested in the question. But there is some information, or at least some uncertainty. It is not clear, Tacitus says, which of two possible motives was involved, and both possible motives already help us understand how that world worked. Some said it was because this slave had made arrangements to buy back his freedom. His master had promised to allow it and then changed his mind.

What does it mean for a slave to buy his own freedom? It is a paradox, but everything in the history of slavery is paradoxical. The slave belongs to his master, who in ancient Rome can do anything he likes to him, even kill him, and no one will say anything. And so everything belonging to the slave automatically belongs to the master. And yet there are many slaves whom their masters permit, for example, to set up a small business or activity, obviously paying part of their earnings to the master, and these slaves can nevertheless accumulate money of their own. That money belongs to the master too, if the master chooses to take it. But respectable people instead allow the slave to accumulate his own *peculium*—that is exactly what they called it—which he may eventually use, if the master agrees, to purchase himself, so to speak.

Naturally, it also happens that a slave has relatives who are free. Masters in ancient Rome often free faithful male and female slaves, people of whom they may even be fond, while those people may have brothers, sisters, or children who are still enslaved. In this case, it appears that this slave's father may in fact have been free and wanted to buy him back. The master had said yes and then changed his mind.

But during the trial on this occasion another possibility also emerges, and Tacitus throws that one in too. He tells these things in two lines; I am making a long story of it, but he throws it out in two lines. Perhaps this slave was in love with a young male prostitute, and the master was his rival and had taken the boy away from him. This brings us, let us say, into the atmosphere of absolute sexual freedom that prevailed in the Roman world, especially among the ruling class. Everyone, or almost everyone, either was or could perfectly easily be bisexual. Male prostitution was as widespread as female prostitution. No one was scandalized; these things were entirely normal.

So one could say: yes, yes, apparently, from what I have heard, the story went like this—the prefect Pedanius Secundus was, so to speak, in love with this boy who prostituted himself and wanted him entirely for himself. His slave had fallen in love with him too. In short, master and slave were rivals, and in the end the slave killed the master.

But the story is significant for a different reason. The law provided that when a master was killed in his own home, whether the culprit was known or unknown made no difference whatsoever: all the slaves living under the same roof had to be crucified. As a matter of principle, because slaves had to know that it was in their interest to protect their master's life and that, if they failed to do so, they would all pay. Guilty or innocent made no difference.

The prefect Pedanius Secundus had four hundred slaves living with him in his house. Do you understand? Men, women, old people, boys, girls, pregnant women—four hundred people. And the law provided that they should all be crucified. When word spread through the city of Rome that this was about to happen, the people rose up. They poured into the squares and streets, protesting and trying to prevent all these people from being executed.

And they did so because in a slave society slaves are everywhere, and their lives are not always separate from everyone else's. There are plenty of slaves in the street whom you cannot distinguish from other people. They work as laborers or mechanics, or run a small shop belonging to their master, and they have relationships with other people. For ordinary people it is completely normal that family friends, or perhaps even relatives, might be the slaves of a wealthy owner. In short, to the common people, slaves are people like us after all. And the prospect of this mass execution produces unrest among the people. The Senate has to debate what to do.

## 3. Cassius Longinus, collective punishment, and the execution of four hundred slaves

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00176–U-00276 -->

We are under the Empire, as I told you, but the Senate still exists. Even under the Empire the Senate actually has enormous power. It has to deal with the emperor, who is a dictator with the power of life and death, but the Senate is, you understand, the assembly of the three hundred, four hundred, or five hundred richest men in the Empire, and so it does count for something.

The Senate debates what to do. Some say that perhaps applying the law in this particular case really would be a little too much. Tacitus, however, is interested only in what I am about to tell you, only in the speech I will now recount. An authoritative senator, Cassius Longinus, rises to speak, and Tacitus reports his speech word for word.

Cassius Longinus says to his fellow senators: You know me. You know that I have often spoken in this chamber when some proposed law introduced an innovation or altered an old law, and I have never opposed it. Not because I do not know that changing old laws is always wrong—because it is always wrong and weakens our authority. But I did not want to oppose such measures and give the impression that I was doing so on principle, out of prejudice. This time, however, I must tell you that we cannot fail to apply the law and change it. We cannot.

A man of consular rank, at the very summit of the imperial aristocracy, has been killed in his own home in a slave conspiracy. No one prevented it. No one reported it, even though the law condemning all those slaves to death was in force. And you would abolish that law? Then, by Hercules, establish impunity. Establish that a master can be killed in his own house with perfect ease. Who will be protected by his high rank, when being prefect of the city did not protect Pedanius Secundus? Who will be protected by having many slaves, when four hundred were not enough to protect him?

They say—and this is where Tacitus obtained the information—that perhaps the murderer was merely avenging an injury, because he had made an agreement to be freed and was not freed, or because the master had taken a young male slave away from him. Very well. In that case, let us rule that the master was justly killed.

Cassius Longinus goes on. The very fact that our ancestors established this law proves that it is just and must not be changed. But let us pretend that we have to debate it for the first time. Surely you do not believe that a slave decided to kill his master without ever letting slip a threatening word, without ever making dangerous remarks? No one heard him? Let us grant that no one heard him, that he managed to prepare the weapon among people who noticed nothing. How did he cross the bedrooms, carry in a lamp, and commit his crime without anyone seeing him?

Our ancestors knew perfectly well that our only safety lay in slaves knowing that it was in their interest to report plots and dangers. And our ancestors did this when slaves were people born here, people they had known from birth. But now, as you understand, the Roman Empire has expanded enormously through victorious wars, and each of those wars has brought millions of slaves into the Empire from the most distant countries. Since our slaves have become foreigners, Cassius Longinus says, people with rites different from ours, an alien religion or perhaps no religion at all—this multitude, our slaves, can be kept in submission only by fear.

And you say that innocent people will die as well. Very well, Cassius Longinus says, that is acceptable, because the great examples made for the public interest always contain something unjust. He gives the example of another Roman practice, rarely applied, but sometimes applied: the decimation of military units that had behaved badly before the enemy. When a military unit had behaved cowardly in the face of the enemy, its commander could order a decimation. That meant all the men were lined up, disarmed, amid the rest of the army, which was armed. Lots were drawn, and one man in ten was beaten to death. Whoever drew the lot drew it. He might be a decorated veteran. He might be someone who had not fled at all that day, even though all the others had. Whoever's turn it was, it was. This case is the same, Cassius Longinus said.

Some of the other senators try to say, yes, but the women; yes, but the children. Nothing doing. In the end the majority sides with Longinus, and they decide to carry out the sentence. But the people are rioting. At this point Emperor Nero has to intervene, send the army into the streets, block the roads, and open a passage along which these four hundred wretched people are taken and all crucified.

At this point someone says that the freed slaves as well—the freedmen whom the dead man had liberated during his lifetime—ought to be expelled from Italy. There, at least, Nero objects. He says that the traditional law is already severe enough and they do not wish to interpret it with still greater cruelty. But the law was applied.

## 4. Dionigi Carli encounters plantation slavery in seventeenth-century Brazil

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00277–U-00343 -->

And that was the first story. I do not think I will comment on the stories I tell you, because all things considered, they seem to speak for themselves. The second story brings us much closer in time, to the seventeenth century, the 1600s. What happened in the meantime? The Roman Empire collapsed, and in our part of the world slavery gradually died out. It was never abolished, but in the medieval and Renaissance world it was no longer an important structure. Slaves were, for example, the domestic servants in the homes of wealthy merchants who had bought a woman captured at sea from privateers. Those were the slaves.

But with the colonization of America, slavery suddenly became extremely important again. This happened with the Spanish colonization of Central and South America, and later with the English colonization of North America, but it began above all in Spanish and Portuguese Central and South America, in Brazil. There was an immense country offering the promise of enormous profits. What crops were grown there? Sugarcane, and later tobacco. European landowners immediately realized that these crops required a great deal of labor, a very great deal. The Indigenous people were not suited to it; the local population, the *Indios*, as the Spanish called them, did not withstand forced labor well, and they were also decimated by disease. There was not enough labor, so it had to be imported.

From the end of the fifteenth century, Europeans had grown accustomed to knowing Africa a little better and had discovered that it was very easy to enslave local people there. And so the trafficking of Black slaves to America began. It continued for centuries and filled America with Black people.

The story I want to tell you is about a Capuchin called Dionigi Carli of Piacenza, a Capuchin missionary, a deeply religious friar who sacrificed his life to serve as a missionary. He went to Africa and to Brazil, traveling between the two coasts. When our Italian Capuchin arrived in Brazil, he encountered Black slavery for the first time—mass slavery, that is. Back home, yes, a duchess might still have had a little Moor as a page, given to her by somebody, but slavery was no longer very visible. When our Capuchin arrived in America, by contrast, he saw it everywhere.

This Capuchin, Dionigi Carli, wrote a book describing his travels. He tells his readers that when you arrive in Brazil, you see them everywhere—these Moors, as he calls them, these Black people. “We then saw many others, four, six, or eight at a time, chained—some by the neck, some by the hands, some by the feet—a salutary medicine, for without it they would all flee together.”

He says: At first they made me feel compassion. At first it seemed cruel to me. But then, when I began to understand what sort of people these Moors are, and that they are people who would not work unless forced, I understood that it had to be done this way. There was no alternative.

He is a very decent man. At first he feels a little compassion, but that is the system. Everyone he meets tells him, of course that is how it is, and it could not be otherwise. It takes him no time at all to accept that, well, of course, yes, yes, it has to be that way.

They take him to see sugarcane being processed. There is a crushing mill turned by slaves with enormous effort, and overseers stand ready with their whips. He says, yes, yes, the poor things; it makes you feel sorry to see them whipped like that. But on the other hand, it is a necessary medicine, because they would not want to do the work. I saw them in Africa, he says. They are people who know how to do nothing but dance. And so, if you want these Black people to work, of course you need the whip.

## 5. The Atlantic crossing and Carli's moral accommodation to the slave trade

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00344–U-00477 -->

Then, during one of his journeys, he boarded a ship in Africa bound for Brazil. He embarked in Angola, a Portuguese colony, on a ship loaded with slaves that also carried passengers and cargo, and he recounts his voyage aboard it. He says that six hundred Moors had already been loaded on this vessel. He tells these things with the utmost calm; they are interesting facts. They were all branded according to custom, some on one shoulder, some on the right arm, some on the left, and others above their breasts. All were branded with fire, each bearing the mark of his or her owner.

Then he describes how they were packed aboard the ship. The men were all crammed into the hold itself, right down at the very bottom, practically in darkness, shut inside. Because, he says, if they were free to move, they might cause trouble. They might set fire to the ship or throw themselves into the sea and drown, because, he says, they did this continually. These Moors being carried toward America threw themselves into the sea and drowned whenever they had the opportunity.

And he says: Do you see why they do this? Because these Moors are truly stupid. They have a superstitious belief that the Portuguese are taking them to America to kill them and make oil from their bodies. And because they believe this, they prefer to kill themselves before arriving. And I, our good friar says, explained to them that this was not true, that it was completely false, that they could rest easy because no one would kill them when they arrived. But it made no difference. They were so stubborn. I saw a Black woman belonging to the ship's pilot, who had permission to walk around freely, throw herself into the sea. He caught her by one foot and managed to pull her back up. Such, the friar says, is the obstinacy of these Ethiopians. He also calls the Moors Ethiopians, always simply meaning Black people.

So all the adult men were crammed down in the hold. Then there were the various decks, with the women on the second deck. The pregnant women, however—forty of the six hundred people embarked—were placed in the stern cabin, where they were a little more comfortable. These were societies of the past in which children were continually being born and then easily died, societies in which a considerable proportion of the women were pregnant at any given moment. We can no longer even picture what that must have looked like. Then there were the children and young people on the first deck below the main deck, packed so tightly that they lay on top of one another and relieved themselves where they were. The stench, he says, was unbearable.

The ship was already overloaded when another seventy Moors arrived to be embarked. And here a problem arose, because Portuguese law—the priorities of different periods are different—perfectly calmly permitted the slave trade, but provided that slaves taken from Africa to America had to be baptized. They had to be taught the catechism, converted to Christianity, and baptized; otherwise it was unlawful to embark them. These seventy new arrivals had not yet been baptized.

So before they could be embarked, the friar had to get busy. Who knows how he did it, in what language or in what manner, but at any rate he explained Christianity to them and baptized them all. “There is excommunication for taking Africans to Brazil, or elsewhere, unless they have first been baptized ... I also baptized those children who were born from time to time to the pregnant women.” Once baptized, they were naturally branded and put aboard.

The voyage went badly because there was no wind and the ship could not move. Why was that? Why? It was truly unusual in that season. Something was wrong. There had to be a reason. Why was God not sending them wind? Were they absolutely certain that all these Moors had been baptized? He asked the captain, and the captain said, yes, yes, now I remember: of course, I brought another four aboard at the last moment, and they have not been baptized.

The friar had them brought to him at once. After instructing them as well as possible, he says, I baptized them, together with another three who had just been born and were still steaming.

But the wind still did not come. Now, all right, this has nothing to do with slavery, but it gives us a glimpse into the mentality of seventeenth-century sailors. The Portuguese sailors took the statue of Saint Anthony of Padua they had aboard and tied it to the mainmast. Then they knelt before Saint Anthony's statue, beat their breasts, and told him: You will remain here until you obtain from God the good wind that will set the ship moving again.

The friar disapproved. This business of tying up the saint was not altogether proper. It would have been better to bring the statue up and pray to him, without the violence of tying him to the mainmast. Nevertheless, it worked. The wind came, the ship set off again, and the saint had granted the favor.

The voyage eventually ended, though not without further difficulties. At the end they calculated that thirty-three Moors had died during the voyage out of the more than six hundred and seventy embarked. The friar says everyone was delighted: it was a grace from God that so few had died, because ordinarily half of them died, and sometimes even more.

He then says that they had every problem in the world during these voyages. These voyages carrying slaves were the most painful and distressing in the world. Not so much because of the captives' suffering; the whole business was badly organized in itself. Why? Because the ships were overloaded. Yet everyone wanted to embark his slaves: the next ship was leaving in a month, and I cannot possibly wait. And so everyone wanted to put his slaves aboard, meaning the ships were always overloaded. But they also had to be loaded with food and water, bearing in mind that these were not crates of sugar—a typical American product, no?—but living people who ate and drank. And so they did in fact have to be given food and water.

Unfortunately, at a certain point the captain came and said that, because of all the delays, their food supplies were running out. The friar told him: Look, I know that an acquaintance of mine had crates of goods placed in the cargo below, and I know there is also food in them. Open the crates; I will take responsibility. We will have some more food, and these provisions, he says, will serve to keep the white people alive. If the Moors die, we shall have to be patient.

Then they checked the water, and there was plenty of it. The friar was delighted. Very well, he said, the Moors will survive for two days on water, and in the meantime the Lord will help us.

Meanwhile, word spread aboard the ship that the food had run out. The Black people in the hold began to grow agitated, shouting and pounding. The friar says, yes, yes, poor things, I can understand them too. We let them vent. One must trust in God and in the Madonna.

At this point the friar slips in a small detail. I was very upset, he says, because I discovered that the sailors had nicknamed the whip they used to beat the slaves *Maria*. And the friar says: You call the whip after the Madonna? How do you expect them to believe what we teach them about Christianity? They will think we are making fools of them.

So, you understand, we have to make an effort not to feel horror toward this friar. For him, what genuinely matters is that we save their souls. Their lives matter only up to a point; what counts is the other life. And we are saving their souls. He truly believes it.

After this they prayed day and night, and all the white people confessed as though they were at the point of death. Then one of the sailors said that perhaps it would be better to kill all the Moors, to make sure there would be enough food. Here the friar objected: it was a diabolical proposal. One person must never be killed so that another can live. He says that if he had not been there, they might even have done it, but he was there and would not allow it.

And here, although I promised not to comment, perhaps this too requires no comment, except to observe how living in a society where slavery is normal produces what seems to us like a dulling of the moral sense: you no longer see things that seem so obvious to us. Are we running a little late? Faster, then. The third story.

## 6. Joseph Knight and the judicial rejection of slavery in Scotland

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00478–U-00647 -->

The third story takes us a little further forward in time, to the eighteenth century, when many voices in Europe began to say that there was something wrong with slavery, that slavery was unjust, that slavery was unlawful. It is the story of a slave who was born in Africa, captured, and sold in Jamaica, an English possession with great sugarcane plantations, where sugar and rum were produced.

His owners gave this slave the name Joseph Knight—Knight, with a K, Joseph Knight—and in Jamaica he was bought by a Scottish planter, Sir John Wedderburn. He was an extremely wealthy planter. He bought this slave, grew fond of him, and took him as his personal manservant. You understand? A slave's fate differed enormously depending on whether he was sent to turn the crushing mill all day under the whip, or was instead the master's manservant, living in the house, well dressed and well fed, with everything that entailed.

The master grew fond of him. After some years, in 1769, he returned from Jamaica to Scotland and brought the slave with him. In Scotland, at the master's home, at Sir John's home, something happened that we would not expect. First, once they arrived in Scotland, he had him baptized. Protestants were evidently less scrupulous than Portuguese Catholics; they were not required to baptize their slaves. But Sir John had his dear manservant Joseph Knight baptized.

And then something happened that I would not have believed possible, although the sources speak of it as entirely normal. Joseph Knight fell in love with a domestic servant in the household, Ann Thompson. She was Scottish and therefore white. They fell in love and married. The master allowed them to marry and even paid the wedding expenses himself. They had a child, whom the master had baptized. The child then died, and the master paid for the burial.

At this point Joseph wanted to go and live with Ann, his wife, in a small house of their own, while remaining in the master's service. Until then they had lived in the house. Do you remember *The Marriage of Figaro*? Figaro and Susanna are the count and countess's servants; they live in the house. Now Joseph and Ann wanted to live on their own.

Here we do not know why, but Joseph and his master quarreled. Something happened that is not clear to us. The master did not want it and refused. It ended very badly: the master dismissed Ann. She was a free woman employed in his service, and he dismissed her. At that point Joseph simply left the house and went to live with his wife.

At that point the master remembered that Joseph was his slave, sent the police to seize him, and had him brought back home. He announced that he was about to return to Jamaica and intended to take Joseph with him.

Here begins the most unexpected part of the story—perhaps not unexpected, but in any event the part that makes the story historically very important. Joseph Knight, the slave, had evidently received advice and made acquaintances and friends. He decided to sue his master in the Scottish courts, to establish that his master had no right to take him back to Jamaica.

What was the situation? At that time the situation in Britain was extremely ambiguous. There were practically no slaves in Britain, except when a wealthy planter returned from the West Indies and, as in this case, brought his slave with him. There had already been several trials of this kind in England, and judgments had declared—I translate—“As soon as a negro comes into England, he becomes free; one may be a villein in England, but not a slave.” And the law would therefore protect him in the enjoyment of his person and his property.

These, however, were judgments by individual judges, not legislation. They could be cited, but they could also be contradicted. And they were English judgments. Here, by contrast, we are in Scotland. You may know that England and Scotland are not only two different nations; the respect in which they are most profoundly different is precisely their legal systems. The Scottish legal system and Scottish law are completely different from their English counterparts.

In 1774, while the rebellious colonists in America were already agitating for liberty and equality, preparing to proclaim that all men were equal even though many of them were naturally slave owners, including George Washington and so forth, Joseph Knight sued his master before the justices of the peace in Perth. He argued that slavery did not exist in Scotland and that, even if he had been lawfully purchased in Jamaica, he automatically became free when he arrived in Scotland.

The master opposed him, and the judges ruled for the master. Among other things, Sir John's lawyer reasoned as follows: Let us think about this. As a matter of principle, it makes no difference whether we are discussing one Negro—I am using their words—or twenty thousand. If you tell us that an owner has no right to transport his slave to Jamaica, then you will also tell us that a person engaged in the mass trade cannot do so either. Is it forbidden to take slaves to Jamaica? Sir John's lawyers said that no one could seriously maintain such a ridiculous proposition in a country inhabited by rational beings. The supposedly ridiculous proposition was that slaves could not be taken to Jamaica.

Very well. Joseph lost the case and appealed to the higher judge, the sheriff of Perth. The sheriff of Perth ruled for Joseph. The condition of slavery was not recognized by the laws of this kingdom and was inconsistent with its principles. The Jamaican regulations concerning slaves did not extend to this kingdom. Therefore, the master had no right to demand Joseph's compulsory service. Do you understand? This was a world and an age in which judges were making law. There were no acts of Parliament. There was public opinion, which the judges interpreted, and it was moving in that direction.

Sir John in turn appealed to the supreme court in Edinburgh. He argued that perhaps slavery did not exist in Scotland, but Scotland did have—and this was true—contracts by which a person bound himself to serve a master for life. Such contracts could bind domestic servants to a master. Craftsmen's apprentices too often signed contracts obliging them to serve for as long as the master wished. These contracts were perfectly lawful in Scotland. So Sir John said: Very well, he is not my slave, agreed. But he is obliged to serve me, and Scottish law provides for that.

Joseph's defense lawyers before Scotland's supreme court in Edinburgh based their case instead on the fact that the matter at issue was slavery. Perhaps for the first time in the entire case, they explained in detail why slavery could not be tolerated in the civilized world. They said: Let us not attempt to establish how this man was captured in Africa and brought to Jamaica. We cannot know it. Jamaican law does not concern itself with that detail; it accepts the situation. But whether he was captured, kidnapped, or sold by his parents—and that happened quite normally too—the injustice in Africa was the same.

The fact that slavery had existed among so many peoples in the past did not make it any less unjust. In the past, some societies had accepted infanticide, for example. That did not make infanticide any less ignoble, Joseph's lawyers said. Nor could they debate the fact that Negro slavery was highly advantageous to the country and produced great profits. That had nothing to do with whether it was just.

And in any event, they said, enlightened authors were by now arguing that even economically slavery was not so profitable after all, and that cotton—no, sugar—and tobacco could be cultivated without slaves. But what mattered was that oppression and injustice could never be justified by the advantages they produced.

The supreme court in Edinburgh rejected Sir John's appeal and confirmed that Joseph was a free man. Since the dominion over this Negro under Jamaican law was unjust, it could not be upheld in this country in any form. Therefore, the defendant, Sir John, had no right to the Negro's services for any period, nor any right to send him out of the country without his consent. The Negro was protected by law against these abuses.

And this case made history. From that moment onward, no one could be held in slavery on the soil of the kingdom of Scotland, just as similar judgments in England produced the same effect there.

It would be wonderful to know what happened afterward to Joseph Knight and his wife Annie, but we do not know. They disappear from history. We have the court records, but he was a free man. Presumably he lived in Scotland, working as a servant or doing something else together with his wife. We know nothing more. What remains in history is the case, *Knight v. Wedderburn*.

I do not know whether you noticed that we began with a story, Tacitus's story, involving hundreds of slaves about whom we do not even know whether they were male or female. I said they were, but Tacitus does not. Our seventeenth-century Capuchin, at least, took an interest in the fact that these were adult men and those were women. He mentions some people individually, though naturally not by name. Here we have encountered a slave with his first and last name.

## 7. Harriet Jacobs, abolitionist testimony, and childhood in slavery

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00648–U-00753 -->

The final stage concerns not a male slave but, this time, an enslaved woman. We know not only her first and last name; she told us her story herself, because she wrote a book to tell it. There are several such cases in the nineteenth-century United States, and this is one of the most famous. Her name was Harriet Jacobs. She was born around 1815 in the southern United States, in North Carolina.

You know that slavery originally existed throughout the United States, but during the eighteenth century the Northern states outlawed it. The Southern states, whose economies were based on cotton and tobacco, and therefore on a large enslaved workforce, retained it and would continue to do so until the end of the American Civil War in 1865.

Harriet was born around 1815 in North Carolina, a slave state, and died in 1897 after living most of her life as a free woman. She was the first Black enslaved woman to write her autobiography, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself.*, which was published in 1861, even before the Civil War, just as the war was about to begin.

Harriet wrote this book with the help, naturally, of acquaintances and contacts among people in the North who were fighting for the abolition of slavery. She wrote specifically to address Northern public opinion, where there were movements hostile to slavery—the abolitionists—but Northern public opinion was by no means unanimously in agreement. Even in the North, there were plenty of people who did not care that slavery existed.

Harriet addressed Northern public opinion with a very precise intention. She did not simply denounce the horrors of slavery in general terms, although those horrors are present too. She spoke from a woman's point of view. One senses that she particularly had female readers in mind, the white women who would read her book. She wanted to show the moral degradation that slavery, and especially the enslavement of women, produced within white slave-owning society.

It was a delicate subject. This was the mid-nineteenth century, the Victorian period, when matters of sex were difficult to discuss, and yet that is what she discussed. In the book's epigraph she says that Northerners know nothing about slavery and have no idea of the depth of degradation involved. And again: “I do earnestly desire to arouse the women of the North to a realizing sense of the condition of two millions of women at the South, still in bondage, suffering what I suffered, and most of them far worse.”

She was therefore the child of slaves. The subject will recur in the family: right from the beginning, they all had fairly light skin. It is clear that, if one could reconstruct the family tree, more than one white master had impregnated a Black enslaved woman. And so they were descended from white people; they were Black; they were slaves. But they lived well and were fairly happy.

Her father was a carpenter. His owners permitted him to have his own carpentry workshop. He paid them an annual rent and otherwise lived his own life. Harriet says: I was born a slave, but I never knew it until I was six years old. I had a happy childhood in which I understood nothing about any of this. It seemed like a normal life.

By working as a carpenter, her father accumulated savings. Like the people in the Roman world we discussed at the beginning, he too hoped to save enough money to buy his children. He was himself a slave, but before freeing himself he wanted to free his children. A slave can own slaves. It is an insane world, as you will see. A slave can perfectly well buy and own other slaves. He hoped to buy his children and free them, but their mistress would never agree.

They had a good mistress who was very close to Harriet's mother. They were milk sisters, because Harriet's grandmother had been wet nurse both to her own daughter and to the little mistress. She had nursed her daughter and the newborn mistress together. But Harriet's mother, the little enslaved girl, was weaned at three months so that the nurse would have enough milk for the little mistress.

As children, the little mistress and Harriet's mother played together. Harriet's mother later died, and Harriet remained with this mistress, who treated her extremely well—almost like a daughter, Harriet says. She taught her to read and write. When Harriet's mother lay dying, the mistress promised her that her children would never have anything to fear. Everyone was fairly convinced that the mistress would free them all in her will.

Instead, when this good mistress—who had treated Harriet like a daughter—died, it did not occur to her to free them in her will. She bequeathed Harriet to a little niece, a young child, who therefore became Harriet's new owner. Other members of the family, Harriet's uncles, were sold at auction by the executors.

Harriet found herself living in the home of the little girl's father. It was a middle-class family. The father, Dr. Flint, was a physician. They were in a small town in North Carolina, an ordinary middle-class family. Harriet was twelve when the mistress died; she moved into the family's home and lived in this situation. As I told you, there was no shortage of paradoxes.

Harriet's grandmother was still alive. Her mother was dead, but her grandmother remained. She was an old Black woman, an elderly enslaved woman, very well known and popular in the town. When the mistress died, everyone expected that she would have freed her, but she had not. Dr. Flint, Harriet's new master, was an executor of the will and decided to sell the old woman at auction too. The town rebelled.

When he decided to sell the elderly lady—let us call her that—Aunt Martha, as everyone called her, she was of course a slave. But the white townspeople thought selling Aunt Martha was wrong, a disgrace. No one appeared at the auction. The price fell and fell and fell. Then the dead mistress's sister arrived, bought Aunt Martha, and freed her.

So Harriet was a slave in Dr. Flint's household, while her grandmother was free at about fifty years old and lived nearby in her own home. She sewed, did work for local ladies, lived well, and accumulated money. At a certain point, the grandmother, who was saving money to buy back her children, managed to buy one of them. She had put aside eight hundred dollars, an enormous sum in the United States of the 1820s and 1830s. She had saved it, bought back one of her children, and freed him.

## 8. Sexual coercion, resistance, escape, and Harriet Jacobs's freedom

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00754–U-00947 -->

At the center of the story, naturally, is Harriet, the young girl in Dr. Flint's household. Technically he was not her owner—her owner was Dr. Flint's daughter, who was a child—but, in short, Harriet soon realized that Dr. Flint, a respected member of the community, habitually slept with his enslaved women and made them pregnant.

She discovered this one day when the master had a male slave whipped. This man was married to another enslaved person. They had just had a baby, and both father and mother were very dark-skinned, Harriet says. The baby was born light-skinned, and the husband became angry with his wife, accusing her of sleeping with the master. The master would not tolerate these things being discussed in his household. So he had the husband whipped, and a few days later had all of them sold.

As they were leaving, the woman—the enslaved woman who had just borne the master's child and was now being sold—said to him: You promised to treat me well. The master replied: Your tongue is too long. These things must not be known.

Harriet says that, through whispers, since the matter was discussed only in the greatest secrecy within the house, she calculated that the master had fathered eleven children with his enslaved women. Each time, he sold the children together with their mothers to avoid the inconvenience. And Harriet says: Look, this happens in every household.

Within white society, men who behaved in this way were sometimes criticized. The ladies especially knew everything and criticized husbands who acted like this. Harriet knew of two cases in which, when a master had a child by an enslaved woman, his wife, the mistress, persuaded him to free the child. But Harriet says these cases were rare. In general, people were so accustomed to it that they no longer noticed.

When Harriet was fourteen she was a beautiful girl, very light-skinned. She could almost pass for white; in fact, she could pass for white. When she was fourteen, the master, who was fifty-five, began telling her: You know, Harriet, that you are in my service. You must do everything I ask. You must obey me, whatever I ask of you. You cannot say no.

Harriet says that entering her fifteenth year was a sad period in the life of an enslaved girl. My master began to whisper filthy things in my ear. She pretended not to notice, and he did not dare go further, because in any event, you understand, one must not overdo things. The neighbors would gossip. He was a physician and a respected person. And then, Harriet says, he was afraid of my grandmother. Old Aunt Martha was now free, known and respected by everyone in town, so Dr. Flint proceeded cautiously.

In such cases, Harriet says, it is useless to hope that the mistress will help you. You are there while the master pursues you, and you might think the mistress would intervene. But no: the mistress is actually jealous of the enslaved woman and begins mistreating her. Harriet says that enslaved children notice from an early age that these inexplicable hatreds exist within the household: the mistress truly hates that woman and always mistreats her—why? Then you grow older and understand.

The mistress did watch her husband. She understood that he had set his sights on Harriet and wanted to prevent him from succeeding. She watched him and, naturally, at a certain point she took Harriet aside and said: Tell me the truth. What have you done with my husband? Harriet said nothing. She swore on the Bible. But the mistress continued treating her badly; she could not help fearing and hating her.

The master saw Harriet distressed and often crying in this situation, and said: Poor child, do not cry. I will make peace between you and the mistress, but let me do things my way. You are a silly little thing; you do not recognize what is good for you. I would pamper you. I would make you a lady.

And here Harriet says: “If God has bestowed beauty upon her, it will prove her greatest curse. That which commands admiration in the white woman only hastens the degradation of the female slave.”

Things continued in this way for some time. The master continued saying filthy things to her, and she pretended not to notice. She wanted to tell her grandmother everything, but she did not, because she was afraid in this situation. She was ashamed and did not dare tell her grandmother everything. The master continued circling around her. He never laid a finger on her, never had her punished, never had her whipped. He simply kept insisting.

Then she, by now a young woman, fell in love. She fell in love with a free Black man, a young carpenter like her father. There were free Black people. Roughly a tenth of the Black population of the Southern states consisted of free Black people whom masters had freed in their wills and who then formed families and had children.

She fell in love with this free Black man, and he offered to buy her, because as a free man, if he had some capital, he could buy her. But she told him the master would never permit it. Dr. Flint would never sell her, least of all to a Black man.

She told everything to a white woman in town whom she trusted. There was this network of complicity—never entirely clear, but one senses that confidences could develop between women, even between a white female owner and an enslaved Black woman. Harriet confided in this white woman, who was a friend of Dr. Flint's and promised to try speaking to him. But the doctor refused. In fact, he was furious and summoned Harriet.

So you want to marry? And a free Negro? I will show you who your master is, whether it is me or that Negro. For the first time, she answered him back, and for the first time he slapped her. Then he delivered a long speech about her ingratitude. He had benefited her in every possible way and treated her extremely well, and this ingratitude was how she repaid him. He told her: Look, I will give you one more chance. Think carefully. As for that Negro, if I see him around the house, I will shoot him like a dog.

For two weeks the master did not speak to her. Then he slipped her a note saying that he regretted hitting her, but it was her fault. She was to blame; she was impossible. Nevertheless, he was sorry and would not do it again.

Meanwhile, the Black man who wanted to marry her received an inheritance in another town, moved away, and left her life. And then—here we enter the most delicate part, which she recounts with great timidity and anxiety—she had a relationship with another white gentleman. Not with the master, but another gentleman, a prosperous man who at one point was a candidate for the North Carolina legislature, or perhaps actually a member of it. He was a gentleman of the town named Mr. Sands. You understand? People saw one another and knew one another; life was shared in any event.

She fell in love with this unmarried gentleman, who often met her in the street. They began talking. In short, it is unclear whether she fell in love, but they certainly had a relationship. She was fifteen and allowed herself to be seduced. In her memoir she has great difficulty admitting this and feels she must apologize. But, she says, I needed a protector. I needed someone to protect me, and he could be that person.

She became pregnant and had to confess it at home. The mistress, Dr. Flint's wife, threw her out. She took refuge with her grandmother and told her everything, and her grandmother threw her out too: a girl who had become pregnant like this, out in the street. But her grandmother reconsidered and called her back.

The father of the expected child, Mr. Sands, came to see her and promised that he would care for the baby, and that as soon as possible he would buy her and the child and free them. The master also came. By now she was staying at her grandmother's house, firmly established there. Dr. Flint told her: You fool, I am a doctor. If you had told me at once, I could have helped you—meaning he could have made her abort. You fool, you did not tell me, and now what? And now I want to know one thing: is this child the child of that Negro you wanted to marry?

She said no, it was the child of a white man. The master went mad with rage but did not hit her. He continued visiting throughout the pregnancy. When the baby was born, Dr. Flint attended her and reminded her that the baby nevertheless belonged to him. She was his slave—his daughter's, technically, but in short, she was his slave, so the baby was his slave.

The child's father, Mr. Sands, also came to see her, but could not officially acknowledge the child because the child was enslaved. Things continued in this way through continual quarrels. She became pregnant again and had another child, also by Mr. Sands. She continued living with her grandmother. Dr. Flint did not dare take her back into his household, especially because his wife would not allow it at any price. So in practice she no longer lived as Dr. Flint's slave, but legally she was still enslaved by him.

The years passed and her children grew. They did not understand what was happening. Why did this white man occasionally come to the house, and why did Dr. Flint shout, threaten, and scream? Her children called him the bad man. But when Dr. Flint lost his head, the grandmother threw him out of the house. She was a free woman and could throw him out, and he left with his tail between his legs.

Finally, when Harriet was twenty-one, she could bear no more and decided to escape. At first, several white women in the neighborhood who knew the whole story took her in and hid her in their homes. She hoped—she hoped—to escape to the North, but it was very difficult.

While she was hidden nearby, Dr. Flint sold her children. He sold them to a slave trader without knowing that the trader already had an agreement with the children's father, Mr. Sands. So the trader bought them. Dr. Flint believed they would be taken far away to the South and that Harriet would never see them again. Instead, they were sold to their father, who freed them.

The whole town knew about this, discussed it, and followed the affair. Harriet says that public opinion was on our side. Everyone was happy that Mr. Sands had bought back our children and freed them.

And then? She could not remain hidden in the homes of the women protecting her, so her grandmother constructed a secret hiding place for her in the house, like Anne Frank, no? There. Harriet moved into it and remained there for several years. She spent years hidden in her grandmother's house, seeing Dr. Flint pass in the street, seeing her own children pass, and seeing Mr. Sands, the father of her children. After a while the children discovered that she was there and came to visit her, but she had to remain silent; no one could see or know anything.

After some years of this life, passage to the North was arranged, so to speak. She found a berth on a ship whose captain was a Southerner. He understood the situation perfectly well but pretended not to. Harriet passed as white, as a white lady, boarded with a ticket, and reached New York.

In New York she happened to encounter one of her master's neighbors, who had come to the city. She was terrified, but the neighbor told her: Look, do not be afraid. I do not care. You had a bad time at the doctor's house. As far as I am concerned, I wish you good luck. I will tell no one. In fact, when I return home, I will visit your grandmother and tell her that I met you.

But at the same time, why was she terrified? She was in New York, a free state. Yet the fact that slavery did not exist in a state did not mean that the owners of fugitive slaves were not permitted to go there and seize them, even in the North. A highly controversial law, recently passed with the involvement of the South, authorized owners to enter free states and recover slaves.

Harriet worked in New York, first as a seamstress and later as a wet nurse. She worked well for good women who housed her and paid her well. They told her: Listen, the solution is for us to buy you. I will buy you.

For a time she resisted. As a matter of principle, she says, she did not want to accept the idea that she was free not because she had the right to be free, but because she had been bought and sold.

In the end, however, Dr. Flint occasionally returned to look for her. The doctor's daughter, who had now grown up and was her true owner, also appeared. Word had spread that Harriet must be in New York, and so Harriet finally agreed.

When the doctor's daughter arrived after they had identified Harriet's hiding place—or rather, where she lived; she was not hidden, she lived there—the New York mistress who employed Harriet confronted the mistress from the South and persuaded her to sell Harriet. Three hundred dollars. The young Southern mistress agreed, returned home with the money, and an instrument of emancipation was drawn up. The New York woman, now Harriet's owner, freed her. Harriet was officially free.

Harriet says that in truth it made her feel bad. On the one hand she was happy and deeply grateful to this woman, but all that happiness turned bitter: “So I was sold at last! A human being sold in the free city of New York! ... I am deeply grateful to the generous friend who procured it, but I despise the miscreant who demanded payment for what never rightfully belonged to him or his.”

## 9. Abolition as genuine moral progress

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00948–U-00966 -->

After that, by then in the 1850s, Harriet met people within New York society who persuaded her to write a book and tell the story of her life. And there, so to speak, it ends.

I would say this. As we will see in the next lectures, when we discuss totalitarianism and democracy, there is no guarantee that history is a continuous process of progress or that, for some unknown reason, things automatically improve. That is not how it is.

But if one examines the history of humanity as a whole, there has been some genuine progress. I mean moral progress—beyond the Internet and, no, not technology, but genuine progress in the way we live together. Some genuine progress has occurred. And one example is certainly the fact that slavery was taken for granted by everyone for such a long time, whereas today we take for granted that it is unacceptable. Thank you.
