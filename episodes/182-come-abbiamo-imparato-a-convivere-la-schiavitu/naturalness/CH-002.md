## 2. Rome in 61 CE and the murder of Pedanius Secundus

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00094–U-00175 -->

Tacitus tells the first story. We're in the Roman Empire in AD 61, during Nero's reign. That year, a major crime rocks Rome: Pedanius Secundus, the city prefect, is murdered at home by one of his slaves. The city prefect is roughly equivalent to a modern interior minister, all right? He's a top official and politician, one of the most powerful men in Rome. And one of his own slaves kills him in his bed.

Why does he do it? Tacitus isn't especially interested. He gives us two possible motives, though, and both tell us something about how this world works. One story is that the slave has arranged to buy his freedom. His master agrees, then changes his mind.

How can a slave buy his own freedom? It's a paradox, but the history of slavery is full of paradoxes. A Roman slave belongs to his master, who can do whatever he wants to him, even kill him, without consequences. Anything the slave owns therefore belongs to the master as well. And yet many masters let their slaves run a small business, provided the master gets a cut. The slave can save the rest. Legally, the master can take that money too. But a respectable master lets the slave build up a *peculium*—that is the actual Roman term—and, with the master's permission, eventually use it to buy himself.

An enslaved person may also have free relatives. Roman masters often free loyal slaves, men and women they've even grown fond of, while leaving those people's brothers, sisters, or children enslaved. In this case, the slave's father may be free and may be trying to buy his son. The master says yes, then backs out.

But another possibility comes up at the trial, and Tacitus mentions that one too. He covers all this in two lines; I'm the one turning it into a long story. Perhaps the slave is in love with a young male prostitute. His master wants the same young man and has taken him away. That gives us a glimpse of the extraordinary sexual freedom of the Roman world, especially among the ruling class. Nearly everyone is, or can quite easily be, bisexual. Male prostitution is as common as female prostitution. No one is shocked. It's all considered perfectly normal.

So perhaps that's what happens: the prefect, Pedanius Secundus, wants this young prostitute all to himself. His slave is in love with the same young man. Master and slave become rivals, and the slave ends up killing his master.

But that's not why the story matters. Roman law says that if a master is killed at home, every slave living under that roof must be crucified. It makes no difference whether the killer is known. The principle is that slaves must understand that protecting their master's life is in their own interest. If they fail, they all pay. Guilt or innocence doesn't matter.

Pedanius Secundus has four hundred slaves in his household. Four hundred. Men and women, old people, boys and girls, pregnant women. And the law says they must all be crucified. When word spreads through Rome, the people rise up. They fill the streets and squares, protesting and trying to stop the execution.

Why? Because in a slave society, enslaved people are everywhere, and their lives aren't sealed off from everyone else's. You pass slaves in the street and can't tell them apart from anyone else. They work as laborers or craftsmen, or run a small shop for their master. They form relationships. For ordinary Romans, it's perfectly normal to have friends, or even relatives, who belong to a wealthy owner. To the common people, slaves are people like them. The threat of a mass execution throws the city into turmoil, and the Senate has to decide what to do.
