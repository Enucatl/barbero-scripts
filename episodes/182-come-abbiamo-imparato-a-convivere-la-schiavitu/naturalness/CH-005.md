## 5. The Atlantic crossing and Carli's moral accommodation to the slave trade

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00344–U-00477 -->

On one of his journeys, Carli boards a ship from Africa to Brazil. He sails from Angola, a Portuguese colony, on a vessel carrying passengers, cargo, and 630 enslaved people. [N-003] He recounts all this with perfect calm, as a series of interesting details. Following custom, every captive is branded with a hot iron—some on one shoulder, some on the right or left arm, and some above the breast. Each carries the mark of an owner.

Then he describes how they're packed onto the ship. The men are crammed into the hold at the very bottom, locked in near-darkness. If they could move around, he says, they might cause trouble. They might set the ship on fire or throw themselves overboard. In fact, he says, these Moors being taken to the Americas drown themselves whenever they get the chance.

And why do they do it? Because, Carli says, these Moors are truly stupid. They superstitiously believe that the Portuguese are taking them to the Americas to kill them and make oil from their bodies. They would rather die before they arrive. Our good friar explains that this isn't true. It's completely false, he tells them. They can stop worrying; no one will kill them when they arrive. But they're too stubborn to listen. He sees a Black woman owned by the ship's pilot, who is allowed to move around freely, throw herself into the sea. The pilot catches her by one foot and pulls her back aboard. Such is the obstinacy of these Ethiopians, the friar says. “Ethiopians” is another word he uses for Black people.

The adult men are crammed into the hold. The women are on the second deck. The pregnant women—forty of the six hundred people who board—are put in the stern cabin, where they have a little more room. These are societies where children are constantly being born and just as easily dying, where a large share of women are pregnant at any given time. We can hardly picture it now. The children and young people are packed onto the deck just below the main deck. They lie on top of one another and relieve themselves where they are. The stench, Carli says, is unbearable.

The ship is already overloaded when another seventy Moors arrive. That creates a problem. Portuguese law—the priorities of every age are different—allows the slave trade without hesitation, but anyone taken from Africa to the Americas must first be baptized. The captives have to learn the catechism, convert to Christianity, and receive baptism. Otherwise, putting them aboard is illegal. These seventy haven't been baptized yet.

So the friar gets to work. Who knows how he manages it, in what language, or by what method, but he teaches them Christianity and baptizes all seventy. “There is excommunication for taking Africans to Brazil, or elsewhere, unless they have first been baptized ... I also baptized those children who were born from time to time to the pregnant women.” Once they're baptized, they're branded and put aboard.

The voyage gets off to a bad start. There's no wind, and the ship can't move. Why? Why would that happen? It's very unusual in that season. Something must be wrong. Why isn't God sending wind? Are they absolutely sure every Moor aboard has been baptized? Carli asks the captain. Ah, yes, the captain says, now I remember. I brought four more aboard at the last minute, and they haven't been baptized.

The friar has them brought up immediately. He instructs them as best he can and baptizes them, along with three newborn babies who, as he puts it, are still steaming.

Still no wind. Now, this has nothing to do with slavery, but it gives us a wonderful glimpse into the minds of seventeenth-century sailors. The Portuguese crew takes its statue of Saint Anthony of Padua and ties it to the mainmast. Then the sailors kneel before it, beat their breasts, and tell the saint: You're staying right here until you get God to send us a good wind.

The friar disapproves. Tying up a saint isn't entirely proper. They should bring the statue up and pray to it without the violence of lashing it to the mast. Still, it works. The wind comes, the ship starts moving, and the saint has granted their request.

After more trouble, the voyage finally ends. Carli reports that thirty-three people are missing when the ship reaches Brazil. Everyone is delighted, he says. It's God's grace that so few have died. Usually half die, and sometimes more.

Carli says these voyages come with every problem imaginable. Slave voyages are the most painful and distressing in the world—not so much because the captives suffer, but because the whole operation is so badly organized. The ships are always overloaded. Every owner insists on sending his slaves now: the next ship doesn't leave for a month, and I can't possibly wait. But the ship also needs food and water. These aren't crates of sugar—a typical American product, right? They're living people. They eat and drink, so someone does have to feed them and give them water.

Eventually the captain announces that the delays have left them short of food. Carli tells him: Look, an acquaintance of mine has crates in the cargo hold, and I know there's food inside. Open them. I'll take responsibility. That gives us enough to keep the white people alive, he says. If the Moors die, we'll simply have to accept it.

They check the water and find plenty. The friar is delighted. Very well, he says. The Moors can live on water for two days, and in the meantime the Lord will help us.

Meanwhile, word spreads that the food is gone. The Black captives in the hold become agitated. They shout and pound on the walls. Yes, yes, Carli says, poor things. I understand. We let them get it out of their systems. We must trust in God and the Madonna.

Then the friar slips in one small detail. He is deeply upset to learn that the sailors have nicknamed the whip they use on the slaves *Maria*. You named the whip after the Madonna? he asks. How can you expect them to believe what we teach them about Christianity? They'll think we're mocking them.

You see, we have to work hard not to recoil from this friar. To him, what truly matters is saving their souls. Their lives matter only so much; the next life is what counts. And he believes, with absolute sincerity, that they're saving these people's souls.

They pray day and night, and all the white people confess as if they're about to die. Then a sailor suggests killing all the Moors to make sure there's enough food. Here the friar draws the line. It's a diabolical proposal, he says. You must never kill one person so another can live. If he weren't there, the crew might even do it. But he is there, and he won't allow it.

And here—though I promised not to comment—perhaps no comment is needed. We can simply notice what living in a society where slavery is normal seems to do to the moral sense. People stop seeing what looks obvious to us. Are we running a little late? Then we'd better speed up. The third story.
