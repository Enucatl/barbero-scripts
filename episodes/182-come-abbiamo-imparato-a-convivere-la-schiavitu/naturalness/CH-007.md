## 7. Harriet Jacobs, abolitionist testimony, and childhood in slavery

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00648–U-00753 -->

Our final story isn't about an enslaved man, but an enslaved woman. We know her full name, and this time she tells us her story herself in a book. Several formerly enslaved people publish their stories in the nineteenth-century United States, and hers becomes one of the most famous. Her name is Harriet Jacobs. She's born in North Carolina in 1813. [N-006]

As you know, slavery once exists throughout the United States. During the eighteenth century, Northern states begin outlawing it. The South keeps it because its economy depends on cotton and tobacco, and therefore on a huge enslaved workforce. Slavery remains there until the Civil War ends in 1865.

Harriet is born enslaved in North Carolina in 1813 and dies in 1897, after spending most of her life free. She's the first Black woman born into slavery to write her autobiography. *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself.* appears in 1861, just as the Civil War is about to begin.

Harriet writes with help from friends and contacts in the Northern abolitionist movement. Her intended audience is Northern public opinion. The North has organized movements against slavery, but Northerners are by no means united. Plenty of them simply don't care that slavery exists.

Harriet has a very specific goal. She doesn't just denounce the horrors of slavery in general, though those horrors are certainly in the book. She tells the story from a woman's point of view, speaking especially to the white women who will read it. She wants to show how slavery—and especially the enslavement of women—corrupts white slaveholding society.

It's a delicate subject. This is the Victorian era, when sex is almost impossible to discuss in public, yet sex is precisely what she needs to discuss. Her epigraph says that Northerners know nothing about slavery or the depths of its degradation. Then she writes: “I do earnestly desire to arouse the women of the North to a realizing sense of the condition of two millions of women at the South, still in bondage, suffering what I suffered, and most of them far worse.”

Harriet's parents are both enslaved. Everyone in the family has fairly light skin, and the reason keeps surfacing in the story. If we could reconstruct their family tree, we would clearly find more than one white master who impregnated an enslaved Black woman. So they're descended from white people, considered Black, and held as slaves. Yet the family lives fairly well and is reasonably happy.

Her father is a carpenter. His owners let him run his own workshop in exchange for an annual payment, and otherwise he lives his own life. Harriet says she was born a slave but didn't know it until she was six. Her childhood is happy. She understands none of this; her life simply feels normal.

Her father saves money from his carpentry, much like the Roman slaves we met at the beginning. He hopes to buy his children before buying his own freedom. Yes, an enslaved man can own slaves. It's an insane world, as you'll see, but he can legally buy and own other people. He wants to buy his children and free them. Their mistress never agrees to sell.

Their mistress is considered a good woman, and she grows up very close to Harriet's mother. The two are milk sisters. Harriet's grandmother nurses both her own baby and the infant mistress. But she has to wean her own daughter, Harriet's mother, at three months so there's enough milk for the white child.

The two girls grow up playing together. Harriet's mother later dies, and Harriet remains with the same mistress. The woman treats her extremely well—almost like a daughter, Harriet says—and teaches her to read and write. As Harriet's mother lies dying, the mistress promises that her children will never have anything to fear. Everyone assumes she'll free them in her will.

But when this “good” mistress dies, the woman who treated Harriet like a daughter never thinks to free her. She leaves Harriet to a young niece, a child who now becomes Harriet's owner. The executors sell other family members, including Harriet's uncles, at auction.

Harriet moves into the home of the little girl's father, Dr. Flint, a physician in a small North Carolina town. It's an ordinary middle-class household. Harriet is twelve when her mistress dies and she enters this new home. As I said, the paradoxes never end.

Harriet's mother is dead, but her grandmother is still alive. She's an older enslaved Black woman, well known and loved throughout the town. Everyone expects the mistress to free her, but she doesn't. Dr. Flint, one of the executors, decides to put her up for auction too. The town rebels.

Legally, the elderly woman everyone calls Aunt Martha is a slave. But the town's white residents consider selling her a disgrace. No one bids. The price falls and falls until the dead mistress's sister steps forward, buys Aunt Martha, and sets her free.

So Harriet remains enslaved in Dr. Flint's household while her grandmother, now free at about fifty, lives nearby in her own home. She sews for local women, makes a decent living, and saves money to buy her children. Eventually she manages to buy one of them. She has saved eight hundred dollars—an enormous sum in the United States of the 1820s and 1830s—and uses it to buy her son and set him free.
