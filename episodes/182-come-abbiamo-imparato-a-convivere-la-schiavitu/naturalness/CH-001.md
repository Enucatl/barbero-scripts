## 1. Slavery as a historical norm and the distinction between slaveholding and slave societies

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00093 -->

So, slavery. It's a huge subject, because until fairly recently, almost every human society has had slaves. In Europe, it isn't until the Enlightenment in the eighteenth century that people begin to consider an idea that had never occurred to the Greeks, brilliant as they were, or to the Romans, or even to Christians: that slavery is inherently wrong, unjust, and shameful. Before then, no one thinks that way. And slavery doesn't finally disappear from the West—from the United States and South America—until the second half of the nineteenth century. In other words, we've lived in a world without legal slavery in the West for only about a hundred or a hundred and fifty years. Before that, slavery is everywhere for thousands of years.

And I mean everywhere, even in places and periods where we might not expect it. We all know that ancient Rome has slaves. But we don't usually picture slaves in the Florence of Lorenzo the Magnificent. They're there, though, because no one has ever formally abolished slavery. I won't say every city in Renaissance Italy is full of slaves, but some really are. In the homes of the rich—princes, but also merchants—it's perfectly normal to find enslaved men and women.

Why? Because the Christian and Muslim worlds are constantly at war, and the Mediterranean is a battlefield. Christian forces board Muslim ships and capture everyone aboard—not just the crew, but civilians, merchants, and passengers. Muslims do the same to Christians. The captives are sold to anyone who wants an enslaved man, or more often an enslaved woman, for the household. Christian galleys also raid the North African coast, just as Barbary pirates raid the Italian coast. But memory is selective. We remember very well that Barbary pirates constantly attack Renaissance Italy. We tend to forget that our side is doing exactly the same thing across the sea.

Because slavery is so widespread, historians make a crucial distinction between societies with slaves and slave societies. What's the difference? A slave society is built on slavery. Without enslaved labor, it either couldn't function at all or would have to function very differently. Ancient Greece and, for a time, the Roman Empire are slave societies. So are Georgia, Alabama, Virginia, and Texas before the American Civil War, along with Caribbean colonies such as Haiti, Santo Domingo, and Jamaica under French or English planters. In all of them, slavery is essential to the system.

Renaissance Italy isn't like that. You don't see slaves everywhere. Rich people may have one or two at home, but without them, very little would change. That's the distinction. Today I'm going to focus on slave societies: places where enslaved people are everywhere, where their numbers are enormous, and where the system depends on them. If I can fit it all into an hour, I'll tell you four stories. They certainly don't add up to a complete history of slave societies. But taken together, they may give us a clearer sense of what it means to live in a world built on slavery.
