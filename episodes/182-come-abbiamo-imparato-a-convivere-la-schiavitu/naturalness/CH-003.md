## 3. Cassius Longinus, collective punishment, and the execution of four hundred slaves

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00176–U-00276 -->

As I said, we're living under the Empire, but the Senate still exists and still wields enormous power. It has to contend with the emperor, a dictator with the power of life and death. But the Senate brings together the three hundred, four hundred, or five hundred richest men in the Empire. That counts for something.

The senators debate what to do. Some argue that enforcing the law in this particular case may be going too far. But Tacitus cares about one thing: the speech I'm about to recount. A leading senator named Cassius Longinus stands up, and Tacitus gives us his speech word for word.

Cassius Longinus tells his fellow senators: You know me. You've heard me speak in this chamber when someone proposed a new law or tried to change an old one, and I haven't stood in the way. Not because I approve of changing old laws. That's always wrong, and it weakens our authority. But I didn't want to seem prejudiced, as if I opposed every change on principle. This time, though, we cannot refuse to enforce the law and then change it. We cannot.

A man of consular rank, someone at the very top of the imperial aristocracy, has been murdered at home in a slave conspiracy. No one stopped it. No one reported it, even though everyone knew the law would condemn them all to death. And now you want to abolish that law? By Hercules, you might as well declare open season on masters. Let them be murdered in their own homes. What good is high rank if being city prefect couldn't protect Pedanius Secundus? What good is a large household if four hundred slaves weren't enough to protect him?

People say—and this is where Tacitus gets his information—that perhaps the killer was simply avenging a wrong. Maybe his master had promised to free him and then refused, or had taken a young male slave away from him. Very well. In that case, let's rule that the master deserved to die.

Longinus continues. The fact that our ancestors created this law proves that it's just and must not be changed. But let's pretend we're debating it for the first time. Do you really believe a slave decided to kill his master without ever letting a threat slip, without ever saying anything suspicious? No one heard him? Fine, let's say no one did. Let's say he prepared the weapon while everyone around him noticed nothing. But how did he pass through the bedrooms, bring in a lamp, and commit the murder without anyone seeing him?

Our ancestors understood that our safety depended on slaves knowing they had to report every plot and every danger. And they passed this law when slaves were born here, among people who had known them from birth. But Rome's victories have expanded the Empire enormously, and every war has brought millions of slaves in from distant lands. Now our slaves are foreigners, Longinus says. They follow different rites and an alien religion, or perhaps no religion at all. The only way to keep this vast population under control is fear.

And innocent people will die? So be it, Longinus says. Great punishments carried out for the public good always include some injustice. He points to another Roman practice, rarely used but still available: decimating a military unit that has disgraced itself in battle. If a unit acts cowardly before the enemy, its commander can line up the men, unarmed and surrounded by the rest of the army, which remains armed. They draw lots, and one man in ten is beaten to death. Whoever draws the lot dies. He may be a decorated veteran. He may be the one man who didn't run that day. It doesn't matter. This case is no different, Longinus says.

Some senators object: but the women, but the children. It gets them nowhere. The majority sides with Longinus and votes to carry out the sentence. But the people are rioting. Nero has to step in, send troops into the streets, block the roads, and clear a route. The four hundred prisoners are marched through and crucified.

Then someone proposes expelling the dead man's freed slaves from Italy as well—the people Pedanius had freed during his lifetime. At least there, Nero objects. The traditional law is harsh enough, he says; there's no need to make it even crueler. But the law itself is enforced.
