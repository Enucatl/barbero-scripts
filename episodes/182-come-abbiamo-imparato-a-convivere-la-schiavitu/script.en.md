# Come abbiamo imparato a convivere: la schiavitù — faithful English translation

## 1. Slavery as a historical norm and the distinction between slaveholding and slave societies

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00093 -->

So, slavery. It's a huge subject, because until fairly recently, almost every human society has had slaves. In Europe, it isn't until the Enlightenment in the eighteenth century that people begin to consider an idea that had never occurred to the Greeks, brilliant as they were, or to the Romans, or even to Christians: that slavery is inherently wrong, unjust, and shameful. Before then, no one thinks that way. And slavery doesn't finally disappear from the West—from the United States and South America—until the second half of the nineteenth century. In other words, we've lived in a world without legal slavery in the West for only about a hundred or a hundred and fifty years. Before that, slavery is everywhere for thousands of years.

And I mean everywhere, even in places and periods where we might not expect it. We all know that ancient Rome has slaves. But we don't usually picture slaves in the Florence of Lorenzo the Magnificent. They're there, though, because no one has ever formally abolished slavery. I won't say every city in Renaissance Italy is full of slaves, but some really are. In the homes of the rich—princes, but also merchants—it's perfectly normal to find enslaved men and women.

Why? Because the Christian and Muslim worlds are constantly at war, and the Mediterranean is a battlefield. Christian forces board Muslim ships and capture everyone aboard—not just the crew, but civilians, merchants, and passengers. Muslims do the same to Christians. The captives are sold to anyone who wants an enslaved man, or more often an enslaved woman, for the household. Christian galleys also raid the North African coast, just as Barbary pirates raid the Italian coast. But memory is selective. We remember very well that Barbary pirates constantly attack Renaissance Italy. We tend to forget that our side is doing exactly the same thing across the sea.

Because slavery is so widespread, historians make a crucial distinction between societies with slaves and slave societies. What's the difference? A slave society is built on slavery. Without enslaved labor, it either couldn't function at all or would have to function very differently. Ancient Greece and, for a time, the Roman Empire are slave societies. So are Georgia, Alabama, Virginia, and Texas before the American Civil War, along with Caribbean colonies such as Haiti, Santo Domingo, and Jamaica under French or English planters. In all of them, slavery is essential to the system.

Renaissance Italy isn't like that. You don't see slaves everywhere. Rich people may have one or two at home, but without them, very little would change. That's the distinction. Today I'm going to focus on slave societies: places where enslaved people are everywhere, where their numbers are enormous, and where the system depends on them. If I can fit it all into an hour, I'll tell you four stories. They certainly don't add up to a complete history of slave societies. But taken together, they may give us a clearer sense of what it means to live in a world built on slavery.

## 2. Rome in 61 CE and the murder of Pedanius Secundus

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00094–U-00175 -->

Tacitus tells the first story. We're in the Roman Empire in AD 61, during Nero's reign. That year, a major crime rocks Rome: Pedanius Secundus, the city prefect, is murdered at home by one of his slaves. The city prefect is roughly equivalent to a modern interior minister, all right? He's a top official and politician, one of the most powerful men in Rome. And one of his own slaves kills him in his bed.

Why does he do it? Tacitus isn't especially interested. He gives us two possible motives, though, and both tell us something about how this world works. One story is that the slave has arranged to buy his freedom. His master agrees, then changes his mind.

How can a slave buy his own freedom? It's a paradox, but the history of slavery is full of paradoxes. A Roman slave belongs to his master, who can do whatever he wants to him, even kill him, without consequences. Anything the slave owns therefore belongs to the master as well. And yet many masters let their slaves run a small business, provided the master gets a cut. The slave can save the rest. Legally, the master can take that money too. But a respectable master lets the slave build up a *peculium*—that is the actual Roman term—and, with the master's permission, eventually use it to buy himself.

An enslaved person may also have free relatives. Roman masters often free loyal slaves, men and women they've even grown fond of, while leaving those people's brothers, sisters, or children enslaved. In this case, the slave's father may be free and may be trying to buy his son. The master says yes, then backs out.

But another possibility comes up at the trial, and Tacitus mentions that one too. He covers all this in two lines; I'm the one turning it into a long story. Perhaps the slave is in love with a young male prostitute. His master wants the same young man and has taken him away. That gives us a glimpse of the extraordinary sexual freedom of the Roman world, especially among the ruling class. Nearly everyone is, or can quite easily be, bisexual. Male prostitution is as common as female prostitution. No one is shocked. It's all considered perfectly normal.

So perhaps that's what happens: the prefect, Pedanius Secundus, wants this young prostitute all to himself. His slave is in love with the same young man. Master and slave become rivals, and the slave ends up killing his master.

But that's not why the story matters. Roman law says that if a master is killed at home, every slave living under that roof must be crucified. It makes no difference whether the killer is known. The principle is that slaves must understand that protecting their master's life is in their own interest. If they fail, they all pay. Guilt or innocence doesn't matter.

Pedanius Secundus has four hundred slaves in his household. Four hundred. Men and women, old people, boys and girls, pregnant women. And the law says they must all be crucified. When word spreads through Rome, the people rise up. They fill the streets and squares, protesting and trying to stop the execution.

Why? Because in a slave society, enslaved people are everywhere, and their lives aren't sealed off from everyone else's. You pass slaves in the street and can't tell them apart from anyone else. They work as laborers or craftsmen, or run a small shop for their master. They form relationships. For ordinary Romans, it's perfectly normal to have friends, or even relatives, who belong to a wealthy owner. To the common people, slaves are people like them. The threat of a mass execution throws the city into turmoil, and the Senate has to decide what to do.

## 3. Cassius Longinus, collective punishment, and the execution of four hundred slaves

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00176–U-00276 -->

As I said, we're living under the Empire, but the Senate still exists and still wields enormous power. It has to contend with the emperor, a dictator with the power of life and death. But the Senate brings together the three hundred, four hundred, or five hundred richest men in the Empire. That counts for something.

The senators debate what to do. Some argue that enforcing the law in this particular case may be going too far. But Tacitus cares about one thing: the speech I'm about to recount. A leading senator named Cassius Longinus stands up, and Tacitus gives us his speech word for word.

Cassius Longinus tells his fellow senators: You know me. You've heard me speak in this chamber when someone proposed a new law or tried to change an old one, and I haven't stood in the way. Not because I approve of changing old laws. That's always wrong, and it weakens our authority. But I didn't want to seem prejudiced, as if I opposed every change on principle. This time, though, we cannot refuse to enforce the law and then change it. We cannot.

A man of consular rank, someone at the very top of the imperial aristocracy, has been murdered at home in a slave conspiracy. No one stopped it. No one reported it, even though everyone knew the law would condemn them all to death. And now you want to abolish that law? By Hercules, you might as well declare open season on masters. Let them be murdered in their own homes. What good is high rank if being city prefect couldn't protect Pedanius Secundus? What good is a large household if four hundred slaves weren't enough to protect him?

People say—and this is where Tacitus gets his information—that perhaps the killer was simply avenging a wrong. Maybe his master had promised to free him and then refused, or had taken a young male slave away from him. Very well. In that case, let's rule that the master deserved to die.

Longinus continues. The fact that our ancestors created this law proves that it's just and must not be changed. But let's pretend we're debating it for the first time. Do you really believe a slave decided to kill his master without ever letting a threat slip, without ever saying anything suspicious? No one heard him? Fine, let's say no one did. Let's say he prepared the weapon while everyone around him noticed nothing. But how did he pass through the bedrooms, bring in a lamp, and commit the murder without anyone seeing him?

Our ancestors understood that our safety depended on slaves knowing they had to report every plot and every danger. And they passed this law when slaves were born here, among people who had known them from birth. But Rome's victories have expanded the Empire enormously, and every war has brought millions of slaves in from distant lands. Now our slaves are foreigners, Longinus says. They follow different rites and an alien religion, or perhaps no religion at all. The only way to keep this vast population under control is fear.

And innocent people will die? So be it, Longinus says. Great punishments carried out for the public good always include some injustice. He points to another Roman practice, rarely used but still available: decimating a military unit that has disgraced itself in battle. If a unit acts cowardly before the enemy, its commander can line up the men, unarmed and surrounded by the rest of the army, which remains armed. They draw lots, and one man in ten is beaten to death. Whoever draws the lot dies. He may be a decorated veteran. He may be the one man who didn't run that day. It doesn't matter. This case is no different, Longinus says.

Some senators object: but the women, but the children. It gets them nowhere. The majority sides with Longinus and votes to carry out the sentence. But the people are rioting. Nero has to step in, send troops into the streets, block the roads, and clear a route. The four hundred prisoners are marched through and crucified.

Then someone proposes expelling the dead man's freed slaves from Italy as well—the people Pedanius had freed during his lifetime. At least there, Nero objects. The traditional law is harsh enough, he says; there's no need to make it even crueler. But the law itself is enforced.

## 4. Dionigi Carli encounters plantation slavery in seventeenth-century Brazil

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00277–U-00343 -->

That's the first story. I don't think I'll comment on these stories, because they speak for themselves. The second brings us much closer to the present, to the seventeenth century. What happens in between? The Roman Empire falls, and slavery gradually fades away in our part of the world. No one abolishes it, but it no longer plays a major structural role in medieval or Renaissance society. The slaves you do find are domestic servants in wealthy merchants' homes—perhaps a woman captured at sea and bought from privateers. That's what slavery looks like.

Then Europeans colonize the Americas, and slavery suddenly becomes essential again. It happens first in the Spanish and Portuguese colonies of Central and South America, especially Brazil, and later in English North America. Brazil is an enormous country that promises enormous profits. What do the colonists grow? Sugarcane, then tobacco. European landowners quickly realize that these crops demand a vast amount of labor. Epidemics, war, exploitation, and forced labor devastate Indigenous populations, while expanding plantations lead colonists to import more and more enslaved Africans. [N-002]

By the end of the fifteenth century, Europeans are becoming more familiar with Africa and discovering how easy it is to enslave people there. The trafficking of enslaved Black people to the Americas begins. It continues for centuries and brings a vast Black population to the Americas.

The man in our next story is Dionigi Carli of Piacenza, a Capuchin missionary. He's a deeply religious friar who devotes his life to missionary work, traveling between Africa and Brazil. When this Italian friar reaches Brazil, he encounters Black slavery on a mass scale for the first time. Back home, a duchess might still have a young Moorish page someone gave her, but slavery is no longer very visible. In the Americas, Carli sees it everywhere.

Carli writes a book about his travels. In Brazil, he tells his readers, you see these “Moors,” as he calls Black people, everywhere. “We then saw many others, four, six, or eight at a time, chained—some by the neck, some by the hands, some by the feet—a salutary medicine, for without it they would all flee together.”

At first, he says, he feels sorry for them. It seems cruel. But then he learns what sort of people these Moors are: people who won't work unless they're forced. So he comes to understand that this is the only way. There's no alternative.

He's a thoroughly decent man. He feels a little compassion at first, but this is the system. Everyone tells him, of course that's how things work; they couldn't work any other way. It takes him almost no time to agree. Well, yes, of course. This is how it has to be.

They take him to see sugarcane being processed. Enslaved workers strain to turn the crushing mill while overseers stand by with whips. Yes, he says, the poor things. You can't help feeling sorry when you see them whipped. But the whip is necessary medicine, because otherwise they won't do the work. I saw them in Africa, he says. They know how to do nothing but dance. So if you want Black people to work, of course you need a whip.

## 5. The Atlantic crossing and Carli's moral accommodation to the slave trade

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00344–U-00477 -->

On one of his journeys, Carli boards a ship from Africa to Brazil. He sails from Angola, a Portuguese colony, on a vessel carrying passengers, cargo, and 630 enslaved people. [N-003] He recounts all this with perfect calm, as a series of interesting details. Following custom, every captive is branded with a hot iron—some on one shoulder, some on the right or left arm, and some above the breast. Each carries the mark of an owner.

Then he describes how they're packed onto the ship. The men are crammed into the hold at the very bottom, locked in near-darkness. If they could move around, he says, they might cause trouble. They might set the ship on fire or throw themselves overboard. In fact, he says, these Moors being taken to the Americas drown themselves whenever they get the chance.

And why do they do it? Because, Carli says, these Moors are truly stupid. They superstitiously believe that the Portuguese are taking them to the Americas to kill them and make oil from their bodies. They would rather die before they arrive. Our good friar explains that this isn't true. It's completely false, he tells them. They can stop worrying; no one will kill them when they arrive. But they're too stubborn to listen. He sees a Black woman owned by the ship's pilot, who is allowed to move around freely, throw herself into the sea. The pilot catches her by one foot and pulls her back aboard. Such is the obstinacy of these Ethiopians, the friar says. “Ethiopians” is another word he uses for Black people.

The adult men are crammed into the hold. The women are on the second deck. The pregnant women—forty of the six hundred people who board—are put in the stern cabin, where they have a little more room. These are societies where children are constantly being born and just as easily dying, where a large share of women are pregnant at any given time. We can hardly picture it now. The children and young people are packed onto the deck just below the main deck. They lie on top of one another and relieve themselves where they are. The stench, Carli says, is unbearable.

The ship is already overloaded when another seventy Moors arrive. That creates a problem. Portuguese law—the priorities of every age are different—allows the slave trade without hesitation, but anyone taken from Africa to the Americas must first be baptized. The captives have to learn the catechism, convert to Christianity, and receive baptism. Otherwise, putting them aboard is illegal. These seventy haven't been baptized yet.

So the friar gets to work. Who knows how he manages it, in what language, or by what method, but he teaches them Christianity and baptizes all seventy. “There is excommunication for taking Africans to Brazil, or elsewhere, unless they have first been baptized ... I also baptized those children who were born from time to time to the pregnant women.” Once they're baptized, they're branded and put aboard.

The voyage gets off to a bad start. There's no wind, and the ship can't move. Why? Why would that happen? It's very unusual in that season. Something must be wrong. Why isn't God sending wind? Are they absolutely sure every Moor aboard has been baptized? Carli asks the captain. Ah, yes, the captain says, now I remember. I brought four more aboard at the last minute, and they haven't been baptized.

The friar has them brought up immediately. He instructs them as best he can and baptizes them, along with three newborn babies who, as he puts it, are still steaming.

Still no wind. Now, this has nothing to do with slavery, but it gives us a wonderful glimpse into the minds of seventeenth-century sailors. The Portuguese crew takes its statue of Saint Anthony of Padua and ties it to the mainmast. Then the sailors kneel before it, beat their breasts, and tell the saint: You're staying right here until you get God to send us a good wind.

The friar disapproves. Tying up a saint isn't entirely proper. They should bring the statue up and pray to it without the violence of lashing it to the mast. Still, it works. The wind comes, the ship starts moving, and the saint has granted their request.

After more trouble, the voyage finally ends. Carli reports that thirty-three people are missing when the ship reaches Brazil. Everyone is delighted, he says. It's God's grace that so few have died. Usually half die, and sometimes more.

Carli says these voyages come with every problem imaginable. Slave voyages are the most painful and distressing in the world—not so much because the captives suffer, but because the whole operation is so badly organized. The ships are always overloaded. Every owner insists on sending his slaves now: the next ship doesn't leave for a month, and I can't possibly wait. But the ship also needs food and water. These aren't crates of sugar—a typical American product, right? They're living people. They eat and drink, so someone does have to feed them and give them water.

Eventually the captain announces that the delays have left them short of food. Carli tells him: Look, an acquaintance of mine has crates in the cargo hold, and I know there's food inside. Open them. I'll take responsibility. That gives us enough to keep the white people alive, he says. If the Moors die, we'll simply have to accept it.

They check the water and find plenty. The friar is delighted. Very well, he says. The Moors can live on water for two days, and in the meantime the Lord will help us.

Meanwhile, word spreads that the food is gone. The Black captives in the hold become agitated. They shout and pound on the walls. Yes, yes, Carli says, poor things. I understand. We let them get it out of their systems. We must trust in God and the Madonna.

Then the friar slips in one small detail. He is deeply upset to learn that the sailors have nicknamed the whip they use on the slaves *Maria*. You named the whip after the Madonna? he asks. How can you expect them to believe what we teach them about Christianity? They'll think we're mocking them.

You see, we have to work hard not to recoil from this friar. To him, what truly matters is saving their souls. Their lives matter only so much; the next life is what counts. And he believes, with absolute sincerity, that they're saving these people's souls.

They pray day and night, and all the white people confess as if they're about to die. Then a sailor suggests killing all the Moors to make sure there's enough food. Here the friar draws the line. It's a diabolical proposal, he says. You must never kill one person so another can live. If he weren't there, the crew might even do it. But he is there, and he won't allow it.

And here—though I promised not to comment—perhaps no comment is needed. We can simply notice what living in a society where slavery is normal seems to do to the moral sense. People stop seeing what looks obvious to us. Are we running a little late? Then we'd better speed up. The third story.

## 6. Joseph Knight and the judicial rejection of slavery in Scotland

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00478–U-00647 -->

The third story moves us forward to the eighteenth century, when more and more Europeans begin to say that slavery is wrong, unjust, and unlawful. It's the story of a man born in Africa, captured, and sold in Jamaica, a British colony whose vast sugarcane plantations produce sugar and rum.

His owners name him Joseph Knight—Knight with a K. In Jamaica, he belongs to Sir John Wedderburn, an extremely wealthy Scottish planter. [N-004] Wedderburn buys Joseph, grows fond of him, and makes him his personal servant. You understand how much an enslaved person's fate can vary. One man spends all day turning a crushing mill under the whip. Another serves the master personally, lives in the house, dresses well, and eats well, with everything that comes with that position.

In 1769, after several years, Sir John returns to Scotland and brings Joseph with him. Then something happens in Sir John's household that we might not expect. First, Sir John has Joseph baptized. Protestants are apparently less particular than Portuguese Catholics; no law requires them to baptize their slaves. But Sir John does have his cherished servant baptized.

Then something happens that I would never have thought possible, though the sources treat it as perfectly ordinary. Joseph falls in love with Ann Thompson, a servant in the household. She's Scottish, and therefore white. They marry with Sir John's permission, and he even pays for the wedding. They have a child. Sir John pays for the baptism and, when the child dies, for the burial.

Joseph now wants to live with Ann in a small home of their own while continuing to serve Sir John. Until then, they both live in the main house. Remember *The Marriage of Figaro*? Figaro and Susanna serve the count and countess, so they live under their roof. Joseph and Ann want a place of their own.

We don't know exactly why, but Joseph and Sir John quarrel over the plan. Sir John refuses and fires Ann, who is a free woman in his employ. Joseph responds by walking out and joining his wife.

Now Sir John remembers that Joseph is his slave. He sends the police to seize him and bring him back, then announces that he's returning to Jamaica and taking Joseph along.

And now we reach the most remarkable part of the story, the part that gives it historical importance. Joseph has clearly received advice and made friends and connections. He sues Sir John in the Scottish courts, arguing that his master has no right to take him back to Jamaica.

What's the legal situation? In Britain, it's deeply ambiguous. There are almost no slaves in the country, except when wealthy planters return from the West Indies and bring enslaved servants with them. English courts have already heard several cases like this and ruled—and I translate—“As soon as a negro comes into England, he becomes free; one may be a villein in England, but not a slave.” The law therefore protects his person and his property.

But these are rulings by individual judges, not laws passed by Parliament. Later judges can cite them or contradict them. And they're English rulings. Joseph is in Scotland. England and Scotland aren't just separate nations; their deepest difference may be their legal systems. Scottish law is entirely distinct from English law.

In 1774, American colonists are demanding liberty and equality. They're preparing to proclaim that all men are equal, even though many of them—including George Washington—are slave owners. That same year, Joseph Knight brings his case before the justices of the peace in Perth. Slavery doesn't exist in Scotland, he argues, so even if Sir John bought him legally in Jamaica, he became free the moment he reached Scottish soil.

Sir John fights the suit, and the judges rule in his favor. His lawyer makes this argument: Think about the principle. It makes no difference whether we're talking about one Negro—I'm using their word—or twenty thousand. If an owner can't take one slave to Jamaica, then a slave trader can't take thousands there either. Are you saying it's illegal to transport slaves to Jamaica? No rational person could seriously defend such a ridiculous idea, Sir John's lawyers say. The “ridiculous idea” is that people can't be taken to Jamaica as slaves.

Joseph loses and appeals to the sheriff of Perth, a higher judge. This time, Joseph wins. Scottish law doesn't recognize slavery, the sheriff rules, and slavery is incompatible with the kingdom's legal principles. Jamaican slave laws don't apply here, so Sir John has no right to force Joseph to serve him. You see what's happening? In this age, judges are making the law. Parliament hasn't acted. The judges are interpreting public opinion, and public opinion is moving in that direction.

Sir John appeals to the supreme court in Edinburgh. Perhaps Scotland doesn't have slavery, he argues, but it does allow people to sign contracts binding themselves to a master for life. That's true. Domestic servants can enter such contracts, and apprentices often agree to serve a craftsman as long as the master wishes. Scottish law recognizes those arrangements. So, Sir John says, fine: Joseph isn't my slave. But he is legally bound to serve me.

Joseph's lawyers insist that this case is about slavery. For perhaps the first time in the proceedings, they explain in detail why the civilized world cannot tolerate it. Don't try to determine how this man was captured in Africa and taken to Jamaica, they say. We can't know. Jamaican law doesn't ask; it simply accepts the result. But whether Joseph was captured, kidnapped, or sold by his parents—which also happened quite routinely—the original injustice in Africa is the same.

Slavery doesn't become just because so many societies practiced it in the past. Some societies once accepted infanticide, Joseph's lawyers point out. That doesn't make infanticide any less vile. Nor do they dispute that Negro slavery benefits the country and generates enormous profits. Profit has nothing to do with justice.

Besides, enlightened writers now argue that slavery isn't even as profitable as people claim, and that cotton—no, sugar—and tobacco can be grown without it. But the central point is simpler: no advantage can justify oppression and injustice.

The supreme court in Edinburgh rejects Sir John's appeal and confirms that Joseph is free. Jamaican law may give Sir John dominion over this Negro, the court rules, but that dominion is unjust and cannot be enforced here in any form. Sir John has no right to Joseph's services for any length of time and cannot send him out of the country without his consent. The law protects Joseph from both abuses.

The case makes history. From that moment on, no one can be held as a slave on Scottish soil, just as similar rulings have produced the same result in England.

It would be wonderful to know what happens next to Joseph and his wife, Ann, but they disappear from the record. We have the court documents, and we know he's free. Presumably he stays in Scotland and works as a servant, or finds some other work, while living with his wife. Beyond that, we know nothing. What survives is the case, *Knight v. Wedderburn*.

I don't know whether you noticed the progression. We began with Tacitus and hundreds of slaves whose sex we don't even know. I called them men and women, but Tacitus doesn't. Our seventeenth-century Capuchin at least distinguishes adult men from women and notices a few individuals, though of course he never gives their names. Now, at last, we've met an enslaved man with a first and last name.

## 7. Harriet Jacobs, abolitionist testimony, and childhood in slavery

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00648–U-00753 -->

Our final story isn't about an enslaved man, but an enslaved woman. We know her full name, and this time she tells us her story herself in a book. Several formerly enslaved people publish their stories in the nineteenth-century United States, and hers becomes one of the most famous. Her name is Harriet Jacobs. She's born in North Carolina in 1813. [N-006]

As you know, slavery once exists throughout the United States. During the eighteenth century, Northern states begin outlawing it. The South keeps it because its economy depends on cotton and tobacco, and therefore on a huge enslaved workforce. Slavery remains there until the Civil War ends in 1865.

Harriet is born enslaved in North Carolina in 1813 and dies in 1897, after spending most of her life free. She's the first Black woman born into slavery to write her autobiography. *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself.* appears in 1861, just as the Civil War is about to begin.

Harriet writes with help from friends and contacts in the Northern abolitionist movement. Her intended audience is Northern public opinion. The North has organized movements against slavery, but Northerners are by no means united. Plenty of them simply don't care that slavery exists.

Harriet has a very specific goal. She doesn't just denounce the horrors of slavery in general, though those horrors are certainly in the book. She tells the story from a woman's point of view, speaking especially to the white women who will read it. She wants to show how slavery—and especially the enslavement of women—corrupts white slaveholding society.

It's a delicate subject. This is the Victorian era, when sex is almost impossible to discuss in public, yet sex is precisely what she needs to discuss. Her epigraph says that Northerners know nothing about slavery or the depths of its degradation. Then she writes: “I do earnestly desire to arouse the women of the North to a realizing sense of the condition of two millions of women at the South, still in bondage, suffering what I suffered, and most of them far worse.”

Harriet's parents are both enslaved. Everyone in the family has fairly light skin, and the reason keeps surfacing in the story. If we could reconstruct their family tree, we would clearly find more than one white master who impregnated an enslaved Black woman. So they're descended from white people, considered Black, and held as slaves. Yet the family lives fairly well and is reasonably happy.

Her father is a carpenter. His owners let him run his own workshop in exchange for an annual payment, and otherwise he lives his own life. Harriet says she was born a slave but didn't know it until she was six. Her childhood is happy. She understands none of this; her life simply feels normal.

Her father saves money from his carpentry, much like the Roman slaves we met at the beginning. He hopes to buy his children before buying his own freedom. Yes, an enslaved man can own slaves. It's an insane world, as you'll see, but he can legally buy and own other people. He wants to buy his children and free them. Their mistress never agrees to sell.

Their mistress is considered a good woman, and she grows up very close to Harriet's mother. The two are milk sisters. Harriet's grandmother nurses both her own baby and the infant mistress. But she has to wean her own daughter, Harriet's mother, at three months so there's enough milk for the white child.

The two girls grow up playing together. Harriet's mother later dies, and Harriet remains with the same mistress. The woman treats her extremely well—almost like a daughter, Harriet says—and teaches her to read and write. As Harriet's mother lies dying, the mistress promises that her children will never have anything to fear. Everyone assumes she'll free them in her will.

But when this “good” mistress dies, the woman who treated Harriet like a daughter never thinks to free her. She leaves Harriet to a young niece, a child who now becomes Harriet's owner. The executors sell other family members, including Harriet's uncles, at auction.

Harriet moves into the home of the little girl's father, Dr. Flint, a physician in a small North Carolina town. It's an ordinary middle-class household. Harriet is twelve when her mistress dies and she enters this new home. As I said, the paradoxes never end.

Harriet's mother is dead, but her grandmother is still alive. She's an older enslaved Black woman, well known and loved throughout the town. Everyone expects the mistress to free her, but she doesn't. Dr. Flint, one of the executors, decides to put her up for auction too. The town rebels.

Legally, the elderly woman everyone calls Aunt Martha is a slave. But the town's white residents consider selling her a disgrace. No one bids. The price falls and falls until the dead mistress's sister steps forward, buys Aunt Martha, and sets her free.

So Harriet remains enslaved in Dr. Flint's household while her grandmother, now free at about fifty, lives nearby in her own home. She sews for local women, makes a decent living, and saves money to buy her children. Eventually she manages to buy one of them. She has saved eight hundred dollars—an enormous sum in the United States of the 1820s and 1830s—and uses it to buy her son and set him free.

## 8. Sexual coercion, resistance, escape, and Harriet Jacobs's freedom

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00754–U-00947 -->

At the center of the story is Harriet, the young girl living in Dr. Flint's home. Technically, he doesn't own her; his young daughter does. But Harriet soon realizes that Dr. Flint, a respected member of the community, regularly has sex with enslaved women in his household and gets them pregnant.

She learns this after Flint has an enslaved man whipped. The man and his enslaved wife have just had a baby. Both parents are very dark-skinned, Harriet says, but the baby is light-skinned. The husband angrily accuses his wife of sleeping with Flint. Flint won't tolerate anyone discussing such things in his house. He has the husband whipped, then sells the entire family a few days later.

As they leave, the woman who has just given birth to Flint's child tells him: You promised to treat me well. Flint replies: You talk too much. No one can know about these things.

People discuss the subject only in whispers, but Harriet calculates that Flint has fathered eleven children with enslaved women. Each time, he avoids trouble by selling the mother and child together. And this, Harriet says, happens in every household.

White society does sometimes criticize men who behave this way. The women, especially, know everything and condemn husbands who do it. Harriet knows of two wives who persuade their husbands to free children fathered with enslaved women. But those cases are rare. Most people are so used to the practice that they barely notice it.

By fourteen, Harriet is beautiful and light-skinned enough to pass for white. Flint is fifty-five. He starts reminding her that she's in his service. You have to do whatever I ask, he tells her. You must obey me. You can't say no.

Entering her fifteenth year is a grim time in an enslaved girl's life, Harriet says. Her master begins whispering filthy things in her ear. She pretends not to understand, and he doesn't dare go further. After all, he can't be too blatant. The neighbors might talk, and he's a respected physician. Besides, Harriet says, he's afraid of her grandmother. Aunt Martha is free now, and everyone in town knows and respects her. So Flint moves carefully.

There's no point hoping the mistress will help, Harriet says. You might expect her to stop her husband's pursuit. Instead, she becomes jealous of the enslaved woman and mistreats her. Enslaved children notice these mysterious hatreds early. Why does the mistress despise this particular woman and treat her so badly? They grow older, and then they understand.

Mrs. Flint does watch her husband. She knows he wants Harriet and is determined to stop him. Eventually she pulls Harriet aside and demands the truth: What have you done with my husband? Harriet denies everything and swears on the Bible. But Mrs. Flint keeps mistreating her. She can't stop fearing and hating her.

Flint sees Harriet crying and tells her: Poor child, don't cry. I'll make peace between you and the mistress, but you have to let me handle things my way. You're a silly little girl. You don't know what's good for you. I would pamper you. I would make you a lady.

Harriet writes: “If God has bestowed beauty upon her, it will prove her greatest curse. That which commands admiration in the white woman only hastens the degradation of the female slave.”

This goes on for some time. Flint keeps whispering filthy things, and Harriet keeps pretending not to understand. She wants to tell her grandmother but is too frightened and ashamed. Flint keeps circling. He never touches her, never punishes her, never has her whipped. He just keeps pressing.

Then Harriet falls in love with a free Black man, a young carpenter like her father. Free Black communities do exist. About a tenth of the South's Black population is free—people emancipated by owners' wills, along with their families and descendants.

The carpenter offers to buy Harriet. As a free man with enough money, he can legally do that. But she knows Flint will never sell her, least of all to a Black man.

Harriet confides in a white woman she trusts. There's a quiet network of female solidarity here. We never see it clearly, but confidences can pass between women, even between a white mistress and an enslaved Black woman. This woman is Flint's friend and promises to speak with him. He refuses and, furious, summons Harriet.

So you want to marry? A free Negro? I'll show you who your master is—me or that Negro. For the first time, Harriet talks back. For the first time, Flint slaps her. Then he launches into a long speech about her ingratitude. He has done everything for her, he says, and treated her wonderfully. Is this how she repays him? I'll give you one more chance, he tells her. Think carefully. And if I see that Negro near this house, I'll shoot him like a dog.

Flint ignores her for two weeks. Then he slips her a note. He regrets hitting her, he says, but it's her fault. She's impossible. Still, he's sorry, and he won't do it again.

Meanwhile, the man who wants to marry her inherits property in another town. He moves away and disappears from her life. Then we reach the most delicate part of the story, which Harriet tells with great anxiety and hesitation. She begins a relationship with another white man—not Flint, but a prosperous local gentleman named Mr. Sands, who either runs for the North Carolina legislature or serves in it. It's a small town. People know one another and constantly cross paths.

Sands is unmarried. He often meets Harriet in the street, and they begin talking. It's not clear whether she loves him, but they do begin a relationship. She's fifteen and lets him seduce her. She struggles to admit this in her memoir and feels compelled to apologize. But she needed a protector, she says, and Sands could protect her.

Harriet becomes pregnant and has to confess. Mrs. Flint throws her out. She goes to her grandmother and tells her everything, and Aunt Martha throws her into the street too. But the grandmother soon reconsiders and calls her back.

Sands visits and promises to care for the baby. As soon as he can, he says, he'll buy Harriet and the child and free them. Flint comes too. Harriet is now settled in her grandmother's house. You fool, he tells her, I'm a doctor. If you'd told me right away, I could have helped you—meaning he could have ended the pregnancy. And now look at you. Tell me one thing: does this child belong to that Negro you wanted to marry?

No, Harriet says. The father is white. Flint flies into a rage but doesn't hit her. He keeps visiting throughout the pregnancy. When the baby is born, he attends the delivery and reminds Harriet that the child still belongs to him. Technically she belongs to his daughter, but for all practical purposes she's his slave, and so is her baby.

Sands visits too, but he can't legally acknowledge an enslaved child as his own. The quarrels continue. Harriet becomes pregnant again and has a second child with Sands. She stays with her grandmother. Flint doesn't dare bring her home, especially because his wife absolutely refuses to allow it. So Harriet no longer lives like Flint's slave, but she remains his property under the law.

The years pass, and the children grow. They don't understand why one white man sometimes visits or why Dr. Flint storms in shouting threats. They call Flint “the bad man.” When he gets out of control, Aunt Martha throws him out. She's free, so she can. And he slinks away.

At twenty-one, Harriet can't take any more and decides to escape. Several white women in the neighborhood know her story. They hide her in their homes while she tries desperately to find a way North, but escape is extremely difficult.

While Harriet is hiding nearby, Flint sells her children to a slave trader. He doesn't know the trader already has a deal with their father, Mr. Sands. Flint thinks the children will be taken deep into the South and Harriet will never see them again. Instead, the trader resells them to Sands, who frees them.

The entire town follows the affair and talks about it. Public opinion is on our side, Harriet says. Everyone is pleased that Sands has bought the children and set them free.

But what now? Harriet can't keep moving among her protectors' homes, so her grandmother builds a secret compartment in the house—like Anne Frank's hiding place, you might say. Harriet moves in and stays there for years. Through a small opening, she watches Flint pass in the street. She watches her children and their father, Mr. Sands, pass by too. Eventually the children learn she's there and visit her, but she must remain silent and invisible. No one can know.

After several years, someone finally arranges her passage North. She gets a berth on a ship with a Southern captain. He understands the situation perfectly well and pretends he doesn't. Harriet passes as a white woman, boards with a ticket, and reaches New York.

In New York, Harriet runs into one of Flint's neighbors. She's terrified. But the neighbor tells her not to worry. I don't care, he says. You had a terrible time in the doctor's house, and I wish you well. I won't tell anyone. When I get home, I'll even visit your grandmother and tell her I saw you.

Why is she so afraid in a free state? Because outlawing slavery doesn't stop owners from entering that state and seizing people who escaped from them. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, recently passed with Southern support, creates a federal system for recapturing enslaved people even in free states. [N-007]

In New York, Harriet works first as a seamstress and later as a wet nurse. She finds kind employers who give her a home and pay her well. One tells her: Listen, there's a solution. I'll buy you.

Harriet resists. She can't accept, on principle, that she would be free because someone bought and sold her rather than because freedom is her right.

But Flint keeps coming back to look for her. His daughter, now grown and still Harriet's legal owner, comes too. Rumors have placed Harriet in New York, so eventually she agrees to the purchase.

When Flint's daughter locates Harriet—not a hiding place, really, but the home where she now lives—Harriet's New York employer confronts her. She persuades the Southern woman to sell Harriet for three hundred dollars. Flint's daughter takes the money and goes home. The legal papers are drawn up, and Harriet's new owner immediately frees her. At last, Harriet is officially free.

But the experience leaves Harriet feeling terrible. She's happy and deeply grateful to the woman who helped her, yet her happiness is bitter: “So I was sold at last! A human being sold in the free city of New York! ... I am deeply grateful to the generous friend who procured it, but I despise the miscreant who demanded payment for what never rightfully belonged to him or his.”

## 9. Abolition as genuine moral progress

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00948–U-00966 -->

By the 1850s, Harriet has met people in New York who persuade her to write a book and tell her life story. And that's more or less where our story ends.

I'll leave you with this. As we'll see in the next episode, when we talk about totalitarianism and democracy, history doesn't guarantee steady progress. Things don't automatically get better for some mysterious reason. That's simply not how history works.

But when we look at human history as a whole, we can see real progress. I mean moral progress—not the internet, not technology, but genuine progress in how we live together. And slavery gives us one clear example. For thousands of years, nearly everyone takes it for granted. Today, we take it for granted that slavery is unacceptable. Thank you.
