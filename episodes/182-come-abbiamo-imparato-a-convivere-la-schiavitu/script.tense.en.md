# Come abbiamo imparato a convivere: la schiavitù — faithful English translation

## 1. Slavery as a historical norm and the distinction between slaveholding and slave societies

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00093 -->

So, slavery. Slavery is an enormous subject, because in fact almost all human societies in the past know slavery, until fairly recent times. In Europe it is only with the Enlightenment in the eighteenth century that someone begins to think something that neither the Greeks, however clever they are, nor the Romans, nor Christians have thought: namely, that there is something intrinsically wrong about slavery, something intrinsically unjust and indeed shameful. Before then, no one has ever thought it. And even so, we have to reach the second half of the nineteenth century before slavery finally disappears from the West as well, from the United States, from South America. So let us say that the period in which slavery ceases to be a problem is roughly a hundred or a hundred and fifty years old, no more than that. Before then, for millennia, slavery has always been present everywhere.

Everywhere means even in places and periods where perhaps we would not expect it. Because, well, I think everybody knows that the ancient Romans have slaves. But the fact that there are slaves even in the Florence of Lorenzo the Magnificent, for example, is not part of our mental furniture, so to speak. And yet there are, because no one has ever formally abolished slavery. In Renaissance Italy, I will not say the cities are full of slaves, but yes, some of them really are full. The homes of the rich, of princes, but also of merchants: it is completely normal to find male and female slaves in the home of a wealthy person in a Renaissance Italian city.

Why? Because the Christian world is in a permanent state of war with the Muslim world, and the Mediterranean is a battlefield. When Muslim ships are boarded, the people aboard them are captured: not only the crew, but civilians, merchants, and passengers. The Muslims naturally do the same. And those people are captured and sold. To whom? To whoever is pleased to buy himself a male slave—or, even more often, a female slave—to keep in the house. Or people are captured during raids carried out by Christian galleys along the coasts of North Africa, raids entirely identical to those carried out by the Barbary pirates along our coasts. Except that, since memory is always selective, we remember perfectly well that the Italian coasts in the Renaissance are continually attacked by Barbary pirates. We tend not to think about the fact that our people are doing the same thing on the other side.

But precisely because slaves are in fact everywhere, historians have been led to draw a very sharp distinction. In our terminology, we say that there are societies with slaves and slave societies. What does that mean? Slave societies are societies actually founded on slavery. They are societies that could not function if there were no slaves, or at any rate would have to function in a very different way. Ancient Greece, the Roman Empire up to a certain period, Georgia, Alabama, Virginia, and Texas before the American Civil War, the Caribbean—Haiti, Santo Domingo, Jamaica under French or English planters—those are slave societies in which slavery is an indispensable part of the system.

Renaissance Italy is not the same thing. You do not see slaves everywhere; of course, you know that rich people have one or two at home, but if they did not have them, almost nothing would change. So we make this distinction. And since today I have to choose, I will concentrate on slave societies: that is, societies in which slaves are omnipresent, extremely numerous, and, so to speak, indispensable to the operation of the system. I will tell you, if I can manage it in an hour, four stories. Obviously, they do not amount to a history of slave societies. But placed together and connected to one another, they may allow us to gain a clearer idea of what it means to live in a world based on slavery.

## 2. Rome in 61 CE and the murder of Pedanius Secundus

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00094–U-00175 -->

The first story is told by Tacitus. So we are in the Roman Empire, in AD 61, during the reign of Nero. Tacitus relates that in that year a very serious crime takes place in Rome: the prefect of the city, Pedanius Secundus, is murdered in his own home by one of his slaves. The prefect of the city is what we might call the minister of the interior, all right? So he is an extremely high-ranking official and politician, one of the most important men in Rome. Tacitus tells us that he is killed in his bed by one of his own slaves.

Why does this slave kill his master? Tacitus is not all that interested in the question. But there is some information, or at least some uncertainty. It is not clear, Tacitus says, which of two possible motives is involved, and both possible motives already help us understand how that world works. Some say it is because this slave has made arrangements to buy back his freedom. His master has promised to allow it and then changed his mind.

What does it mean for a slave to buy his own freedom? It is a paradox, but everything in the history of slavery is paradoxical. The slave belongs to his master, who in ancient Rome can do anything he likes to him, even kill him, and no one will say anything. And so everything belonging to the slave automatically belongs to the master. And yet there are many slaves whom their masters permit, for example, to set up a small business or activity, obviously paying part of their earnings to the master, and these slaves can nevertheless accumulate money of their own. That money belongs to the master too, if the master chooses to take it. But respectable people instead allow the slave to accumulate his own *peculium*—that is exactly what they called it—which he may eventually use, if the master agrees, to purchase himself, so to speak.

Naturally, it also happens that a slave has relatives who are free. Masters in ancient Rome often free faithful male and female slaves, people of whom they may even be fond, while those people may have brothers, sisters, or children who are still enslaved. In this case, it appears that this slave's father may in fact be free and wants to buy him back. The master has said yes and then changed his mind.

But during the trial on this occasion another possibility also emerges, and Tacitus throws that one in too. He tells these things in two lines; I am making a long story of it, but he throws it out in two lines. Perhaps this slave is in love with a young male prostitute, and the master is his rival and has taken the boy away from him. This brings us, let us say, into the atmosphere of absolute sexual freedom that prevails in the Roman world, especially among the ruling class. Everyone, or almost everyone, either is or can perfectly easily be bisexual. Male prostitution is as widespread as female prostitution. No one is scandalized; these things are entirely normal.

So one could say: yes, yes, apparently, from what I have heard, the story goes like this—the prefect Pedanius Secundus is, so to speak, in love with this boy who prostitutes himself and wants him entirely for himself. His slave has fallen in love with him too. In short, master and slave are rivals, and in the end the slave kills the master.

But the story is significant for a different reason. The law provides that when a master is killed in his own home, whether the culprit is known or unknown makes no difference whatsoever: all the slaves living under the same roof have to be crucified. As a matter of principle, because slaves have to know that it is in their interest to protect their master's life and that, if they fail to do so, they will all pay. Guilty or innocent makes no difference.

The prefect Pedanius Secundus has four hundred slaves living with him in his house. Do you understand? Men, women, old people, boys, girls, pregnant women—four hundred people. And the law provides that they should all be crucified. When word spreads through the city of Rome that this is about to happen, the people rise up. They pour into the squares and streets, protesting and trying to prevent all these people from being executed.

And they did so because in a slave society slaves are everywhere, and their lives are not always separate from everyone else's. There are plenty of slaves in the street whom you cannot distinguish from other people. They work as laborers or mechanics, or run a small shop belonging to their master, and they have relationships with other people. For ordinary people it is completely normal that family friends, or perhaps even relatives, might be the slaves of a wealthy owner. In short, to the common people, slaves are people like us after all. And the prospect of this mass execution produces unrest among the people. The Senate has to debate what to do.

## 3. Cassius Longinus, collective punishment, and the execution of four hundred slaves

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00176–U-00276 -->

We are under the Empire, as I told you, but the Senate still exists. Even under the Empire the Senate actually has enormous power. It has to deal with the emperor, who is a dictator with the power of life and death, but the Senate is, you understand, the assembly of the three hundred, four hundred, or five hundred richest men in the Empire, and so it does count for something.

The Senate debates what to do. Some say that perhaps applying the law in this particular case really would be a little too much. Tacitus, however, is interested only in what I am about to tell you, only in the speech I will now recount. An authoritative senator, Cassius Longinus, rises to speak, and Tacitus reports his speech word for word.

Cassius Longinus says to his fellow senators: You know me. You know that I have often spoken in this chamber when some proposed law introduced an innovation or altered an old law, and I have never opposed it. Not because I do not know that changing old laws is always wrong—because it is always wrong and weakens our authority. But I did not want to oppose such measures and give the impression that I was doing so on principle, out of prejudice. This time, however, I must tell you that we cannot fail to apply the law and change it. We cannot.

A man of consular rank, at the very summit of the imperial aristocracy, has been killed in his own home in a slave conspiracy. No one has prevented it. No one has reported it, even though the law condemning all those slaves to death is in force. And you would abolish that law? Then, by Hercules, establish impunity. Establish that a master can be killed in his own house with perfect ease. Who will be protected by his high rank, when being prefect of the city has not protected Pedanius Secundus? Who will be protected by having many slaves, when four hundred have not been enough to protect him?

They say—and this is where Tacitus obtained the information—that perhaps the murderer was merely avenging an injury, because he had made an agreement to be freed and was not freed, or because the master had taken a young male slave away from him. Very well. In that case, let us rule that the master was justly killed.

Cassius Longinus goes on. The very fact that our ancestors established this law proves that it is just and must not be changed. But let us pretend that we have to debate it for the first time. Surely you do not believe that a slave decided to kill his master without ever letting slip a threatening word, without ever making dangerous remarks? No one heard him? Let us grant that no one heard him, that he managed to prepare the weapon among people who noticed nothing. How did he cross the bedrooms, carry in a lamp, and commit his crime without anyone seeing him?

Our ancestors knew perfectly well that our only safety lay in slaves knowing that it was in their interest to report plots and dangers. And our ancestors did this when slaves were people born here, people they had known from birth. But now, as you understand, the Roman Empire has expanded enormously through victorious wars, and each of those wars has brought millions of slaves into the Empire from the most distant countries. Since our slaves have become foreigners, Cassius Longinus says, people with rites different from ours, an alien religion or perhaps no religion at all—this multitude, our slaves, can be kept in submission only by fear.

And you say that innocent people will die as well. Very well, Cassius Longinus says, that is acceptable, because the great examples made for the public interest always contain something unjust. He gives the example of another Roman practice, rarely applied, but sometimes applied: the decimation of military units that have behaved badly before the enemy. When a military unit has behaved cowardly in the face of the enemy, its commander can order a decimation. That means all the men are lined up, disarmed, amid the rest of the army, which is armed. Lots are drawn, and one man in ten is beaten to death. Whoever draws the lot draws it. He may be a decorated veteran. He may be someone who has not fled at all that day, even though all the others have. Whoever's turn it is, it is. This case is the same, Cassius Longinus says.

Some of the other senators try to say, yes, but the women; yes, but the children. Nothing doing. In the end the majority sides with Longinus, and they decide to carry out the sentence. But the people are rioting. At this point Emperor Nero has to intervene, send the army into the streets, block the roads, and open a passage along which these four hundred wretched people are taken and all crucified.

At this point someone says that the freed slaves as well—the freedmen whom the dead man had liberated during his lifetime—ought to be expelled from Italy. There, at least, Nero objects. He says that the traditional law is already severe enough and they do not wish to interpret it with still greater cruelty. But the law is applied.

## 4. Dionigi Carli encounters plantation slavery in seventeenth-century Brazil

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00277–U-00343 -->

And that is the first story. I do not think I will comment on the stories I tell you, because all things considered, they seem to speak for themselves. The second story brings us much closer in time, to the seventeenth century, the 1600s. What happens in the meantime? The Roman Empire collapses, and in our part of the world slavery gradually dies out. It is never abolished, but in the medieval and Renaissance world it is no longer an important structure. Slaves are, for example, the domestic servants in the homes of wealthy merchants who have bought a woman captured at sea from privateers. Those are the slaves.

But with the colonization of America, slavery suddenly becomes extremely important again. This happens with the Spanish colonization of Central and South America, and later with the English colonization of North America, but it begins above all in Spanish and Portuguese Central and South America, in Brazil. There is an immense country offering the promise of enormous profits. What crops are grown there? Sugarcane, and later tobacco. European landowners immediately realize that these crops require a great deal of labor, a very great deal. Indigenous populations are devastated by epidemics, warfare, exploitation, and forced labor, while expanding plantation economies drive colonists to import growing numbers of enslaved Africans. [N-002]

From the end of the fifteenth century, Europeans grow accustomed to knowing Africa a little better and discover that it is very easy to enslave local people there. And so the trafficking of Black slaves to America begins. It continues for centuries and fills America with Black people.

The story I want to tell you is about a Capuchin called Dionigi Carli of Piacenza, a Capuchin missionary, a deeply religious friar who sacrifices his life to serve as a missionary. He goes to Africa and to Brazil, traveling between the two coasts. When our Italian Capuchin arrives in Brazil, he encounters Black slavery for the first time—mass slavery, that is. Back home, yes, a duchess might still have a little Moor as a page, given to her by somebody, but slavery is no longer very visible. When our Capuchin arrives in America, by contrast, he sees it everywhere.

This Capuchin, Dionigi Carli, writes a book describing his travels. He tells his readers that when you arrive in Brazil, you see them everywhere—these Moors, as he calls them, these Black people. “We then saw many others, four, six, or eight at a time, chained—some by the neck, some by the hands, some by the feet—a salutary medicine, for without it they would all flee together.”

He says: At first they made me feel compassion. At first it seemed cruel to me. But then, when I began to understand what sort of people these Moors are, and that they are people who would not work unless forced, I understood that it had to be done this way. There was no alternative.

He is a very decent man. At first he feels a little compassion, but that is the system. Everyone he meets tells him, of course that is how it is, and it could not be otherwise. It takes him no time at all to accept that, well, of course, yes, yes, it has to be that way.

They take him to see sugarcane being processed. There is a crushing mill turned by slaves with enormous effort, and overseers stand ready with their whips. He says, yes, yes, the poor things; it makes you feel sorry to see them whipped like that. But on the other hand, it is a necessary medicine, because they would not want to do the work. I saw them in Africa, he says. They are people who know how to do nothing but dance. And so, if you want these Black people to work, of course you need the whip.

## 5. The Atlantic crossing and Carli's moral accommodation to the slave trade

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00344–U-00477 -->

Then, during one of his journeys, he boards a ship in Africa bound for Brazil. He embarks in Angola, a Portuguese colony, on a ship loaded with slaves that also carries passengers and cargo, and he recounts his voyage aboard it. Carli says the ship carries 630 enslaved people. [N-003] He tells these things with the utmost calm; they are interesting facts. They are all branded according to custom, some on one shoulder, some on the right arm, some on the left, and others above their breasts. All are branded with fire, each bearing the mark of his or her owner.

Then he describes how they are packed aboard the ship. The men are all crammed into the hold itself, right down at the very bottom, practically in darkness, shut inside. Because, he says, if they were free to move, they might cause trouble. They might set fire to the ship or throw themselves into the sea and drown, because, he says, they do this continually. These Moors being carried toward America throw themselves into the sea and drown whenever they have the opportunity.

And he says: Do you see why they do this? Because these Moors are truly stupid. They have a superstitious belief that the Portuguese are taking them to America to kill them and make oil from their bodies. And because they believe this, they prefer to kill themselves before arriving. And I, our good friar says, explained to them that this was not true, that it was completely false, that they could rest easy because no one would kill them when they arrived. But it made no difference. They were so stubborn. I saw a Black woman belonging to the ship's pilot, who had permission to walk around freely, throw herself into the sea. He caught her by one foot and managed to pull her back up. Such, the friar says, is the obstinacy of these Ethiopians. He also calls the Moors Ethiopians, always simply meaning Black people.

So all the adult men are crammed down in the hold. Then there are the various decks, with the women on the second deck. The pregnant women, however—forty of the six hundred people embarked—are placed in the stern cabin, where they are a little more comfortable. These are societies of the past in which children are continually being born and then easily die, societies in which a considerable proportion of the women are pregnant at any given moment. We can no longer even picture what that must look like. Then there are the children and young people on the first deck below the main deck, packed so tightly that they lie on top of one another and relieve themselves where they are. The stench, he says, is unbearable.

The ship is already overloaded when another seventy Moors arrive to be embarked. And here a problem arises, because Portuguese law—the priorities of different periods are different—perfectly calmly permits the slave trade, but provides that slaves taken from Africa to America have to be baptized. They have to be taught the catechism, converted to Christianity, and baptized; otherwise it is unlawful to embark them. These seventy new arrivals have not yet been baptized.

So before they can be embarked, the friar has to get busy. Who knows how he does it, in what language or in what manner, but at any rate he explains Christianity to them and baptizes them all. “There is excommunication for taking Africans to Brazil, or elsewhere, unless they have first been baptized ... I also baptized those children who were born from time to time to the pregnant women.” Once baptized, they are naturally branded and put aboard.

The voyage goes badly because there is no wind and the ship cannot move. Why is that? Why? It is truly unusual in that season. Something is wrong. There has to be a reason. Why is God not sending them wind? Are they absolutely certain that all these Moors have been baptized? He asks the captain, and the captain says, yes, yes, now I remember: of course, I brought another four aboard at the last moment, and they have not been baptized.

The friar has them brought to him at once. After instructing them as well as possible, he says, I baptize them, together with another three who have just been born and are still steaming.

But the wind still does not come. Now, all right, this has nothing to do with slavery, but it gives us a glimpse into the mentality of seventeenth-century sailors. The Portuguese sailors take the statue of Saint Anthony of Padua they have aboard and tie it to the mainmast. Then they kneel before Saint Anthony's statue, beat their breasts, and tell him: You will remain here until you obtain from God the good wind that will set the ship moving again.

The friar disapproves. This business of tying up the saint is not altogether proper. It would be better to bring the statue up and pray to him, without the violence of tying him to the mainmast. Nevertheless, it works. The wind comes, the ship sets off again, and the saint has granted the favor.

The voyage eventually ends, though not without further difficulties. Carli later reports that thirty-three are missing when the ship reaches Brazil. The friar says everyone is delighted: it is a grace from God that so few have died, because ordinarily half of them die, and sometimes even more.

He then says that they have every problem in the world during these voyages. These voyages carrying slaves are the most painful and distressing in the world. Not so much because of the captives' suffering; the whole business is badly organized in itself. Why? Because the ships are overloaded. Yet everyone wants to embark his slaves: the next ship is leaving in a month, and I cannot possibly wait. And so everyone wants to put his slaves aboard, meaning the ships are always overloaded. But they also have to be loaded with food and water, bearing in mind that these are not crates of sugar—a typical American product, no?—but living people who eat and drink. And so they do in fact have to be given food and water.

Unfortunately, at a certain point the captain comes and says that, because of all the delays, their food supplies are running out. The friar tells him: Look, I know that an acquaintance of mine has had crates of goods placed in the cargo below, and I know there is also food in them. Open the crates; I will take responsibility. We will have some more food, and these provisions, he says, will serve to keep the white people alive. If the Moors die, we shall have to be patient.

Then they check the water, and there is plenty of it. The friar is delighted. Very well, he says, the Moors will survive for two days on water, and in the meantime the Lord will help us.

Meanwhile, word spread aboard the ship that the food had run out. The Black people in the hold began to grow agitated, shouting and pounding. The friar says, yes, yes, poor things, I can understand them too. We let them vent. One must trust in God and in the Madonna.

At this point the friar slips in a small detail. I was very upset, he says, because I discovered that the sailors had nicknamed the whip they used to beat the slaves *Maria*. And the friar says: You call the whip after the Madonna? How do you expect them to believe what we teach them about Christianity? They will think we are making fools of them.

So, you understand, we have to make an effort not to feel horror toward this friar. For him, what genuinely matters is that we save their souls. Their lives matter only up to a point; what counts is the other life. And we are saving their souls. He truly believes it.

After this they pray day and night, and all the white people confess as though they are at the point of death. Then one of the sailors says that perhaps it would be better to kill all the Moors, to make sure there would be enough food. Here the friar objects: it is a diabolical proposal. One person must never be killed so that another can live. He says that if he were not there, they might even do it, but he is there and will not allow it.

And here, although I promised not to comment, perhaps this too requires no comment, except to observe how living in a society where slavery is normal produces what seems to us like a dulling of the moral sense: you no longer see things that seem so obvious to us. Are we running a little late? Faster, then. The third story.

## 6. Joseph Knight and the judicial rejection of slavery in Scotland

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00478–U-00647 -->

The third story takes us a little further forward in time, to the eighteenth century, when many voices in Europe begin to say that there is something wrong with slavery, that slavery is unjust, that slavery is unlawful. It is the story of a slave who is born in Africa, captured, and sold in Jamaica, an English possession with great sugarcane plantations, where sugar and rum are produced.

His owners gave this slave the name Joseph Knight—Knight, with a K, Joseph Knight—and in Jamaica he was held by a Scottish planter, Sir John Wedderburn. [N-004] He was an extremely wealthy planter. He bought this slave, grew fond of him, and took him as his personal manservant. You understand? A slave's fate differed enormously depending on whether he was sent to turn the crushing mill all day under the whip, or was instead the master's manservant, living in the house, well dressed and well fed, with everything that entailed.

The master grew fond of him. After some years, in 1769, he returned from Jamaica to Scotland and brought the slave with him. In Scotland, at the master's home, at Sir John's home, something happened that we would not expect. First, once they arrived in Scotland, he had him baptized. Protestants were evidently less scrupulous than Portuguese Catholics; they were not required to baptize their slaves. But Sir John had his dear manservant Joseph Knight baptized.

And then something happened that I would not have believed possible, although the sources speak of it as entirely normal. Joseph Knight fell in love with a domestic servant in the household, Ann Thompson. She was Scottish and therefore white. They fell in love and married. The master allowed them to marry and even paid the wedding expenses himself. They had a child, whom the master had baptized. The child then died, and the master paid for the burial.

At this point Joseph wanted to go and live with Ann, his wife, in a small house of their own, while remaining in the master's service. Until then they had lived in the house. Do you remember *The Marriage of Figaro*? Figaro and Susanna are the count and countess's servants; they live in the house. Now Joseph and Ann wanted to live on their own.

Here we do not know why, but Joseph and his master quarreled. Something happened that is not clear to us. The master did not want it and refused. It ended very badly: the master dismissed Ann. She was a free woman employed in his service, and he dismissed her. At that point Joseph simply left the house and went to live with his wife.

At that point the master remembered that Joseph was his slave, sent the police to seize him, and had him brought back home. He announced that he was about to return to Jamaica and intended to take Joseph with him.

Here begins the most unexpected part of the story—perhaps not unexpected, but in any event the part that makes the story historically very important. Joseph Knight, the slave, had evidently received advice and made acquaintances and friends. He decided to sue his master in the Scottish courts, to establish that his master had no right to take him back to Jamaica.

What was the situation? At that time the situation in Britain was extremely ambiguous. There were practically no slaves in Britain, except when a wealthy planter returned from the West Indies and, as in this case, brought his slave with him. There had already been several trials of this kind in England, and judgments had declared—I translate—“As soon as a negro comes into England, he becomes free; one may be a villein in England, but not a slave.” And the law would therefore protect him in the enjoyment of his person and his property.

These, however, were judgments by individual judges, not legislation. They could be cited, but they could also be contradicted. And they were English judgments. Here, by contrast, we are in Scotland. You may know that England and Scotland are not only two different nations; the respect in which they are most profoundly different is precisely their legal systems. The Scottish legal system and Scottish law are completely different from their English counterparts.

In 1774, while the rebellious colonists in America were already agitating for liberty and equality, preparing to proclaim that all men were equal even though many of them were naturally slave owners, including George Washington and so forth, Joseph Knight sued his master before the justices of the peace in Perth. He argued that slavery did not exist in Scotland and that, even if he had been lawfully purchased in Jamaica, he automatically became free when he arrived in Scotland.

The master opposed him, and the judges ruled for the master. Among other things, Sir John's lawyer reasoned as follows: Let us think about this. As a matter of principle, it makes no difference whether we are discussing one Negro—I am using their words—or twenty thousand. If you tell us that an owner has no right to transport his slave to Jamaica, then you will also tell us that a person engaged in the mass trade cannot do so either. Is it forbidden to take slaves to Jamaica? Sir John's lawyers said that no one could seriously maintain such a ridiculous proposition in a country inhabited by rational beings. The supposedly ridiculous proposition was that slaves could not be taken to Jamaica.

Very well. Joseph lost the case and appealed to the higher judge, the sheriff of Perth. The sheriff of Perth ruled for Joseph. The condition of slavery was not recognized by the laws of this kingdom and was inconsistent with its principles. The Jamaican regulations concerning slaves did not extend to this kingdom. Therefore, the master had no right to demand Joseph's compulsory service. Do you understand? This was a world and an age in which judges were making law. There were no acts of Parliament. There was public opinion, which the judges interpreted, and it was moving in that direction.

Sir John in turn appealed to the supreme court in Edinburgh. He argued that perhaps slavery did not exist in Scotland, but Scotland did have—and this was true—contracts by which a person bound himself to serve a master for life. Such contracts could bind domestic servants to a master. Craftsmen's apprentices too often signed contracts obliging them to serve for as long as the master wished. These contracts were perfectly lawful in Scotland. So Sir John said: Very well, he is not my slave, agreed. But he is obliged to serve me, and Scottish law provides for that.

Joseph's defense lawyers before Scotland's supreme court in Edinburgh based their case instead on the fact that the matter at issue was slavery. Perhaps for the first time in the entire case, they explained in detail why slavery could not be tolerated in the civilized world. They said: Let us not attempt to establish how this man was captured in Africa and brought to Jamaica. We cannot know it. Jamaican law does not concern itself with that detail; it accepts the situation. But whether he was captured, kidnapped, or sold by his parents—and that happened quite normally too—the injustice in Africa was the same.

The fact that slavery had existed among so many peoples in the past did not make it any less unjust. In the past, some societies had accepted infanticide, for example. That did not make infanticide any less ignoble, Joseph's lawyers said. Nor could they debate the fact that Negro slavery was highly advantageous to the country and produced great profits. That had nothing to do with whether it was just.

And in any event, they said, enlightened authors were by now arguing that even economically slavery was not so profitable after all, and that cotton—no, sugar—and tobacco could be cultivated without slaves. But what mattered was that oppression and injustice could never be justified by the advantages they produced.

The supreme court in Edinburgh rejected Sir John's appeal and confirmed that Joseph was a free man. Since the dominion over this Negro under Jamaican law was unjust, it could not be upheld in this country in any form. Therefore, the defendant, Sir John, had no right to the Negro's services for any period, nor any right to send him out of the country without his consent. The Negro was protected by law against these abuses.

And this case made history. From that moment onward, no one could be held in slavery on the soil of the kingdom of Scotland, just as similar judgments in England produced the same effect there.

It would be wonderful to know what happened afterward to Joseph Knight and his wife Annie, but we do not know. They disappear from history. We have the court records, but he was a free man. Presumably he lived in Scotland, working as a servant or doing something else together with his wife. We know nothing more. What remains in history is the case, *Knight v. Wedderburn*.

I do not know whether you noticed that we began with a story, Tacitus's story, involving hundreds of slaves about whom we do not even know whether they were male or female. I said they were, but Tacitus does not. Our seventeenth-century Capuchin, at least, took an interest in the fact that these were adult men and those were women. He mentions some people individually, though naturally not by name. Here we have encountered a slave with his first and last name.

## 7. Harriet Jacobs, abolitionist testimony, and childhood in slavery

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00648–U-00753 -->

The final stage concerns not a male slave but, this time, an enslaved woman. We know not only her first and last name; she tells us her story herself, because she writes a book to tell it. There are several such cases in the nineteenth-century United States, and this is one of the most famous. Her name is Harriet Jacobs. She is born in 1813 in the southern United States, in North Carolina. [N-006]

You know that slavery originally existed throughout the United States, but during the eighteenth century the Northern states outlawed it. The Southern states, whose economies were based on cotton and tobacco, and therefore on a large enslaved workforce, retained it and would continue to do so until the end of the American Civil War in 1865.

Harriet was born in 1813 in North Carolina, a slave state, and died in 1897 after living most of her life as a free woman. She was the first Black enslaved woman to write her autobiography, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself.*, which was published in 1861, even before the Civil War, just as the war was about to begin.

Harriet wrote this book with the help, naturally, of acquaintances and contacts among people in the North who were fighting for the abolition of slavery. She wrote specifically to address Northern public opinion, where there were movements hostile to slavery—the abolitionists—but Northern public opinion was by no means unanimously in agreement. Even in the North, there were plenty of people who did not care that slavery existed.

Harriet addressed Northern public opinion with a very precise intention. She did not simply denounce the horrors of slavery in general terms, although those horrors are present too. She spoke from a woman's point of view. One senses that she particularly had female readers in mind, the white women who would read her book. She wanted to show the moral degradation that slavery, and especially the enslavement of women, produced within white slave-owning society.

It was a delicate subject. This was the mid-nineteenth century, the Victorian period, when matters of sex were difficult to discuss, and yet that is what she discussed. In the book's epigraph she says that Northerners know nothing about slavery and have no idea of the depth of degradation involved. And again: “I do earnestly desire to arouse the women of the North to a realizing sense of the condition of two millions of women at the South, still in bondage, suffering what I suffered, and most of them far worse.”

She was therefore the child of slaves. The subject will recur in the family: right from the beginning, they all had fairly light skin. It is clear that, if one could reconstruct the family tree, more than one white master had impregnated a Black enslaved woman. And so they were descended from white people; they were Black; they were slaves. But they lived well and were fairly happy.

Her father was a carpenter. His owners permitted him to have his own carpentry workshop. He paid them an annual rent and otherwise lived his own life. Harriet says: I was born a slave, but I never knew it until I was six years old. I had a happy childhood in which I understood nothing about any of this. It seemed like a normal life.

By working as a carpenter, her father accumulated savings. Like the people in the Roman world we discussed at the beginning, he too hoped to save enough money to buy his children. He was himself a slave, but before freeing himself he wanted to free his children. A slave can own slaves. It is an insane world, as you will see. A slave can perfectly well buy and own other slaves. He hoped to buy his children and free them, but their mistress would never agree.

They had a good mistress who was very close to Harriet's mother. They were milk sisters, because Harriet's grandmother had been wet nurse both to her own daughter and to the little mistress. She had nursed her daughter and the newborn mistress together. But Harriet's mother, the little enslaved girl, was weaned at three months so that the nurse would have enough milk for the little mistress.

As children, the little mistress and Harriet's mother played together. Harriet's mother later died, and Harriet remained with this mistress, who treated her extremely well—almost like a daughter, Harriet says. She taught her to read and write. When Harriet's mother lay dying, the mistress promised her that her children would never have anything to fear. Everyone was fairly convinced that the mistress would free them all in her will.

Instead, when this good mistress—who had treated Harriet like a daughter—died, it did not occur to her to free them in her will. She bequeathed Harriet to a little niece, a young child, who therefore became Harriet's new owner. Other members of the family, Harriet's uncles, were sold at auction by the executors.

Harriet found herself living in the home of the little girl's father. It was a middle-class family. The father, Dr. Flint, was a physician. They were in a small town in North Carolina, an ordinary middle-class family. Harriet was twelve when the mistress died; she moved into the family's home and lived in this situation. As I told you, there was no shortage of paradoxes.

Harriet's grandmother was still alive. Her mother was dead, but her grandmother remained. She was an old Black woman, an elderly enslaved woman, very well known and popular in the town. When the mistress died, everyone expected that she would have freed her, but she had not. Dr. Flint, Harriet's new master, was an executor of the will and decided to sell the old woman at auction too. The town rebelled.

When he decided to sell the elderly lady—let us call her that—Aunt Martha, as everyone called her, she was of course a slave. But the white townspeople thought selling Aunt Martha was wrong, a disgrace. No one appeared at the auction. The price fell and fell and fell. Then the dead mistress's sister arrived, bought Aunt Martha, and freed her.

So Harriet was a slave in Dr. Flint's household, while her grandmother was free at about fifty years old and lived nearby in her own home. She sewed, did work for local ladies, lived well, and accumulated money. At a certain point, the grandmother, who was saving money to buy back her children, managed to buy one of them. She had put aside eight hundred dollars, an enormous sum in the United States of the 1820s and 1830s. She had saved it, bought back one of her children, and freed him.

## 8. Sexual coercion, resistance, escape, and Harriet Jacobs's freedom

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00754–U-00947 -->

At the center of the story, naturally, is Harriet, the young girl in Dr. Flint's household. Technically he is not her owner—her owner is Dr. Flint's daughter, who is a child—but, in short, Harriet soon realizes that Dr. Flint, a respected member of the community, habitually sleeps with his enslaved women and makes them pregnant.

She discovers this one day when the master has a male slave whipped. This man is married to another enslaved person. They have just had a baby, and both father and mother are very dark-skinned, Harriet says. The baby is born light-skinned, and the husband becomes angry with his wife, accusing her of sleeping with the master. The master will not tolerate these things being discussed in his household. So he has the husband whipped, and a few days later has all of them sold.

As they are leaving, the woman—the enslaved woman who has just borne the master's child and is now being sold—says to him: You promised to treat me well. The master replies: Your tongue is too long. These things must not be known.

Harriet says that, through whispers, since the matter is discussed only in the greatest secrecy within the house, she calculates that the master has fathered eleven children with his enslaved women. Each time, he sells the children together with their mothers to avoid the inconvenience. And Harriet says: Look, this happens in every household.

Within white society, men who behave in this way are sometimes criticized. The ladies especially know everything and criticize husbands who act like this. Harriet knows of two cases in which, when a master has a child by an enslaved woman, his wife, the mistress, persuades him to free the child. But Harriet says these cases are rare. In general, people are so accustomed to it that they no longer notice.

When Harriet is fourteen she is a beautiful girl, very light-skinned. She can almost pass for white; in fact, she can pass for white. When she is fourteen, the master, who is fifty-five, begins telling her: You know, Harriet, that you are in my service. You must do everything I ask. You must obey me, whatever I ask of you. You cannot say no.

Harriet says that entering her fifteenth year is a sad period in the life of an enslaved girl. My master began to whisper filthy things in my ear. She pretends not to notice, and he does not dare go further, because in any event, you understand, one must not overdo things. The neighbors will gossip. He is a physician and a respected person. And then, Harriet says, he is afraid of my grandmother. Old Aunt Martha is now free, known and respected by everyone in town, so Dr. Flint proceeds cautiously.

In such cases, Harriet says, it is useless to hope that the mistress will help you. You are there while the master pursues you, and you might think the mistress would intervene. But no: the mistress is actually jealous of the enslaved woman and begins mistreating her. Harriet says that enslaved children notice from an early age that these inexplicable hatreds exist within the household: the mistress truly hates that woman and always mistreats her—why? Then you grow older and understand.

The mistress does watch her husband. She understands that he has set his sights on Harriet and wants to prevent him from succeeding. She watches him and, naturally, at a certain point she takes Harriet aside and says: Tell me the truth. What have you done with my husband? Harriet says nothing. She swears on the Bible. But the mistress continues treating her badly; she cannot help fearing and hating her.

The master sees Harriet distressed and often crying in this situation, and says: Poor child, do not cry. I will make peace between you and the mistress, but let me do things my way. You are a silly little thing; you do not recognize what is good for you. I would pamper you. I would make you a lady.

And here Harriet says: “If God has bestowed beauty upon her, it will prove her greatest curse. That which commands admiration in the white woman only hastens the degradation of the female slave.”

Things continue in this way for some time. The master continues saying filthy things to her, and she pretends not to notice. She wants to tell her grandmother everything, but she does not, because she is afraid in this situation. She is ashamed and does not dare tell her grandmother everything. The master continues circling around her. He never lays a finger on her, never has her punished, never has her whipped. He simply keeps insisting.

Then she, by now a young woman, falls in love. She falls in love with a free Black man, a young carpenter like her father. There are free Black people. Roughly a tenth of the Black population of the Southern states consists of free Black people whom masters have freed in their wills and who then form families and have children.

She falls in love with this free Black man, and he offers to buy her, because as a free man, if he has some capital, he can buy her. But she tells him the master will never permit it. Dr. Flint will never sell her, least of all to a Black man.

She tells everything to a white woman in town whom she trusts. There is this network of complicity—never entirely clear, but one senses that confidences can develop between women, even between a white female owner and an enslaved Black woman. Harriet confides in this white woman, who is a friend of Dr. Flint's and promises to try speaking to him. But the doctor refuses. In fact, he is furious and summons Harriet.

So you want to marry? And a free Negro? I will show you who your master is, whether it is me or that Negro. For the first time, she answers him back, and for the first time he slaps her. Then he delivers a long speech about her ingratitude. He has benefited her in every possible way and treated her extremely well, and this ingratitude is how she repays him. He tells her: Look, I will give you one more chance. Think carefully. As for that Negro, if I see him around the house, I will shoot him like a dog.

For two weeks the master does not speak to her. Then he slips her a note saying that he regrets hitting her, but it is her fault. She is to blame; she is impossible. Nevertheless, he is sorry and will not do it again.

Meanwhile, the Black man who wants to marry her receives an inheritance in another town, moves away, and leaves her life. And then—here we enter the most delicate part, which she recounts with great timidity and anxiety—she has a relationship with another white gentleman. Not with the master, but another gentleman, a prosperous man who at one point is a candidate for the North Carolina legislature, or perhaps actually a member of it. He is a gentleman of the town named Mr. Sands. You understand? People see one another and know one another; life is shared in any event.

She falls in love with this unmarried gentleman, who often meets her in the street. They begin talking. In short, it is unclear whether she falls in love, but they certainly have a relationship. She is fifteen and allows herself to be seduced. In her memoir she has great difficulty admitting this and feels she must apologize. But, she says, I needed a protector. I needed someone to protect me, and he could be that person.

She becomes pregnant and has to confess it at home. The mistress, Dr. Flint's wife, throws her out. She takes refuge with her grandmother and tells her everything, and her grandmother throws her out too: a girl who has become pregnant like this, out in the street. But her grandmother reconsiders and calls her back.

The father of the expected child, Mr. Sands, comes to see her and promises that he will care for the baby, and that as soon as possible he will buy her and the child and free them. The master also comes. By now she is staying at her grandmother's house, firmly established there. Dr. Flint tells her: You fool, I am a doctor. If you had told me at once, I could have helped you—meaning he could have made her abort. You fool, you did not tell me, and now what? And now I want to know one thing: is this child the child of that Negro you wanted to marry?

She says no, it is the child of a white man. The master goes mad with rage but does not hit her. He continues visiting throughout the pregnancy. When the baby is born, Dr. Flint attends her and reminds her that the baby nevertheless belongs to him. She is his slave—his daughter's, technically, but in short, she is his slave, so the baby is his slave.

The child's father, Mr. Sands, also comes to see her, but cannot officially acknowledge the child because the child is enslaved. Things continue in this way through continual quarrels. She becomes pregnant again and has another child, also by Mr. Sands. She continues living with her grandmother. Dr. Flint does not dare take her back into his household, especially because his wife will not allow it at any price. So in practice she no longer lives as Dr. Flint's slave, but legally she is still enslaved by him.

The years pass and her children grow. They do not understand what is happening. Why does this white man occasionally come to the house, and why does Dr. Flint shout, threaten, and scream? Her children call him the bad man. But when Dr. Flint loses his head, the grandmother throws him out of the house. She is a free woman and can throw him out, and he leaves with his tail between his legs.

Finally, when Harriet is twenty-one, she can bear no more and decides to escape. At first, several white women in the neighborhood who know the whole story take her in and hide her in their homes. She hopes—she hopes—to escape to the North, but it is very difficult.

While she is hidden nearby, Dr. Flint sells her children. He sells them to a slave trader without knowing that the trader already has an agreement with the children's father, Mr. Sands. So the trader buys them. Dr. Flint believes they will be taken far away to the South and that Harriet will never see them again. Instead, they are sold to their father, who frees them.

The whole town knows about this, discusses it, and follows the affair. Harriet says that public opinion is on our side. Everyone is happy that Mr. Sands has bought back our children and freed them.

And then? She cannot remain hidden in the homes of the women protecting her, so her grandmother constructs a secret hiding place for her in the house, like Anne Frank, no? There. Harriet moves into it and remains there for several years. She spends years hidden in her grandmother's house, seeing Dr. Flint pass in the street, seeing her own children pass, and seeing Mr. Sands, the father of her children. After a while the children discover that she is there and come to visit her, but she has to remain silent; no one can see or know anything.

After some years of this life, passage to the North is arranged, so to speak. She finds a berth on a ship whose captain is a Southerner. He understands the situation perfectly well but pretends not to. Harriet passes as white, as a white lady, boards with a ticket, and reaches New York.

In New York she happens to encounter one of her master's neighbors, who has come to the city. She is terrified, but the neighbor tells her: Look, do not be afraid. I do not care. You had a bad time at the doctor's house. As far as I am concerned, I wish you good luck. I will tell no one. In fact, when I return home, I will visit your grandmother and tell her that I met you.

But at the same time, why is she terrified? She is in New York, a free state. Yet the fact that slavery does not exist in a state does not mean that the owners of fugitive slaves are not permitted to go there and seize them, even in the North. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, recently passed with the involvement of the South, creates a federal enforcement system for the recapture of enslaved people in free states. [N-007]

Harriet works in New York, first as a seamstress and later as a wet nurse. She works well for good women who house her and pay her well. They tell her: Listen, the solution is for us to buy you. I will buy you.

For a time she resists. As a matter of principle, she says, she does not want to accept the idea that she is free not because she has the right to be free, but because she has been bought and sold.

In the end, however, Dr. Flint occasionally returns to look for her. The doctor's daughter, who has now grown up and is her true owner, also appears. Word has spread that Harriet must be in New York, and so Harriet finally agrees.

When the doctor's daughter arrives after they have identified Harriet's hiding place—or rather, where she lives; she is not hidden, she lives there—the New York mistress who employs Harriet confronts the mistress from the South and persuades her to sell Harriet. Three hundred dollars. The young Southern mistress agrees, returns home with the money, and an instrument of emancipation is drawn up. The New York woman, now Harriet's owner, frees her. Harriet is officially free.

Harriet says that in truth it makes her feel bad. On the one hand she is happy and deeply grateful to this woman, but all that happiness turns bitter: “So I was sold at last! A human being sold in the free city of New York! ... I am deeply grateful to the generous friend who procured it, but I despise the miscreant who demanded payment for what never rightfully belonged to him or his.”

## 9. Abolition as genuine moral progress

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00948–U-00966 -->

After that, by then in the 1850s, Harriet meets people within New York society who persuade her to write a book and tell the story of her life. And there, so to speak, it ends.

I would say this. As we will see in the next lectures, when we discuss totalitarianism and democracy, there is no guarantee that history is a continuous process of progress or that, for some unknown reason, things automatically improve. That is not how it is.

But if one examines the history of humanity as a whole, there has been some genuine progress. I mean moral progress—beyond the Internet and, no, not technology, but genuine progress in the way we live together. Some genuine progress has occurred. And one example is certainly the fact that slavery is taken for granted by everyone for such a long time, whereas today we take for granted that it is unacceptable. Thank you.
