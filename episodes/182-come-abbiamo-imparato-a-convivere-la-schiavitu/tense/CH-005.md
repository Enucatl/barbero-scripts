## 5. The Atlantic crossing and Carli's moral accommodation to the slave trade

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00344–U-00477 -->

Then, during one of his journeys, he boards a ship in Africa bound for Brazil. He embarks in Angola, a Portuguese colony, on a ship loaded with slaves that also carries passengers and cargo, and he recounts his voyage aboard it. Carli says the ship carries 630 enslaved people. [N-003] He tells these things with the utmost calm; they are interesting facts. They are all branded according to custom, some on one shoulder, some on the right arm, some on the left, and others above their breasts. All are branded with fire, each bearing the mark of his or her owner.

Then he describes how they are packed aboard the ship. The men are all crammed into the hold itself, right down at the very bottom, practically in darkness, shut inside. Because, he says, if they were free to move, they might cause trouble. They might set fire to the ship or throw themselves into the sea and drown, because, he says, they do this continually. These Moors being carried toward America throw themselves into the sea and drown whenever they have the opportunity.

And he says: Do you see why they do this? Because these Moors are truly stupid. They have a superstitious belief that the Portuguese are taking them to America to kill them and make oil from their bodies. And because they believe this, they prefer to kill themselves before arriving. And I, our good friar says, explained to them that this was not true, that it was completely false, that they could rest easy because no one would kill them when they arrived. But it made no difference. They were so stubborn. I saw a Black woman belonging to the ship's pilot, who had permission to walk around freely, throw herself into the sea. He caught her by one foot and managed to pull her back up. Such, the friar says, is the obstinacy of these Ethiopians. He also calls the Moors Ethiopians, always simply meaning Black people.

So all the adult men are crammed down in the hold. Then there are the various decks, with the women on the second deck. The pregnant women, however—forty of the six hundred people embarked—are placed in the stern cabin, where they are a little more comfortable. These are societies of the past in which children are continually being born and then easily die, societies in which a considerable proportion of the women are pregnant at any given moment. We can no longer even picture what that must look like. Then there are the children and young people on the first deck below the main deck, packed so tightly that they lie on top of one another and relieve themselves where they are. The stench, he says, is unbearable.

The ship is already overloaded when another seventy Moors arrive to be embarked. And here a problem arises, because Portuguese law—the priorities of different periods are different—perfectly calmly permits the slave trade, but provides that slaves taken from Africa to America have to be baptized. They have to be taught the catechism, converted to Christianity, and baptized; otherwise it is unlawful to embark them. These seventy new arrivals have not yet been baptized.

So before they can be embarked, the friar has to get busy. Who knows how he does it, in what language or in what manner, but at any rate he explains Christianity to them and baptizes them all. “There is excommunication for taking Africans to Brazil, or elsewhere, unless they have first been baptized ... I also baptized those children who were born from time to time to the pregnant women.” Once baptized, they are naturally branded and put aboard.

The voyage goes badly because there is no wind and the ship cannot move. Why is that? Why? It is truly unusual in that season. Something is wrong. There has to be a reason. Why is God not sending them wind? Are they absolutely certain that all these Moors have been baptized? He asks the captain, and the captain says, yes, yes, now I remember: of course, I brought another four aboard at the last moment, and they have not been baptized.

The friar has them brought to him at once. After instructing them as well as possible, he says, I baptize them, together with another three who have just been born and are still steaming.

But the wind still does not come. Now, all right, this has nothing to do with slavery, but it gives us a glimpse into the mentality of seventeenth-century sailors. The Portuguese sailors take the statue of Saint Anthony of Padua they have aboard and tie it to the mainmast. Then they kneel before Saint Anthony's statue, beat their breasts, and tell him: You will remain here until you obtain from God the good wind that will set the ship moving again.

The friar disapproves. This business of tying up the saint is not altogether proper. It would be better to bring the statue up and pray to him, without the violence of tying him to the mainmast. Nevertheless, it works. The wind comes, the ship sets off again, and the saint has granted the favor.

The voyage eventually ends, though not without further difficulties. Carli later reports that thirty-three are missing when the ship reaches Brazil. The friar says everyone is delighted: it is a grace from God that so few have died, because ordinarily half of them die, and sometimes even more.

He then says that they have every problem in the world during these voyages. These voyages carrying slaves are the most painful and distressing in the world. Not so much because of the captives' suffering; the whole business is badly organized in itself. Why? Because the ships are overloaded. Yet everyone wants to embark his slaves: the next ship is leaving in a month, and I cannot possibly wait. And so everyone wants to put his slaves aboard, meaning the ships are always overloaded. But they also have to be loaded with food and water, bearing in mind that these are not crates of sugar—a typical American product, no?—but living people who eat and drink. And so they do in fact have to be given food and water.

Unfortunately, at a certain point the captain comes and says that, because of all the delays, their food supplies are running out. The friar tells him: Look, I know that an acquaintance of mine has had crates of goods placed in the cargo below, and I know there is also food in them. Open the crates; I will take responsibility. We will have some more food, and these provisions, he says, will serve to keep the white people alive. If the Moors die, we shall have to be patient.

Then they check the water, and there is plenty of it. The friar is delighted. Very well, he says, the Moors will survive for two days on water, and in the meantime the Lord will help us.

Meanwhile, word spread aboard the ship that the food had run out. The Black people in the hold began to grow agitated, shouting and pounding. The friar says, yes, yes, poor things, I can understand them too. We let them vent. One must trust in God and in the Madonna.

At this point the friar slips in a small detail. I was very upset, he says, because I discovered that the sailors had nicknamed the whip they used to beat the slaves *Maria*. And the friar says: You call the whip after the Madonna? How do you expect them to believe what we teach them about Christianity? They will think we are making fools of them.

So, you understand, we have to make an effort not to feel horror toward this friar. For him, what genuinely matters is that we save their souls. Their lives matter only up to a point; what counts is the other life. And we are saving their souls. He truly believes it.

After this they pray day and night, and all the white people confess as though they are at the point of death. Then one of the sailors says that perhaps it would be better to kill all the Moors, to make sure there would be enough food. Here the friar objects: it is a diabolical proposal. One person must never be killed so that another can live. He says that if he were not there, they might even do it, but he is there and will not allow it.

And here, although I promised not to comment, perhaps this too requires no comment, except to observe how living in a society where slavery is normal produces what seems to us like a dulling of the moral sense: you no longer see things that seem so obvious to us. Are we running a little late? Faster, then. The third story.
