## 8. Sexual coercion, resistance, escape, and Harriet Jacobs's freedom

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00754–U-00947 -->

At the center of the story, naturally, is Harriet, the young girl in Dr. Flint's household. Technically he is not her owner—her owner is Dr. Flint's daughter, who is a child—but, in short, Harriet soon realizes that Dr. Flint, a respected member of the community, habitually sleeps with his enslaved women and makes them pregnant.

She discovers this one day when the master has a male slave whipped. This man is married to another enslaved person. They have just had a baby, and both father and mother are very dark-skinned, Harriet says. The baby is born light-skinned, and the husband becomes angry with his wife, accusing her of sleeping with the master. The master will not tolerate these things being discussed in his household. So he has the husband whipped, and a few days later has all of them sold.

As they are leaving, the woman—the enslaved woman who has just borne the master's child and is now being sold—says to him: You promised to treat me well. The master replies: Your tongue is too long. These things must not be known.

Harriet says that, through whispers, since the matter is discussed only in the greatest secrecy within the house, she calculates that the master has fathered eleven children with his enslaved women. Each time, he sells the children together with their mothers to avoid the inconvenience. And Harriet says: Look, this happens in every household.

Within white society, men who behave in this way are sometimes criticized. The ladies especially know everything and criticize husbands who act like this. Harriet knows of two cases in which, when a master has a child by an enslaved woman, his wife, the mistress, persuades him to free the child. But Harriet says these cases are rare. In general, people are so accustomed to it that they no longer notice.

When Harriet is fourteen she is a beautiful girl, very light-skinned. She can almost pass for white; in fact, she can pass for white. When she is fourteen, the master, who is fifty-five, begins telling her: You know, Harriet, that you are in my service. You must do everything I ask. You must obey me, whatever I ask of you. You cannot say no.

Harriet says that entering her fifteenth year is a sad period in the life of an enslaved girl. My master began to whisper filthy things in my ear. She pretends not to notice, and he does not dare go further, because in any event, you understand, one must not overdo things. The neighbors will gossip. He is a physician and a respected person. And then, Harriet says, he is afraid of my grandmother. Old Aunt Martha is now free, known and respected by everyone in town, so Dr. Flint proceeds cautiously.

In such cases, Harriet says, it is useless to hope that the mistress will help you. You are there while the master pursues you, and you might think the mistress would intervene. But no: the mistress is actually jealous of the enslaved woman and begins mistreating her. Harriet says that enslaved children notice from an early age that these inexplicable hatreds exist within the household: the mistress truly hates that woman and always mistreats her—why? Then you grow older and understand.

The mistress does watch her husband. She understands that he has set his sights on Harriet and wants to prevent him from succeeding. She watches him and, naturally, at a certain point she takes Harriet aside and says: Tell me the truth. What have you done with my husband? Harriet says nothing. She swears on the Bible. But the mistress continues treating her badly; she cannot help fearing and hating her.

The master sees Harriet distressed and often crying in this situation, and says: Poor child, do not cry. I will make peace between you and the mistress, but let me do things my way. You are a silly little thing; you do not recognize what is good for you. I would pamper you. I would make you a lady.

And here Harriet says: “If God has bestowed beauty upon her, it will prove her greatest curse. That which commands admiration in the white woman only hastens the degradation of the female slave.”

Things continue in this way for some time. The master continues saying filthy things to her, and she pretends not to notice. She wants to tell her grandmother everything, but she does not, because she is afraid in this situation. She is ashamed and does not dare tell her grandmother everything. The master continues circling around her. He never lays a finger on her, never has her punished, never has her whipped. He simply keeps insisting.

Then she, by now a young woman, falls in love. She falls in love with a free Black man, a young carpenter like her father. There are free Black people. Roughly a tenth of the Black population of the Southern states consists of free Black people whom masters have freed in their wills and who then form families and have children.

She falls in love with this free Black man, and he offers to buy her, because as a free man, if he has some capital, he can buy her. But she tells him the master will never permit it. Dr. Flint will never sell her, least of all to a Black man.

She tells everything to a white woman in town whom she trusts. There is this network of complicity—never entirely clear, but one senses that confidences can develop between women, even between a white female owner and an enslaved Black woman. Harriet confides in this white woman, who is a friend of Dr. Flint's and promises to try speaking to him. But the doctor refuses. In fact, he is furious and summons Harriet.

So you want to marry? And a free Negro? I will show you who your master is, whether it is me or that Negro. For the first time, she answers him back, and for the first time he slaps her. Then he delivers a long speech about her ingratitude. He has benefited her in every possible way and treated her extremely well, and this ingratitude is how she repays him. He tells her: Look, I will give you one more chance. Think carefully. As for that Negro, if I see him around the house, I will shoot him like a dog.

For two weeks the master does not speak to her. Then he slips her a note saying that he regrets hitting her, but it is her fault. She is to blame; she is impossible. Nevertheless, he is sorry and will not do it again.

Meanwhile, the Black man who wants to marry her receives an inheritance in another town, moves away, and leaves her life. And then—here we enter the most delicate part, which she recounts with great timidity and anxiety—she has a relationship with another white gentleman. Not with the master, but another gentleman, a prosperous man who at one point is a candidate for the North Carolina legislature, or perhaps actually a member of it. He is a gentleman of the town named Mr. Sands. You understand? People see one another and know one another; life is shared in any event.

She falls in love with this unmarried gentleman, who often meets her in the street. They begin talking. In short, it is unclear whether she falls in love, but they certainly have a relationship. She is fifteen and allows herself to be seduced. In her memoir she has great difficulty admitting this and feels she must apologize. But, she says, I needed a protector. I needed someone to protect me, and he could be that person.

She becomes pregnant and has to confess it at home. The mistress, Dr. Flint's wife, throws her out. She takes refuge with her grandmother and tells her everything, and her grandmother throws her out too: a girl who has become pregnant like this, out in the street. But her grandmother reconsiders and calls her back.

The father of the expected child, Mr. Sands, comes to see her and promises that he will care for the baby, and that as soon as possible he will buy her and the child and free them. The master also comes. By now she is staying at her grandmother's house, firmly established there. Dr. Flint tells her: You fool, I am a doctor. If you had told me at once, I could have helped you—meaning he could have made her abort. You fool, you did not tell me, and now what? And now I want to know one thing: is this child the child of that Negro you wanted to marry?

She says no, it is the child of a white man. The master goes mad with rage but does not hit her. He continues visiting throughout the pregnancy. When the baby is born, Dr. Flint attends her and reminds her that the baby nevertheless belongs to him. She is his slave—his daughter's, technically, but in short, she is his slave, so the baby is his slave.

The child's father, Mr. Sands, also comes to see her, but cannot officially acknowledge the child because the child is enslaved. Things continue in this way through continual quarrels. She becomes pregnant again and has another child, also by Mr. Sands. She continues living with her grandmother. Dr. Flint does not dare take her back into his household, especially because his wife will not allow it at any price. So in practice she no longer lives as Dr. Flint's slave, but legally she is still enslaved by him.

The years pass and her children grow. They do not understand what is happening. Why does this white man occasionally come to the house, and why does Dr. Flint shout, threaten, and scream? Her children call him the bad man. But when Dr. Flint loses his head, the grandmother throws him out of the house. She is a free woman and can throw him out, and he leaves with his tail between his legs.

Finally, when Harriet is twenty-one, she can bear no more and decides to escape. At first, several white women in the neighborhood who know the whole story take her in and hide her in their homes. She hopes—she hopes—to escape to the North, but it is very difficult.

While she is hidden nearby, Dr. Flint sells her children. He sells them to a slave trader without knowing that the trader already has an agreement with the children's father, Mr. Sands. So the trader buys them. Dr. Flint believes they will be taken far away to the South and that Harriet will never see them again. Instead, they are sold to their father, who frees them.

The whole town knows about this, discusses it, and follows the affair. Harriet says that public opinion is on our side. Everyone is happy that Mr. Sands has bought back our children and freed them.

And then? She cannot remain hidden in the homes of the women protecting her, so her grandmother constructs a secret hiding place for her in the house, like Anne Frank, no? There. Harriet moves into it and remains there for several years. She spends years hidden in her grandmother's house, seeing Dr. Flint pass in the street, seeing her own children pass, and seeing Mr. Sands, the father of her children. After a while the children discover that she is there and come to visit her, but she has to remain silent; no one can see or know anything.

After some years of this life, passage to the North is arranged, so to speak. She finds a berth on a ship whose captain is a Southerner. He understands the situation perfectly well but pretends not to. Harriet passes as white, as a white lady, boards with a ticket, and reaches New York.

In New York she happens to encounter one of her master's neighbors, who has come to the city. She is terrified, but the neighbor tells her: Look, do not be afraid. I do not care. You had a bad time at the doctor's house. As far as I am concerned, I wish you good luck. I will tell no one. In fact, when I return home, I will visit your grandmother and tell her that I met you.

But at the same time, why is she terrified? She is in New York, a free state. Yet the fact that slavery does not exist in a state does not mean that the owners of fugitive slaves are not permitted to go there and seize them, even in the North. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, recently passed with the involvement of the South, creates a federal enforcement system for the recapture of enslaved people in free states. [N-007]

Harriet works in New York, first as a seamstress and later as a wet nurse. She works well for good women who house her and pay her well. They tell her: Listen, the solution is for us to buy you. I will buy you.

For a time she resists. As a matter of principle, she says, she does not want to accept the idea that she is free not because she has the right to be free, but because she has been bought and sold.

In the end, however, Dr. Flint occasionally returns to look for her. The doctor's daughter, who has now grown up and is her true owner, also appears. Word has spread that Harriet must be in New York, and so Harriet finally agrees.

When the doctor's daughter arrives after they have identified Harriet's hiding place—or rather, where she lives; she is not hidden, she lives there—the New York mistress who employs Harriet confronts the mistress from the South and persuades her to sell Harriet. Three hundred dollars. The young Southern mistress agrees, returns home with the money, and an instrument of emancipation is drawn up. The New York woman, now Harriet's owner, frees her. Harriet is officially free.

Harriet says that in truth it makes her feel bad. On the one hand she is happy and deeply grateful to this woman, but all that happiness turns bitter: “So I was sold at last! A human being sold in the free city of New York! ... I am deeply grateful to the generous friend who procured it, but I despise the miscreant who demanded payment for what never rightfully belonged to him or his.”
