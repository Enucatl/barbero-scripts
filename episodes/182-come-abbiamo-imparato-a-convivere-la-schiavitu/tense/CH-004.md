## 4. Dionigi Carli encounters plantation slavery in seventeenth-century Brazil

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00277–U-00343 -->

And that is the first story. I do not think I will comment on the stories I tell you, because all things considered, they seem to speak for themselves. The second story brings us much closer in time, to the seventeenth century, the 1600s. What happens in the meantime? The Roman Empire collapses, and in our part of the world slavery gradually dies out. It is never abolished, but in the medieval and Renaissance world it is no longer an important structure. Slaves are, for example, the domestic servants in the homes of wealthy merchants who have bought a woman captured at sea from privateers. Those are the slaves.

But with the colonization of America, slavery suddenly becomes extremely important again. This happens with the Spanish colonization of Central and South America, and later with the English colonization of North America, but it begins above all in Spanish and Portuguese Central and South America, in Brazil. There is an immense country offering the promise of enormous profits. What crops are grown there? Sugarcane, and later tobacco. European landowners immediately realize that these crops require a great deal of labor, a very great deal. Indigenous populations are devastated by epidemics, warfare, exploitation, and forced labor, while expanding plantation economies drive colonists to import growing numbers of enslaved Africans. [N-002]

From the end of the fifteenth century, Europeans grow accustomed to knowing Africa a little better and discover that it is very easy to enslave local people there. And so the trafficking of Black slaves to America begins. It continues for centuries and fills America with Black people.

The story I want to tell you is about a Capuchin called Dionigi Carli of Piacenza, a Capuchin missionary, a deeply religious friar who sacrifices his life to serve as a missionary. He goes to Africa and to Brazil, traveling between the two coasts. When our Italian Capuchin arrives in Brazil, he encounters Black slavery for the first time—mass slavery, that is. Back home, yes, a duchess might still have a little Moor as a page, given to her by somebody, but slavery is no longer very visible. When our Capuchin arrives in America, by contrast, he sees it everywhere.

This Capuchin, Dionigi Carli, writes a book describing his travels. He tells his readers that when you arrive in Brazil, you see them everywhere—these Moors, as he calls them, these Black people. “We then saw many others, four, six, or eight at a time, chained—some by the neck, some by the hands, some by the feet—a salutary medicine, for without it they would all flee together.”

He says: At first they made me feel compassion. At first it seemed cruel to me. But then, when I began to understand what sort of people these Moors are, and that they are people who would not work unless forced, I understood that it had to be done this way. There was no alternative.

He is a very decent man. At first he feels a little compassion, but that is the system. Everyone he meets tells him, of course that is how it is, and it could not be otherwise. It takes him no time at all to accept that, well, of course, yes, yes, it has to be that way.

They take him to see sugarcane being processed. There is a crushing mill turned by slaves with enormous effort, and overseers stand ready with their whips. He says, yes, yes, the poor things; it makes you feel sorry to see them whipped like that. But on the other hand, it is a necessary medicine, because they would not want to do the work. I saw them in Africa, he says. They are people who know how to do nothing but dance. And so, if you want these Black people to work, of course you need the whip.
