## 9. Abolition as genuine moral progress

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00948–U-00966 -->

After that, by then in the 1850s, Harriet meets people within New York society who persuade her to write a book and tell the story of her life. And there, so to speak, it ends.

I would say this. As we will see in the next lectures, when we discuss totalitarianism and democracy, there is no guarantee that history is a continuous process of progress or that, for some unknown reason, things automatically improve. That is not how it is.

But if one examines the history of humanity as a whole, there has been some genuine progress. I mean moral progress—beyond the Internet and, no, not technology, but genuine progress in the way we live together. Some genuine progress has occurred. And one example is certainly the fact that slavery is taken for granted by everyone for such a long time, whereas today we take for granted that it is unacceptable. Thank you.
