## 2. Rome in 61 CE and the murder of Pedanius Secundus

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00094–U-00175 -->

The first story is told by Tacitus. So we are in the Roman Empire, in AD 61, during the reign of Nero. Tacitus relates that in that year a very serious crime takes place in Rome: the prefect of the city, Pedanius Secundus, is murdered in his own home by one of his slaves. The prefect of the city is what we might call the minister of the interior, all right? So he is an extremely high-ranking official and politician, one of the most important men in Rome. Tacitus tells us that he is killed in his bed by one of his own slaves.

Why does this slave kill his master? Tacitus is not all that interested in the question. But there is some information, or at least some uncertainty. It is not clear, Tacitus says, which of two possible motives is involved, and both possible motives already help us understand how that world works. Some say it is because this slave has made arrangements to buy back his freedom. His master has promised to allow it and then changed his mind.

What does it mean for a slave to buy his own freedom? It is a paradox, but everything in the history of slavery is paradoxical. The slave belongs to his master, who in ancient Rome can do anything he likes to him, even kill him, and no one will say anything. And so everything belonging to the slave automatically belongs to the master. And yet there are many slaves whom their masters permit, for example, to set up a small business or activity, obviously paying part of their earnings to the master, and these slaves can nevertheless accumulate money of their own. That money belongs to the master too, if the master chooses to take it. But respectable people instead allow the slave to accumulate his own *peculium*—that is exactly what they called it—which he may eventually use, if the master agrees, to purchase himself, so to speak.

Naturally, it also happens that a slave has relatives who are free. Masters in ancient Rome often free faithful male and female slaves, people of whom they may even be fond, while those people may have brothers, sisters, or children who are still enslaved. In this case, it appears that this slave's father may in fact be free and wants to buy him back. The master has said yes and then changed his mind.

But during the trial on this occasion another possibility also emerges, and Tacitus throws that one in too. He tells these things in two lines; I am making a long story of it, but he throws it out in two lines. Perhaps this slave is in love with a young male prostitute, and the master is his rival and has taken the boy away from him. This brings us, let us say, into the atmosphere of absolute sexual freedom that prevails in the Roman world, especially among the ruling class. Everyone, or almost everyone, either is or can perfectly easily be bisexual. Male prostitution is as widespread as female prostitution. No one is scandalized; these things are entirely normal.

So one could say: yes, yes, apparently, from what I have heard, the story goes like this—the prefect Pedanius Secundus is, so to speak, in love with this boy who prostitutes himself and wants him entirely for himself. His slave has fallen in love with him too. In short, master and slave are rivals, and in the end the slave kills the master.

But the story is significant for a different reason. The law provides that when a master is killed in his own home, whether the culprit is known or unknown makes no difference whatsoever: all the slaves living under the same roof have to be crucified. As a matter of principle, because slaves have to know that it is in their interest to protect their master's life and that, if they fail to do so, they will all pay. Guilty or innocent makes no difference.

The prefect Pedanius Secundus has four hundred slaves living with him in his house. Do you understand? Men, women, old people, boys, girls, pregnant women—four hundred people. And the law provides that they should all be crucified. When word spreads through the city of Rome that this is about to happen, the people rise up. They pour into the squares and streets, protesting and trying to prevent all these people from being executed.

And they did so because in a slave society slaves are everywhere, and their lives are not always separate from everyone else's. There are plenty of slaves in the street whom you cannot distinguish from other people. They work as laborers or mechanics, or run a small shop belonging to their master, and they have relationships with other people. For ordinary people it is completely normal that family friends, or perhaps even relatives, might be the slaves of a wealthy owner. In short, to the common people, slaves are people like us after all. And the prospect of this mass execution produces unrest among the people. The Senate has to debate what to do.
