# Structural outline

## Overview

Barbero argues that slavery was treated as normal across most of recorded history and that its modern moral delegitimization is a rare example of genuine progress. He distinguishes societies that merely contained enslaved people from societies structurally dependent on slavery, then develops the argument through four increasingly personal narratives: Roman collective punishment, Dionigi Carli's seventeenth-century Atlantic voyage, Joseph Knight's freedom suit, and Harriet Jacobs's autobiography.

## 1. Historical prevalence and analytical categories

- Transcript: U-00001–U-00093; original audio: 00:26–05:18.
- Slavery remained broadly legitimate until Enlightenment criticism and survived in the Americas into the nineteenth century.
- Renaissance Italy illustrates a society containing enslaved domestic workers without depending structurally on slavery.
- Greece, Rome, the antebellum American South, and Caribbean plantation colonies illustrate slave societies.
- Research targets: the chronology of abolition and Enlightenment delegitimization (C-001); Mediterranean and Renaissance Italian slavery (C-002); the slave-society distinction and examples (C-003).

## 2. Pedanius Secundus and Roman slavery

- Transcript: U-00094–U-00175; original audio: 05:20–10:59.
- Tacitus's account begins with the murder of the urban prefect Pedanius Secundus by one of his slaves in 61 CE.
- Possible motives expose manumission through a slave's peculium and sexual relations in the Roman household.
- Roman law required every enslaved person under the same roof to be executed when a master was murdered there; public opposition forces Senate debate.
- Research targets: Roman peculium and manumission (C-004); the legal basis and scope of collective household punishment (C-005); Tacitus's alternative motives for the murder (Q-001).

## 3. Cassius Longinus and collective punishment

- Transcript: U-00176–U-00276; original audio: 11:00–17:19.
- Cassius Longinus defends inherited law, deterrence, and exemplary injustice before the Senate.
- His comparison with military decimation makes the preservation of elite security explicit.
- The Senate accepts the argument; Nero deploys troops against the crowd and all four hundred enslaved household members are executed.
- Research targets: the wording and transmission of Longinus's speech (Q-002); the Senate decision, crowd protest, Nero's intervention, and executions (C-006).

## 4. Dionigi Carli in Brazil

- Transcript: U-00277–U-00343; original audio: 17:19–21:46.
- European colonization and plantation labor revive mass slavery in the Americas and drive the Atlantic trade.
- Capuchin missionary Dionigi Carli initially feels compassion but rapidly adopts the plantation system's racialized rationalizations.
- Supporting examples include chained captives, forced labor in sugar production, and the description of whipping as necessary medicine.
- Research targets: the causal account of colonial labor and the growth of the Atlantic trade (C-007); Carli's reported change of view and wording (Q-003).

## 5. Carli's Atlantic crossing

- Transcript: U-00344–U-00477; original audio: 21:47–31:09.
- Carli describes roughly 670 branded captives packed aboard a ship from Angola to Brazil, including pregnant women and children.
- Captives' suicide attempts are interpreted through a reported fear that Europeans would render their bodies into oil (Q-004).
- Portuguese baptism requirements, newborn baptisms, and the coercion of Saint Anthony reveal the crew's religious framework (Q-005).
- Thirty-three captives reportedly die, a toll Carli presents as unusually low; food scarcity exposes an explicit hierarchy of white and enslaved lives (C-008).
- Carli objects when sailors call the whip “Maria,” fearing damage to conversion rather than centering the captives' suffering (Q-006).

## 6. Joseph Knight's freedom suit

- Transcript: U-00478–U-00647; original audio: 31:09–42:22.
- Joseph Knight is taken from Jamaica to Scotland by John Wedderburn, marries Ann Thompson, leaves the household, and is forcibly returned.
- Knight sues, arguing that Jamaican slave status cannot operate under Scottish law.
- Earlier English judicial language about freedom upon landing provides legal context (Q-007).
- Successive Scottish rulings culminate in the Court of Session rejecting Wedderburn's claim to Knight's perpetual service and removal (Q-008).
- Research target: the identities, procedural history, holdings, and wider legal effect of *Knight v Wedderburn* (C-009).

## 7. Harriet Jacobs's testimony and childhood

- Transcript: U-00648–U-00753; original audio: 42:22–50:03.
- The narrative moves from sources that largely erase enslaved individuals to an enslaved woman's own autobiography.
- Harriet Jacobs's *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* addresses Northern women and emphasizes slavery's sexual and moral degradation (Q-009, Q-010).
- Her early childhood, family members' skilled work, informal earnings, inheritance by a child, and her grandmother's eventual purchase and freedom reveal slavery's legal paradoxes.
- Research target: Jacobs's biography, publication history, pseudonyms, and autobiographical reliability (C-010).

## 8. Coercion, resistance, escape, and legal freedom

- Transcript: U-00754–U-00947; original audio: 50:04–65:03.
- Jacobs describes Dr. Flint's sexual exploitation of enslaved women and his pursuit of her, alongside Mrs. Flint's jealousy (C-011).
- Candidate source passages include Flint's assertion of mastery (Q-011), Jacobs's statement that beauty is a curse for an enslaved woman (Q-012), and Flint's apologetic note after striking her (Q-013).
- Jacobs has two children with Mr. Sands, escapes, hides for years in her grandmother's garret, and reaches New York.
- The fugitive-slave regime leaves her vulnerable even in a free state (C-012).
- Her employer purchases and emancipates her; Jacobs records mixed gratitude and bitterness at being sold in New York (Q-014).

## 9. Moral progress

- Transcript: U-00948–U-00966; original audio: 65:03–66:06.
- Barbero rejects a general law of automatic historical progress while presenting the near-universal delegitimization of slavery as genuine moral progress.
- Research target: the broad modern legal abolition and delegitimization claim (C-013).
