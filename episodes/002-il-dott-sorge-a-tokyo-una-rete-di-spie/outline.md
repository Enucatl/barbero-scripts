# Episode outline: Richard Sorge and his Tokyo spy ring

## Overview

The lecture begins with the violent factional struggle inside the Japanese army and uses the
February 1936 coup attempt to introduce the one foreign journalist who could explain Japan to
European readers: Richard Sorge. It then traces Sorge's transformation from decorated German
soldier to Soviet intelligence officer, his Shanghai apprenticeship, his construction of a
convincing Nazi-journalist identity, and the complementary access supplied by Eugen Ott, Hotsumi
Ozaki, and radio operator Max Clausen. The final movement argues that the ring affected the course
of the Second World War by reporting Japanese and German intentions, before narrating its exposure,
trial, execution, and postwar memory.

## 1. Army revolt and the journalist who understands Japan

- Transcript: U-00001–U-00124; original audio: 00:50–07:57.
- Barbero moves from Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa's killing of General Nagata to the February 26
  Incident, presenting both as symptoms of generational and class conflict inside the army.
- Western observers cannot interpret the crisis, while Sorge publishes a successful explanation;
  the Soviet republication of his article threatens his cover and reveals the hidden radio link.
- Digressions: the samurai ideal and the rivalry between army and navy.
- Quotations: Aizawa's shame at needing more than one sword stroke [Q-001]; Sorge's warning to
  Moscow after the Pravda publication [Q-002].
- Claims: the factional interpretation of the army crisis [C-001]; Sorge's exceptional analytical
  position and the article's circulation [C-002]; the ring's decisive wartime influence [C-003].

## 2. From German war volunteer to Soviet operative

- Transcript: U-00125–U-00194; original audio: 08:00–12:22.
- Sorge's German-Russian family, First World War service, wounds, political conversion, doctorate,
  recruitment by the Comintern, and move to Moscow establish his dual identity.
- Christiane Gerlach's recollection introduces Sorge's charisma and the family nickname Ika.
- Quotations: Christiane's recollection that Sorge never needed to court anyone [Q-003].
- Claims: the chronology of Sorge's war service, party membership, doctorate, and Soviet move
  [C-004]; Christiane Gerlach's identity and recollection [C-005].

## 3. Shanghai apprenticeship and the Tokyo assignment

- Transcript: U-00195–U-00299; original audio: 12:29–19:17.
- General Jan Karlovič Bērziņš moves Sorge from Comintern agitation into Red Army intelligence and
  sends him to Shanghai, where he builds a network amid concessions, warlords, Communists, and
  Japanese expansion.
- In Moscow, Sorge jokingly proposes Japan and is assigned an experimental mission in a country
  hostile to Communism and obsessed with espionage.
- Claims: the 1929 recruitment and Shanghai mission [C-006]; Soviet strategic need for intelligence
  on Japan and the Kwantung Army [C-007].

## 4. Building the German and Nazi cover

- Transcript: U-00300–U-00390; original audio: 19:21–25:58.
- Sorge returns openly to Berlin, obtains journalism commissions and introductions from Karl
  Haushofer, secures a passport, and applies to join the Nazi Party.
- Haushofer's geopolitics, ties to Rudolf Hess, Jewish family connections, and tragic later fate
  form the lecture's largest biographical digression.
- Quotations: Sorge's first Tokyo boast that he can lead them all by the nose [Q-004].
- Claims: Haushofer's introductions and influence [C-008]; the failures that allowed Sorge to
  obtain a passport and Nazi membership [C-009].

## 5. Eugen Ott and the German embassy

- Transcript: U-00391–U-00470; original audio: 26:01–31:54.
- Sorge wins the confidence of German diplomats and becomes especially close to Eugen Ott, whose
  promotion from military adviser to ambassador gives Sorge extraordinary access to reports,
  codes, and secret documents.
- Sorge refuses formal embassy and Nazi posts that might trigger a background check, while
  cultivating the persona of a witty, critical Nazi.
- Claims: Sorge's access through Ott [C-010]; the chronology and nature of Sorge's Nazi Party
  membership [C-011].

## 6. Hotsumi Ozaki and the Japanese network

- Transcript: U-00471–U-00559; original audio: 31:58–38:12.
- Open-source intelligence is unusually difficult for foreigners in Japan, making Japanese agents
  essential. Sorge reconnects with Shanghai contacts, above all journalist and government adviser
  Hotsumi Ozaki, and recruits Japanese Communists returned from the United States.
- The ring's idealism, tradecraft, courier routes, and uncertain legal position are described.
- Claims: Ozaki's professional access and role [C-012]; the composition and motivation of the
  Japanese network [C-013].

## 7. Max Clausen and the radio link

- Transcript: U-00560–U-00638; original audio: 38:17–43:24.
- Max Clausen is recruited from maritime radio work, trained in Moscow, and sent to Tokyo with his
  wife. He builds a portable transmitter from locally acquired parts while establishing a
  profitable industrial cover.
- Moscow's decision to make the ring use Clausen's business income creates friction, but his radio
  gives the ring frequent direct contact with Soviet receivers.
- Claims: Clausen's recruitment, transmitter, and commercial cover [C-014].

## 8. Sorge's double life in Tokyo

- Transcript: U-00639–U-00702; original audio: 43:28–48:10.
- Sorge adopts Japanese domestic habits while living a conspicuous nightlife among German
  diplomats. His drinking, relationship with Hanako Ishii, and motorcycle crash reveal the strain
  and recklessness beneath his cover.
- Quotations: Hanako's recollection that Sorge was kind although he did not love her [Q-005].
- Claims: the motorcycle crash and Clausen's removal of compromising material [C-015].

## 9. Strategic intelligence and the war in Mongolia

- Transcript: U-00703–U-00770; original audio: 48:11–53:03.
- Sorge learns of the secret Anti-Comintern Pact negotiations through the German embassy, while
  Ozaki supplies plans and assessments from circles connected to the Kwantung Army.
- Barbero links this intelligence to Soviet preparation for the 1939 border war and Zhukov's
  victory, which discourages a Japanese northern strategy.
- Claims: Sorge's reporting on the Anti-Comintern Pact [C-016]; the ring's contribution to Soviet
  knowledge before Khalkhin Gol and the consequences of the Japanese defeat [C-017].

## 10. Barbarossa, the southern strategy, and Sorge's last message

- Transcript: U-00771–U-00843; original audio: 53:12–57:36.
- Sorge warns Moscow of the German invasion, but Stalin rejects multiple warnings. Sorge then uses
  Ozaki's access to report that Japan will strike south rather than attack Siberia, allowing Soviet
  forces to move west for the defense of Moscow.
- Barbero adds the stronger claim that Ozaki helped shape, not merely report, Japan's decision.
- Quotations: Sorge's answer to Hanako—he weeps because he is alone [Q-006]; the interrogators'
  postwar explanation for suppressing Ozaki's influence [Q-007].
- Claims: the timing and treatment of Sorge's Barbarossa warnings [C-018]; the effect of the
  southern-strategy reports on Soviet deployments [C-019]; Ozaki's influence on policy [C-020].

## 11. Discovery and arrest of the ring

- Transcript: U-00844–U-00910; original audio: 57:39–61:40.
- The Tokkō follows a chain from a long-surveilled Communist, through Chiyo Kitabayashi and Yotoku
  Miyagi, to Ozaki, Sorge, and Clausen. German officials initially defend Sorge because his cover
  has survived earlier suspicions.
- Quotations: Sorge's earlier “lead them by the nose” boast returns as an ironic refrain [Q-004].
- Claims: the arrest chain and dates [C-021]; German warnings, Schellenberg's judgment, and embassy
  protests [C-022].

## 12. Trial, execution, and memory

- Transcript: U-00911–U-00975; original audio: 61:49–65:58.
- Barbero distinguishes brutal police practice from the lengthy, formal prosecutorial inquiry,
  describes the defendants' sentences and Sorge and Ozaki's execution, and closes with respectful
  Japanese assessments of both men.
- Quotations: Clausen's pro-Japanese prison declaration [Q-008]; the Tokkō report that Ozaki viewed
  the work as honorable [Q-009]; prosecutor Yoshikawa's tribute to Sorge [Q-010].
- Claims: interrogation conditions and trial procedure [C-023]; execution date, survival of other
  ring members, and postwar memory [C-024].

## Consolidated targets

- Quotations: Q-001–Q-010.
- Claims: C-001–C-024.
- Recurring terminology: Richard Sorge; Hotsumi Ozaki; Max Clausen; Eugen Ott; Tokkō; Kempeitai;
  Kwantung Army; Anti-Comintern Pact; Khalkhin Gol/Nomonhan; northern and southern strategies.
- Transcript-only uncertainties: exact wording and transmission history of several recollections;
  whether some reported dialogue is documentary quotation or Barbero's dramatized paraphrase;
  the degree of causal influence attributable to the ring rather than to broader intelligence and
  Japanese strategic conditions.
