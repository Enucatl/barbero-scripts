# C-019: Did Richard Sorge’s intelligence save Moscow?

## Research question

The lecture presents a famous causal sequence: Sorge established that Japan would attack south
rather than invade Siberia; Stalin could therefore transfer troops from the Soviet Far East; and
those troops arrived just in time to stop the Germans outside Moscow in December 1941.

All three elements have a basis in the historical record. Sorge reported on Japanese intentions,
Soviet formations moved west, and eastern formations fought around Moscow. The question is whether
the surviving evidence joins them into that simple chain of cause and effect.

The most important recent challenge is Shimizu Ryotaro’s “Undecided Decision: Japan’s
‘Northbound’ and Richard Sorge Spy Ring in 1941,” published by Japan’s National Institute for
Defense Studies. Shimizu does not argue that Sorge was unimportant. He argues that the surviving
telegrams, Japanese decision records, and deployment chronology do not demonstrate that one
decisive Sorge report caused Stalin to release the formations that saved Moscow.

## The older story and its source

Shimizu traces the canonical formulation to Charles A. Willoughby’s 1952 *Shanghai Conspiracy*.
Willoughby, who had served as Douglas MacArthur’s intelligence chief, wrote that Sorge assured his
superiors there would be no Japanese attack, after which “the Siberian divisions were entrained for
the West” and appeared in the successful defense of Moscow.

That account joined three facts:

1. Sorge reported on Japanese intentions.
2. Soviet formations were transferred west.
3. Some eastern formations fought effectively around Moscow.

The resulting inference was that the first fact caused the second, which caused the third. Later
biographies often repeated the sequence. Peter Herde’s 2010 *Neue Deutsche Biographie* entry says
Sorge’s information about Japan’s decision not to attack enabled Stalin to withdraw elite
formations for the winter offensive. A Nippon.com account similarly says Sorge’s September report
removed the eastern threat, allowing Stalin to transfer forces that halted the German advance.

Barbero is therefore presenting a widespread historical interpretation, not inventing an unusual
claim. The issue is that newer access to Sorge’s reports and closer analysis of Japanese policy
make the traditional causal sequence look much less certain.

## Japan had not made one clean decision

The first problem concerns what Japan had actually decided.

At the Imperial Conference of July 2, 1941, Japan prioritized expansion into southern French
Indochina. But the same decision also authorized secret preparations for war against the Soviet
Union. It was not a final choice of the south *instead of* the north. Both options remained alive.

On July 7, the Japanese General Staff initiated the Kantokuen mobilization for a possible Soviet
war. The plan envisioned twenty-five divisions, and the Kwantung Army was to grow from roughly
330,000 to as many as 850,000 personnel. Japan was visibly preparing a northern capability while
also advancing south.

Sorge’s own reporting reflects this ambiguity:

- On July 3, he transmitted one source’s expectation that Japan would enter the war against the
  USSR within five weeks.
- Ozaki simultaneously reported that Japan would honor the Soviet neutrality pact and was sending
  divisions to Saigon.
- Miyagi reported reinforcement of Japan’s northern and eastern positions.
- On August 7 and August 11, Sorge continued to warn about mobilization in both directions.
- On August 23, he reported that an offensive near Vladivostok remained possible.
- On September 14, he reported that Japan had *probably* abandoned a 1941 invasion, but that some
  700,000 troops would remain in Manchuria in preparation for possible action the following spring.
- In early October, the ring was still analyzing military transport and railway construction to
  determine whether the northern option had really been abandoned.

This was valuable intelligence, but it was not a categorical message that Siberia was safe.
Shimizu argues that Sorge’s reports accurately reflected Tokyo’s continuing indecision.

## Moscow did not necessarily treat Sorge as decisive

The second problem is how Moscow received the reports.

The Soviet leadership had institutional reasons to distrust Sorge. His former GRU patron, Jānis
Bērziņš, had been purged; Sorge’s security practices had previously been criticized; and senior GRU
officer Konstantin Kolganov instructed that his reports be checked against other sources.

Older accounts sometimes say that a GRU officer marked the decisive telegram “trustworthy.”
Shimizu notes that this annotated telegram does not appear in Andrey Fesyun’s modern documentary
collection. What does survive is Kolganov’s warning that Sorge’s material required corroboration.

The same pattern appears in the surviving reports about Operation Barbarossa. Sorge’s intelligence
reached the Soviet leadership, but GRU annotations classified some of it as doubtful. This does not
mean Moscow ignored everything Sorge sent. It means that the statement “Sorge reported it, so
Stalin believed it and ordered the transfer” requires evidence that has not been found.

The strongest missing link would be a Stavka order, memorandum, diary entry, or command discussion
explicitly connecting a redeployment decision to Sorge’s report. Current scholarship has not
identified such a document.

## Troops moved west, but not in one clean release

The revisionist argument is sometimes misunderstood as claiming that no eastern formations went
west. Some unquestionably did. Formations such as the 78th Rifle Division played important roles
around Moscow.

The disagreement concerns their scale, timing, origin, and cause. “Siberian divisions” is itself
imprecise: formations came from the Far Eastern Front, the Transbaikal Front, the Siberian Military
District, and newly raised reserves elsewhere east of European Russia. Transfers began before
Sorge’s supposedly decisive September report; others were ordered during October and November as
the crisis around Moscow developed.

The chronology therefore resembles a continuing national redeployment process more than a single
switch thrown in response to one intelligence report. Shimizu, drawing on Saburō Hayashi’s study of
the Kwantung Army and Soviet Far Eastern forces, concludes that there was no grand-scale relocation
to Moscow before winter. This does not deny individual transfers. It rejects the image of Stalin
suddenly emptying the eastern defenses once Sorge gave an all-clear.

## Why the Soviet Far East was not simply safe

Even when Sorge judged an attack during 1941 unlikely, Japan retained a huge army in Manchuria and
continued northern preparations. His September report itself envisaged roughly 700,000 troops
remaining there for possible action the next spring.

Moscow also knew that Japan had attacked Soviet-aligned Mongolia only two years earlier, that the
Kwantung Army had a record of acting without full civilian authorization, that the July policy
retained the northern option, and that Kantokuen was a real mobilization. Sorge continued reporting
northern military preparations into October.

His intelligence could reduce the estimated short-term danger without making the frontier safe.
Shimizu argues that Moscow could not feel fully relieved until Pearl Harbor made Japan’s southern
war irreversible in December 1941. That conclusion may itself be stated too categorically, but it
captures the central objection to the older account: September intelligence reduced uncertainty;
it did not eliminate it.

## Sorge’s place among the causes of the Soviet victory

Fresh eastern formations mattered at Moscow. So did Soviet reserves raised across the USSR,
formations transferred from other military districts, German attrition, overstretched supply
lines, Soviet defensive reorganization, weather, terrain, and the concentration of forces for the
December counteroffensive.

Soviet intelligence also had diplomatic reporting, signals intelligence, military observation,
and other human sources. Even if Sorge’s reports influenced the threat assessment, their individual
effect cannot be isolated from this larger body of information.

The evidence supports three propositions at different levels of confidence:

- Strongly supported: Sorge supplied valuable, high-level intelligence about Japanese policy.
- Plausible but difficult to isolate: his reports contributed to Moscow’s judgment that a Japanese
  attack during 1941 was becoming less likely.
- Not demonstrated: his September report caused Stalin to release the decisive formations that
  saved Moscow.

## Assessment

Shimizu’s study is important because it combines a modern collection of Sorge’s telegrams with
Japanese strategic records and military chronology. Its sources include Andrey Fesyun’s 2019
collection of Sorge telegrams and letters; the Japanese *Contemporary Historical Materials: The
Sorge Case*; Japanese official war history; the papers of Sorge-case prosecutor Ōta Taizō in the
National Diet Library; and Japanese studies of the Soviet and Kwantung armies.

It does not produce a Soviet document declaring that Sorge had no effect. Its conclusion rests in
part on a negative finding: no evidence has been found that his intelligence caused the Soviet
strategic decision. That justifies removing certainty, not replacing the traditional story with an
equally categorical denial.

C-019 is therefore best classified as **misleading compression**, not a simple factual error. The
intelligence and troop movements are real. The immediate and exclusive causal connection between
them is not established.

## Proposed recording narration

By mid-September, Sorge was reporting that Japan was unlikely to attack the Soviet Union that
year. His intelligence helped Moscow judge the danger in the east. Soviet formations were already
moving west, and several of them would fight around Moscow. Sorge had not single-handedly saved the
city—but his reports gave the Kremlin one more reason to take that calculated risk.

This wording preserves the drama and Sorge’s genuine achievement while distinguishing a plausible
contribution from an unproved sole cause.
