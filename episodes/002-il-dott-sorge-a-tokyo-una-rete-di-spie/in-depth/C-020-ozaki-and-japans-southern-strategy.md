# C-020: Did Hotsumi Ozaki help steer Japan toward war in the south?

## Research question

The lecture argues that Hotsumi Ozaki did more than report confidential Japanese deliberations to
Richard Sorge. As an influential participant in circles around Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro,
Ozaki repeatedly argued that attacking the Soviet Union was dangerous and that Japan should turn
against the Americans instead. Barbero says Ozaki thereby contributed to Japan’s strategic
decision.

This raises two separate questions:

1. Did Ozaki possess the access and opportunity to advocate policy inside Konoe’s circle?
2. Can his advocacy be shown to have materially changed Japan’s decision?

The first proposition is well supported. The second is plausible but cannot be isolated from the
much larger military, institutional, diplomatic, and economic forces driving Japanese policy.

## What Barbero says

The relevant passage is U-00815–U-00832, at original audio 55:42–56:53. In close English:

> Ozaki was an influential man, an adviser to the government, and Ozaki contributed to bringing
> about that decision. In every highly confidential discussion he participated in, Ozaki insisted:
> attacking the Soviet Union is dangerous; it isn’t worthwhile. Much better to attack the
> Americans.
>
> The Japanese prosecutors who interrogated them after their arrest were shocked. They almost had
> the impression that Japan’s war against America had been—not exactly caused, I wouldn’t say
> that—but that these damned Communists had helped things move in that direction.
>
> The Japanese prosecutors’ hair stood on end at the thought of what the country would say if it
> discovered that, in this mortal war we had entered, the Communists had led us by the nose and
> induced us to do what they wanted.
>
> So much so that, after the war, the prosecutors told the Americans: we decided not to investigate
> this aspect further because we were afraid that a revolution would break out if the country
> learned about it.

The passage operates at three levels:

- Barbero directly says that Ozaki “contributed to bringing about” the decision.
- He supplies a mechanism: Ozaki repeatedly advocated against a Soviet war in confidential
  discussions.
- He attributes the most dramatic interpretation—Communists leading Japan by the nose—to the
  Japanese prosecutors.

Crucially, Barbero expressly stops short of saying that Ozaki caused the Pacific War: “not exactly
caused, I wouldn’t say that.” The editorial decision at the end of this article treats that hedge as
sufficient.

## Ozaki’s access is firmly established

Ozaki was not an ordinary newspaper informant. He had been an *Asahi Shimbun* correspondent and a
recognized specialist on China. He participated in the Shōwa Research Association and belonged to
policy circles around Konoe. Those relationships gave him access to confidential deliberations at
the highest civilian levels.

Sorge’s report of April 18, 1941 demonstrates the quality of this access. It describes Ozaki
visiting Konoe when the prime minister received news of the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact and
includes Konoe’s immediate telephone conversation with Army Minister Tōjō Hideki.

Ozaki also learned the outcome of the July 2 Imperial Conference almost immediately, enabling
Sorge to report it to Moscow on July 3. That report correctly conveyed both halves of Japan’s
ambiguous policy: expansion into southern Indochina and continued preparation for a possible war
against the Soviet Union.

There is therefore no serious reason to doubt that Ozaki heard confidential policy discussions and
could express political views among people connected with Konoe.

## Ozaki was an advocate, not merely a collector

Ozaki was a committed Marxist intellectual with a developed political vision for East Asia. He
opposed Japanese imperialism, hoped for political transformation in Japan and China, and believed
that war against the Soviet Union would be disastrous.

It is credible that he argued for those views rather than silently recording the opinions of
others. He had both the conviction and the opportunity to advocate against the northern strategy.
The evidence therefore supports describing him as more than a passive source.

What is harder to establish is the magnitude of his influence. An adviser may participate in a
discussion, argue consistently, and even anticipate the eventual outcome without being a decisive
cause of that outcome.

## Where Japanese policy was formally made

Japan’s decisive national-policy choices were not made by Konoe’s informal advisers. They emerged
from the Imperial Headquarters–Government Liaison Conference and were then submitted for imperial
ratification. The Cabinet, Army, Navy, War Ministry, Navy Ministry, and their general staffs brought
different powers and interests to this process.

Moriyama Atsushi’s study of the September 1941 *Guidelines for Implementing National Policy*
describes a system that often resolved conflict by incorporating incompatible positions into the
same document. The Army, Navy, Cabinet, and general staffs compromised without eliminating their
disagreements. The resulting policy simultaneously pursued negotiations and prepared for war.

Konoe’s civilian advisers could shape his analysis and arguments, but they did not command the
armed services or vote as members of the principal decision-making conference. No record currently
in the episode ledger identifies a formal decision that changed because of an intervention by
Ozaki.

## The southern strategy did not originate with Ozaki

The southward advance was already a major current in Japanese strategic thought. Ozaki neither
invented it nor introduced it into government.

In April 1941, American ambassador Joseph Grew described two widely visible schools of Japanese
opinion. One wanted immediate southern expansion while the Axis position was strong. The other
preferred slower penetration to avoid premature war with both the Soviet Union and the United
States. The debate existed across official, semiofficial, and public political life months before
the decisive summer crisis.

The principal forces pushing Japan south included:

- the continuing and costly war in China;
- dependence on imported oil, rubber, and other strategic resources;
- the resources of Southeast Asia and the Dutch East Indies;
- the Navy’s institutional priorities;
- occupation of southern French Indochina;
- the American freezing of Japanese assets and effective oil embargo;
- the failure of negotiations with Washington;
- and institutional competition between the Army and Navy.

Ozaki could advocate in the same direction as these forces. The available evidence cannot measure
his independent effect against them.

## Japan did not immediately abandon the northern option

The July 2 Imperial Conference prioritized the south but also authorized secret preparations for
war against the USSR. Five days later, the General Staff began the Kantokuen mobilization, which
greatly strengthened the Kwantung Army for a possible northern campaign.

Sorge’s reports continued to reflect uncertainty. On August 23, he reported that an offensive near
Vladivostok remained possible. His September assessment said an invasion during 1941 had probably
been abandoned, while hundreds of thousands of Japanese troops remained in Manchuria for possible
action the following spring.

This is relevant to Ozaki’s influence. If he had successfully persuaded Japan to reject the
northern strategy outright, a clearer institutional result might be expected. Instead, Japan
continued to preserve both options while events and resource pressures increasingly drew it south.

## The prosecutors’ interpretation

The most dramatic part of Barbero’s account comes from the Japanese prosecutorial interpretation:
that Communist penetration of Konoe’s circle may have helped maneuver Japan into war against the
United States, and that public disclosure might have provoked revolutionary anger.

This interpretation is historically significant, but it is not neutral proof of Ozaki’s causal
power. Wartime investigators were constructing a treason and espionage case. Presenting the ring as
capable of distorting national policy increased the gravity of the offense and explained how a
deeply placed network had operated for years.

A later investigation by the Yokohama Bar Association concluded that the prosecution had expanded
Japanese security law improperly. It argued that some allegedly secret information consisted of
newspaper reporting plus Ozaki’s judgments and opinions, rather than demonstrably protected state
secrets. This does not mean Ozaki was innocent of intelligence work; it cautions against accepting
every prosecutorial inference as an objective account of policymaking.

The specific postwar statement about fear of revolution remains a deferred quotation target. The
translated case records and postwar testimony exist, but this research pass did not recover the
precise original wording with an exact locator.

## Reporting, advocacy, and causation

Ozaki’s exceptional accuracy can itself create an impression of causal influence:

1. He learned what senior figures were discussing.
2. He analyzed the balance among competing policies.
3. He advocated his own preferred course.
4. He reported the likely outcome to Sorge.
5. Events developed in the direction he had predicted and supported.

Accurate prediction and advocacy do not, by themselves, prove that his intervention changed the
outcome. Demonstrating that stronger claim would require evidence such as minutes recording a
consequential Ozaki intervention, a policymaker’s diary crediting his advice, testimony that a
decision changed because of him, or a traceable proposal drafted by Ozaki and adopted by the
government. No such evidence has been identified in the present research ledger.

The evidence supports a graduated assessment:

- Certain: Ozaki penetrated Konoe’s political circle and reported confidential deliberations.
- Strongly supported: he opposed war with the USSR and favored the southern alternative.
- Plausible: he advocated these views in politically connected discussions.
- Unproved: his advocacy materially changed the policy of the Army, Navy, Cabinet, or Imperial
  Conference.
- Too strong without qualification: Ozaki or the Sorge ring caused Japan to attack the United
  States.

## Editorial decision

**Decision: retain Barbero’s original interpretation.**

Barbero does not claim that Ozaki single-handedly caused the Pacific War. He explicitly interrupts
the causal suggestion with “not exactly caused, I wouldn’t say that,” then presents the more
dramatic language as the horrified interpretation of the Japanese prosecutors.

That hedge is sufficient for the episode’s narrative register. The surrounding passage attributes
influence rather than sole causation, while preserving the historically important fact that
investigators themselves feared Communist penetration may have affected policy.

When `accuracy-notes.yaml` is created after faithful translation, C-020 should therefore receive
`decision: retain-original`. The unresolved quotation provenance for the prosecutors’ postwar
statement should remain disclosed in `quotes.yaml`; it does not require removal of Barbero’s
contextual wording.
