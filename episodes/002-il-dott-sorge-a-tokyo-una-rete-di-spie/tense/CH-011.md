## 11. The Tokyo ring is uncovered

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00844–U-00910 -->

That same day Ozaki is arrested. Three days later Sorge, Clausen and all the others are arrested. Sorge’s network falls almost by chance—or rather, it is so extensive that sooner or later the Japanese are bound to find a thread. But they find it accidentally and spend a very long time following it.

Two years earlier, in November 1939, the Tokkō—the Japanese political police—arrests and tortures a member of the underground Communist Party. Required to name other Communists, he names a Mrs. Kitabayashi, a former emigrant who had lived in Los Angeles before returning to Japan. The Tokkō places this elderly woman and her husband under surveillance. They watch the couple for two years, hoping something will happen, but find nothing. Eventually, in the autumn of 1941, they tire of waiting and arrest them.

Ordered to name Communists, Mrs. Kitabayashi searches her memory and recalls a Japanese Communist she had known in California named Miyagi. The secret police have never heard of him. They arrest him. Miyagi is the first Japanese Sorge recruited into his network. Under torture at police headquarters, he throws himself from a window—the sort of thing that happens in every country, as you see. He fails to kill himself and breaks a leg. The police bring him back upstairs. The next day he begins naming names: Sorge, Ozaki, Max Clausen.

Even without this, Sorge might not have lasted much longer. The Berlin archives show that in 1940 the Nazi Party, rummaging through its old files, rediscovers information that Sorge had been a Communist in his youth and becomes suspicious. Walter Schellenberg of the German security service makes inquiries. But Sorge appears extraordinarily valuable: a loyal Nazi and intimate friend of Ambassador Ott, who says he needs him badly. The Germans therefore leave Sorge alone, merely warning Ott, without names or commitments, to watch the people around him.

Ott worries that the embassy contains a mole. He tells nobody except his best friend, Doctor Sorge. The Germans trust Sorge so completely that when the Japanese secret police arrest him, the embassy formally protests the detention of a distinguished member of Tokyo’s German community. Ambassador Ott visits him in prison, and for quite some time the embassy presses the Japanese to release him. Surely it has to be a mistake.

The Japanese public, from the little it learns in the newspapers, assumes Sorge has been arrested as a German spy; allies naturally spy on one another too. The Germans have complete faith in him. As Sorge had once said, “I believe I am leading them all by the nose.” When the truth emerges in Berlin, Schellenberg is summoned by Heinrich Himmler for what he later calls a very unpleasant conversation. Sorge and Ozaki are arrested and brought to trial in a long proceeding.
