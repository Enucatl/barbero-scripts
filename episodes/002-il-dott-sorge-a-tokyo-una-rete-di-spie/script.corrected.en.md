# Dr. Sorge in Tokyo: A Spy Ring — corrected English translation

## 1. The crisis inside the Japanese army and the hidden journalist

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00124 -->

Good evening, thank you. Well, we are still here; we are not going anywhere. So: Tokyo, in the summer of 1935. Get your bearings. We are between the two wars. The First is already receding into the past, while the Second is already very close. The world is already plunging toward the Second World War.

We are in Tokyo in the summer of 1935. In Italy there is Mussolini, at the height of his popularity. In Germany there is Hitler. In the Soviet Union there is Stalin. In the United States there is Roosevelt, and the end of the Great Depression is beginning to come into view. In Japan, things are happening that seem very strange to Western eyes.

One day in July 1935, a certain Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa takes the train to Tokyo from the town where he is stationed. He goes to the War Ministry and looks for the office of one of its officials, General Nagata. He enters the office, draws his sword, and kills General Nagata. At his trial Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa will say that he is ashamed of only one thing: that he did not manage to kill him with a single stroke of the sword, as a properly trained samurai should.

The trial reveals that this murder inside the Japanese War Ministry is not a private matter. It is connected with a conflict tearing the Japanese army apart. On one side are the older, moderate generals in power, tied to Japan’s great industrial and financial combines. On the other is an entire generation of young officers, often from farming or otherwise modest backgrounds: extremists with strong fascist sympathies.

Japan has a tradition of conflicts of this kind. There is a Japanese term dating back to the fifteenth century, *gekokujō*, meaning the overthrow of the elders by the young. That is what is happening in Japan. The young officers are seething and want to seize power.

Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa’s murder trial lasts for months. Then one day during the trial, in February 1936, a group of young officers brings the soldiers out of their barracks. They occupy the War Ministry, Parliament, and police headquarters. They attack the homes of the prime minister and several cabinet ministers. Two ministers are killed in their own homes. The prime minister survives only because the young officers who burst into his house find his brother-in-law, mistake him for the owner, and kill him instead.

The rebel officers then announce that they have taken action out of a sense of duty to the emperor, and they do nothing else—as though they expect Emperor Hirohito to intervene on their behalf. For two days the government negotiates with the rebels, almost as if it recognizes them as a legitimate counterpart. Meanwhile, it has all the occupied buildings surrounded, mainly by naval troops. Everyone knows that in Japan the navy and the army hate each other. After three days the rebels surrender. Most are tried, sentenced to death, and executed.

The Western governments make a great—well, I am not going to stop now and ask how clear it is to you what was happening in Japan at that moment. It was not clear at all to the Western governments. Nobody understood a thing. Japan was a distant, complicated country. Nobody knew the language. It was impenetrable, communicated little with the outside world, and in the West there were very few specialists, if any, who understood the inner workings of this society in crisis. Even the handful of European journalists in Tokyo were groping in the dark.

There was only one journalist capable of explaining what was happening. He was a German journalist who had been accredited in Tokyo for a couple of years, a member of the Nazi Party, on excellent terms with his embassy, and possessed of a strangely thorough knowledge of Japanese politics.

After the young officers’ insurrection, this journalist publishes—or rather sends back to Germany from Tokyo—a major article explaining these complicated dynamics very effectively: this struggle of class and generation within the army. The article is a great success and is reproduced around the world. Even *Pravda* in Moscow translates it into Russian.

The journalist’s friends congratulate him. “You have become famous all over the world. You are the expert on Japan even for those Communists in Moscow.” The journalist laughs, but inwardly he is furious. That evening he visits another German, a small industrialist established in a suburb of Tokyo. Hidden inside his house, the industrialist keeps a radio transmitter capable of reaching Siberia, where the transmission can be received and relayed to Moscow.

The journalist calls Moscow and says, “Have you lost your minds, publishing my articles in *Pravda*? Do not let it happen again. Nobody must imagine that there is any connection between me and the Soviet Union.”

The journalist’s name was Richard Sorge. He had been a member of the Communist Party since 1918, and he headed a spy ring planted in Tokyo by Red Army intelligence. Of all the spy networks that operated during the First and Second World Wars, this was perhaps the one that most directly influenced the outcome of the Second World War.

## 2. Richard Sorge from the Western Front to the Comintern

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00125–U-00194 -->

And it is Dr. Sorge and his spy ring that I will try to tell you about this evening.

Richard Sorge was the son of a German engineer and a Russian mother. At high school in Berlin he was first in his class in every subject—one of those boys who, let us say, are generally not especially popular with their classmates. His schoolmates nicknamed him “the prime minister.” Yet like all the young German students of his generation, born at the end of the nineteenth century, he was a good German patriot. When the First World War broke out, he volunteered and spent four years fighting at the front.

He was wounded three times in the legs, the last time very seriously, and remained slightly lame for the rest of his life. And, like so many others, Richard Sorge changed during the war. He encountered people whom a bourgeois boy like him would never previously have met: pacifists, Social Democrats, and Communists. He became a Communist himself. Perhaps the fact that he had lost two brothers in the war also played some part in directing him that way.

In any event, immediately after the war, Sorge—still in his twenties—joins the Communist Party. The following year he earns a degree in political science. From then on he will be Dr. Sorge. By this point the German police have already registered him as a Communist agitator.

I stress this because, as we will shortly see, ten years later nobody in the German police remembers him. The police—the Germans—are not always as efficient as we sometimes imagine. This case is a spectacular example: they lost his record. In 1920 they knew perfectly well that this young Dr. Sorge was a Communist.

The Germans forget about him; the Soviets notice him. He also speaks Russian—his mother is Russian—and eventually they invite him to Moscow to work for the Comintern, the organization coordinating all the Communist parties of the world. He accepts.

In 1925, at the age of twenty-nine, Dr. Sorge leaves Germany and settles in Moscow. At first he stays at the famous Hotel Lux, reserved for foreign Communists. Togliatti stayed there too, along with many people who later came to a bad end. That same year Sorge takes Soviet citizenship and a Soviet Communist Party card.

He has brought his wife Christiane with him. She is German and had been married to one of his university professors before leaving her husband for Sorge. He takes her to the Soviet Union, but things do not go very well there. Christiane does not like it. Eventually she leaves him and returns to Germany.

Christiane now leaves our story, but I wanted to mention her because she later left a very instructive observation about Richard Sorge. Many years afterward, people asked her about her affair with her husband’s student: had he seduced her? Christiane said—she called him by his childhood nickname, Ika. As a child learning to speak, Richard had difficulty pronouncing his name. Instead of saying “Richard,” he said “Ika,” and his family had called him that ever since.

Christiane said: “Ika did not seduce me at all. No, no, I was the one who fell in love. Ika never needed to court anyone. Everyone flocked around him, men and women.”

And so, during the 1920s, this man whom everyone liked and who fascinated everyone was repeatedly sent on missions abroad.

## 3. Shanghai and the assignment to build a Tokyo network

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00195–U-00299 -->

On behalf of the Comintern, Sorge works among dockworkers in Scandinavia and miners in England. Then, in 1929, his destiny changes. He is summoned to the office of General Jānis Bērziņš, commander of the Fourth Department of Red Army intelligence, the department responsible for establishing networks of agents abroad. General Bērziņš proposes that Dr. Sorge go to Shanghai and establish a spy network in China.

It is 1929. China is boiling. Chiang Kai-shek rules in theory, but in reality much of the country is controlled by warlords. There is a powerful underground Communist movement, and the Japanese are waiting at the borders for an opportunity to begin their invasion. Moscow badly needs intelligence about China but has no legal means of obtaining it. There is not even a Soviet consulate, and Chiang Kai-shek kills every Communist he can lay his hands on.

Bērziņš asks Sorge to enter a country like that and find out whether a Communist spy network can be built there. Dr. Sorge accepts. He goes to China presenting himself as a journalist who intends to write reports—and he genuinely writes them.

He settles in Shanghai. I will not dwell on it. When I was preparing this lecture, I told myself I would spend ten minutes on Shanghai because it is too fascinating. Instead, not even one minute, because there is no time. But imagine the city: theoretically under Chinese sovereignty, while half of Shanghai consists of Western concessions governed and policed by Western countries. There is a particularly large French Concession controlled by French police. Immediately outside are the Japanese, continually threatening invasion. There are underground Communists, rich Western industrialists and financiers—a mad world of Western restaurants and German bookshops.

Dr. Sorge remains in this world for three years. He sends Germany excellent analyses of the Chinese situation and makes a name for himself as an expert on the Far East. He sends an enormous quantity of intelligence to Moscow.

He returns to Moscow in 1932. He is welcomed with hearty handshakes and asked whether he is ready to leave on another mission. Dr. Sorge says that first he would like to finish the book he has started on Chinese agriculture. He is someone who becomes genuinely interested in things. The scholar’s soul is there: he has a degree in political science, he is an economist, and whenever he lives in a country he begins studying it. He is writing a book on Chinese agriculture, so please leave him alone until he has finished it.

General Bērziņš says, all right, if you really must finish the book, how long do you think—anyway, two months later they summon him again and ask whether the book is finished. It is not. But Red Army intelligence is eager to send Dr. Sorge back into the world. They ask, “Where shall we send you next?”

Sorge jokes, “Well, I have been in China. I could go to Japan. While I was in Shanghai, I made a little trip to Tokyo. I liked it; it is an interesting country. I could return there, if you insist.” He is joking. A few days later General Bērziņš calls him back and says he repeated the joke to the Central Committee of the Soviet Communist Party, where they took it very seriously. They badly need agents in Tokyo.

Consider what kind of country Japan is at that moment. Beyond being opaque—indeed, impenetrable to Westerners—it is openly xenophobic, obsessed with spies, and every foreigner is followed by the police, sometimes by several police forces. There is the Kempeitai, the army’s military police, and the Tokkō, the secret police equivalent of the Gestapo. It is fiercely anti-Communist. And it borders the Soviet Union.

Remember the Russo-Japanese War of 1905. It sounds like another age, but not even thirty years have passed. The Japanese are firmly established in Korea and Manchuria and face the Soviet Union across the frontier. In Manchuria stands a powerful Japanese force, the Kwantung Army. It behaves almost like an independent power, conducting its own policy toward China and the Soviet Union and barely obeying the Japanese government. You have understood that this is a time when, if the generals tire of the Japanese prime minister, they have him killed.

Moscow urgently needs to know what Japan intends to do, and nobody understands it. It needs to know the mood of the Kwantung Army and whether the borders of Mongolia and Manchuria are in danger. Yet the Soviet Union has no agents at all in Japan. Nobody even knows whether it is possible to have one, whether a radio transmitter can communicate from Japan to the Soviet Union, or whether Japanese Communists can be recruited and persuaded to work for Moscow.

They tell Dr. Sorge, “This experiment needs to be attempted. If you like, go there, stay quietly for a couple of years, and see whether it can be done.” The idea greatly interests Ika. It excites him. He will go.

## 4. A German journalist constructs his Nazi cover

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00300–U-00390 -->

So Dr. Sorge must be sent from Moscow to Tokyo, into a fiercely anti-Communist country with no relations with the Soviet Union. Sorge has a very clear principle: the fewer lies you tell, the harder you are to expose. If possible, you should not tell any lies at all. He will therefore go to Tokyo as Dr. Richard Sorge, journalist and expert on the Far East. There is only one small detail: naturally, he cannot arrive from Russia.

He returns to Berlin in May 1933. Notice the date: Hitler has been in power for only a few months. Relations with the Soviet Union still exist. Germany and the Soviet Union are both regarded, so to speak, as pariahs of the international community, and at that moment they maintain relations.

In Berlin, Sorge applies for a passport to Japan. Since he already writes newspaper articles about the East, he visits German newspapers and asks whether they would like reports from Tokyo. Several are interested.

He does something else. At the time Germany is home to a curious and very famous figure, Professor Karl Haushofer, a world-renowned scholar of geopolitics and strategy—a sort of Edward Luttwak, to give you the idea. He is also a soldier who reached the rank of general during the First World War, something Luttwak would probably appreciate, although I do not think anyone ever made him a general. Haushofer is enormously respected in Germany and is an expert on Japan. Before the First World War he had served there as a German military instructor.

Haushofer argues that Germany lost the First World War because Germans were ignorant of the world’s geopolitical situation. Germany and Japan were enemies in that war. He theorizes that next time they must unite; only together can they succeed. These are not merely an academic’s theories, because Haushofer happens to be a close friend of Rudolf Hess, Hitler’s right-hand man, and can therefore exert a concrete influence on Nazi German policy.

One could devote an entire lecture to Haushofer. I will make a small digression because he is too fascinating. You understand the type: a hard-line German patriot dreaming of revenge. His small problem is that his wife is half Jewish. Through his friendship with Hess, however, he obtains a certificate of Aryan status for his wife and children, placing them out of danger—almost.

I am jumping ahead, but the story is tragic and compelling. In 1944 one of Haushofer’s sons, a young officer, will be implicated in the July 20 plot against Hitler and executed. In 1945, after Germany’s defeat, Professor Haushofer and his half-Jewish wife will kill themselves with arsenic.

But all that is still eleven years away. It is 1933, and the brilliant young journalist Dr. Sorge visits the illustrious professor in Munich. He explains that he is going to Tokyo and asks whether Haushofer is interested. Of course he is. Can he provide letters of introduction? Of course; Haushofer knows everyone.

Sorge will leave Berlin carrying a letter from this great Nazi intellectual to the German ambassador in Tokyo. And not only that. Today one flies to Japan—it takes time, but one gets there. In those days one travels by ship through America. Since Sorge will stop in the United States, Haushofer asks whether he would also like a letter to the Japanese ambassador in Washington. Of course he would.

Thus Sorge, a Soviet spy, leaves Berlin equipped with letters from prominent Nazis for every destination that might be useful. The passport worries him more, because obtaining it means going to the police, and ten years earlier the police knew perfectly well who Richard Sorge was: a registered Communist.

He takes the risk and goes to the Berlin police. They have no idea who he is and issue the passport without difficulty. The Nazi regime has only just been installed; the Gestapo is still getting into its stride and evidently has not perfected its methods. The young Communist agitator has disappeared, though the name is unchanged. Now there is a famous German journalist leaving for Tokyo.

Before departing, Sorge also applies to join the Nazi Party. He is so sure of himself that, after arriving in Tokyo, he sends his first message to Moscow: “I believe I can lead them all by the nose.” And that is exactly what he does.

Nobody is led by the nose more completely than the German embassy.

We are in 1933. We are accustomed to thinking of the alliance between Germany and Japan, the Rome–Berlin–Tokyo Axis—we joined it too, God forgive us. But in 1933 nothing like that exists. To the Japanese, Germany is simply another European country, and one that had been their enemy during the First World War.

## 5. Eugen Ott and access to the German embassy

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00391–U-00470 -->

Imagine the German diplomats in Tokyo, living in a country they scarcely understand themselves. It is 1933; Hitler and the Nazi Party have just come to power in Germany, and the career diplomats are by no means all Nazis. There they are in Tokyo, trying to understand what is happening at home, when this gift from heaven drops into their laps: a delightful compatriot, extremely well connected and well informed, who comes in and explains everything. He also knows far more about Japan than the German diplomats stationed there. They adore him. He never needs to ask them for help; as his wife Christiane used to say, Ika never needs to ask anyone for anything—they all come to him.

They ask whether he wants letters of introduction to the Japanese government. “I already have them,” Sorge says. How? “I went to the Japanese embassy in Washington and they gave me some.” At the German embassy their eyes widen: this is a marvelous man, someone to treasure. Doctor Sorge is lucky too. One of the people for whom he receives an introduction is Colonel Ott, a German instructor with the Japanese army. Sorge goes to see him. Again, picture Colonel Ott and his wife living in a provincial Japanese city, knowing nobody and kept at arm’s length. Then this cheerful, famous, affluent, immensely likable compatriot arrives from Berlin, and they immediately become the closest of friends.

Not even the trial established whether there was also an affair between Doctor Sorge and Colonel Ott’s wife; that remained gossip. But they certainly become intimate friends. They play chess, both are veterans, and a solid friendship develops. Why did I say Sorge was lucky? Six months later Ott is promoted and transferred to Tokyo as military attaché. A few years later the German ambassador is recalled to the Foreign Ministry on Wilhelmstrasse. Berlin has no Japan expert to replace him, so it promotes Colonel Ott, who becomes German ambassador in Tokyo—and his greatest and most faithful friend is Doctor Sorge.

These are the years when Germany and Japan are drawing together. Sorge is the ambassador’s intimate friend and comes and goes at the embassy as though it were his own home. When Ott needs to put a particularly complicated dispatch into cipher, he asks Doctor Sorge to help. When he sends a report to Berlin, he asks Sorge to lend a hand writing it. When confidential papers arrive, he says, “Would you like a look too?” Of course. Sometimes Sorge says, “This is really interesting. May I take it home?” Certainly—and at home Sorge photographs it.

At one point Ambassador Ott asks whether he should appoint Sorge head of the embassy press office. It is the one occasion on which Sorge behaves a little oddly. He knows that an official embassy post would have to be reported to the ministry in Berlin, where someone might dig into his past again. He does not like that prospect, so he tells Ott that he does not want an official position, though naturally he will continue helping with secret dispatches. Ott is disappointed, then accepts it. The moral is that one of Soviet spy Richard Sorge’s principal sources throughout those years is the German embassy, where he is entirely at home and sees everything.

The embassy diplomats also like him because he is extraordinarily skillful politically. He has applied to join the Nazi Party, and his membership card duly reaches him in Tokyo. There is a marvelous detail that shows what sort of organization the Nazi Party was. In the Berlin archives historians found Richard Sorge’s membership record in the overseas section. All it says is: name, Richard Sorge; residence, Tokyo, China. Someone has crossed out “China” in pencil and written “Japan.” That is everything the Nazi Party knows about its new member.

But in Tokyo Sorge understands that these German diplomats are not fanatical Nazis. He plays the Nazi, but a witty, critical one who makes jokes and with whom they can speak freely, and they love him for it. At one point the Tokyo Nazi organization wants to make him its leader. He discusses it with Ambassador Ott, who jokes that the Party could occasionally use a man with common sense. Sorge nevertheless refuses the post. He prefers to remain unofficial. And then there is the network—the network of Japanese agents.

## 6. Hotsumi Ozaki and the Japanese agents

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00471–U-00559 -->

Why does Sorge need Japanese agents? What we call espionage, even today, largely consists not of stealing secret documents by breaking safes and codes but of working with what the trade calls open sources. Newspapers publish an enormous amount. So do specialist journals and company reports. It is all public, but reading, comparing, checking it and writing reports is a vast undertaking. Every intelligence network has to do it.

In Japan in the 1930s, even working with these so-called open sources is far from easy. Hardly any foreigner knows Japanese well; Sorge learns some, but never masters it. More importantly, Japan is obsessed with spies. Foreigners are followed and watched, and newsagents and booksellers are forbidden to sell them books or magazines dealing with Japan’s internal affairs. If a foreigner asks about such publications, the bookseller has a duty to report him to the police. So even information freely available to Japanese readers is difficult for a foreigner to collect. Sorge therefore needs Japanese collaborators, and a genuine network grows around him.

Sorge had spent three years in Shanghai and met Japanese people there, some of them Communists whom he had approached under a false name and tested for willingness to work for communism. He now re-establishes contact. There is a whole novelistic story I would like to tell—someone instructed to appear in Nara Park on a certain day wearing a green tie; another man with a brown tie and a newspaper in his right pocket; the phrase, “Fine weather today, isn’t it?” That is how Sorge reconnects with people he knew before.

I will mention only one, because he becomes the most important, almost Sorge’s alter ego, and later plays a major part at the trial. He is a famous journalist for Japan’s leading newspaper, the *Asahi Shimbun*. His name is Hotsumi Ozaki. He too is a China expert, closely connected to Japanese political society, acquainted with ministers and prime ministers, and eventually appointed an official government adviser on Chinese affairs.

Remember the Kwantung Army in Manchuria, which behaves almost like an independent power and represents the principal threat along the Soviet frontier. It naturally has close relationships with financial and industrial combines, foremost among them the South Manchuria Railway. Ozaki is appointed to its research organization, putting him in close contact with the Kwantung Army’s decision-making world. Publicly, Ozaki is known, respected and famous in Japan. Secretly, he is a Communist; when approached, he confirms that he is willing to work for the Comintern and provide information.

Other Japanese Communists are found in the interesting world of Japanese emigrants in California. In the 1920s and 1930s the Communist Party has a real presence in the United States, before war and especially McCarthyism sweep much of it away. A number of Japanese immigrants become Communists. Moscow’s agents seek them out, ask whether they are willing to work, and send more than one back to Japan to contact Doctor Sorge. He eventually has many agents. All act from ideological conviction: there is very little money and enormous danger. Not one betrays him voluntarily.

The extraordinary thing is that Sorge lives completely in the open and violates almost no Japanese law. Secretly photographing German embassy papers might concern the Germans, but the Japanese scarcely care. Years later, when he is arrested, it is not initially obvious what law can be used against him. Japan has, however, recently passed a law imposing death on anyone who communicates information to a Communist country; that will provide the legal basis for condemning him.

The information can be sent on microfilm by courier to Shanghai, a cosmopolitan city of travelers and grand hotels. There too the meetings take place by methods straight out of a novel.

## 7. Max Clausen and the clandestine radio

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00560–U-00638 -->

“On the appointed day, go to the Imperial Hotel bar with a yellow book and place it on the right of your table. You will see someone with a green book on the left of his. Look at one another, say nothing, and meet that evening in such-and-such a street.” That is how it all works. But microfilm cannot be carried to Shanghai every day, while Moscow wants constant information. The world situation is changing, war is drawing closer, and Moscow is desperately hungry for intelligence. So they need radio, and radio requires a European technician.

The crucial technician Sorge eventually receives is another German, Max Clausen, from Hamburg, Germany’s reddest city. Clausen had been a wireless operator in the imperial army during the Great War, then shipped out on fishing boats as a radio operator. On one of them a Soviet agent approaches him, asks whether he is still a Communist and whether he is willing to work for communism. Clausen says yes. They give him a first-class ticket to Moscow and a Thomas Mann novel, telling him that his destination is inside it. Underlined passages form a coded message—an easy enough code, presumably because if he cannot solve it they have no use for him.

Clausen deciphers it on the first-class train, reaches Moscow, goes to the indicated address and is hired. He receives advanced radio training and becomes a virtuoso. Sorge has grown tired of his previous operator, who is too frightened and hardly ever transmits, so Clausen is sent to Tokyo.

He travels with his wife, because spies arouse less suspicion when accompanied by wives; all the European members of Sorge’s group have one. In his luggage Clausen carries precisely two radio valves, models for the others he must obtain. He buys every other component in Tokyo—not in radio shops, which might attract suspicion, but in shops selling electric lamps. It takes three months. Soviet intelligence works for the long term.

With those components he builds a transmitter that can be dismantled in five minutes, reassembled in ten, and carried in a small bag. He can transmit from different houses across the city without being pinned down. He too needs a cover. Since he is a worker, they invent a business for him: Clausen moves to Tokyo as a small industrialist and opens a firm manufacturing specialized photographic machinery for technical drawings—something whose exact purpose even I have never quite understood.

The cover business thrives. It makes a great deal of money and eventually employs fourteen people. This becomes a problem: Clausen, the former worker turned proprietor, has less time for spying and begins to find successful industrial life much more interesting. Toward the end he wavers, though he continues his intelligence work. Moscow then makes a dramatic mistake. Money is scarce, and someone says that since Clausen’s company is so profitable, the network should use his money. His feelings are hurt—the classic proletarian who has become a boss.

Otherwise the arrangement works splendidly. Clausen is an expert operator, able to transmit to the Soviet Union. From the summer of 1938, as the world plunges toward war, Moscow tells the group: transmit whenever you wish. During the first quarter-hour of every hour, twenty-four hours a day, someone will be listening on your frequency.

## 8. Sorge's double life in Tokyo

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00639–U-00702 -->

Meanwhile Doctor Sorge lives very well in Tokyo. He is charming company and everyone knows him. He adopts a Japanese way of life, renting a small Japanese house with a garden and a Japanese housekeeper who cooks for him. At home he wears a kimono and sleeps on tatami. He fills the house with every book he can obtain on Japanese history and economics; when he is arrested, the police find more than a thousand books about Japan. He bathes every day in a wooden Japanese tub. His German embassy friends find this slightly astonishing, because daily bathing has occurred to almost no European of that era.

By day he lives like a Japanese. At night he drinks with his German friends. Doctor Sorge is a magnificent drinker, and Tokyo is an extraordinary city: xenophobia and secret police dominate it, yet it is full of Western nightspots. Restaurants for foreigners employ Japanese waitresses who dye their hair blond or orange, until the secret police prohibit this unpatriotic practice. German beer halls have names like the Rheingold, where Sorge is often seen in the small hours, roaring drunk with friends from the German embassy. There are luxurious Japanese restaurants with geishas and less luxurious ones with more available girls.

Sorge stays out all night, gets drunk and sleeps with women. Eventually he finds a steady companion, Hanako, a waitress at the Rheingold. She becomes his lover. He takes her out of the restaurant and asks what she would like to do. She wants to study languages, so he pays for her education. Hanako remains faithful to Sorge without knowing anything of his true identity. After the war she often speaks of him: “I knew perfectly well that he wasn’t in love with me, but he was so kind.” She, however, is desperately in love with him.

There is a problem in all this. Heavy drinking carries risks. When Sorge drinks he becomes noisy and sometimes brutal. The hostesses of the small European community in Tokyo begin to distrust him. He is a great friend of their husbands, but they stop inviting him to dinner because drink makes him unpleasant. It seems strange that a man of his caliber should have such a weakness, but the strain of a double identity must be discharged somehow, and Sorge discharges it through alcohol.

A woman who knew him as a young man in Berlin and meets him again in 1930s Tokyo says: “I had known him as a romantic, idealistic student, but he had become a violent man and a heavy drinker. He was still very handsome, but his cheekbones and mouth had grown hollow, his nose had sharpened; he was completely changed.” Soon his appearance changes further.

Another of Sorge’s passions is racing madly on a motorcycle he bought from Max Clausen. One night, after drinking heavily, he crashes into a wall near the American embassy. Japanese policemen arrive. He has just enough time to ask them to summon Max Clausen. At the hospital, Sorge gives Clausen his house keys: he is a prominent German citizen, so embassy officials will secure his home, and Clausen must get there first and remove compromising papers. Then Sorge loses consciousness.

He wakes with a fractured jaw and several teeth gone. An acquaintance says that after he leaves hospital, the scars make him resemble a Japanese theatrical mask and give his face an almost demonic expression.

## 9. The Anti-Comintern Pact and the war in Mongolia

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00703–U-00770 -->

So far I have not gone into the details of the information Sorge and his people supplied to Moscow. But as I said, if any spy network influenced the course of the Second World War, it was Sorge’s. When he first went to Tokyo, the Soviet Union’s great problem was obtaining intelligence about Japan and Japanese policy. While he was there, however, the alliance between Germany and Japan came into being—an alliance decidedly directed against the Soviet Union, so much so that it was called the Anti-Comintern Pact.

Germany and Japan negotiated this alliance in total secrecy. On the German side even the Foreign Ministry was not informed. Foreign Minister von Ribbentrop handled the negotiation personally; his ministry knew nothing, and neither did the German embassy in Tokyo. Eventually Ambassador Ott picked up hints of what was happening and decided to write to Berlin: kindly explain to me, the ambassador in Tokyo, whether we really are forming an alliance with Japan.

But the matter was too large and exciting to keep to himself, so Ott told his dear friend Doctor Sorge and asked him to help encipher the dispatch. Berlin replied. In substance, Moscow learned of the negotiations almost before the German Foreign Ministry did. That is one example.

Moscow also needed to know what the Kwantung Army intended. Japan believed it had to expand or suffocate, but in which direction? North against the Soviet Union? Into China? Across the Pacific against America or the British and French colonies? The Japanese themselves did not know, because different factions favored different courses. The Kwantung Army pressed for a northern attack on the Soviet Union.

At just that moment Ozaki entered the orbit of the South Manchuria Railway and was therefore directly informed about the Kwantung Army’s intentions. He could obtain secret documents setting out everything the army knew about Soviet troops in Siberia and Mongolia. Eventually the Kwantung Army decided to try its strength. Its officers believed the Soviets weak and thought that if Japan invaded Mongolia—an independent but Communist country protected by the Soviet Union—they would pass through it like a knife through butter. The Japanese had precise intelligence about Soviet strength in the region. What they did not know was that Sorge had told Moscow what they knew, giving the Soviets time to change their deployment. Through Japanese agents Sorge had also sent the complete organization of the Kwantung Army.

In the summer of 1939 the army launched an experiment, one of those things it did without even informing the Japanese government until afterward. This was no minor border party: several divisions crossed into Mongolia and advanced. A real, undeclared war began, which all sides agreed to conceal from the world. It was August 1939 and the world had other things to think about, with Hitler about to invade Poland.

As the Japanese advanced in Mongolia, the Soviet forces facing them were very well informed, thanks in part to Sorge’s reports. Their commander was a general who would become famous six years later when he entered Berlin: Georgy Zhukov. He was not yet famous, and almost nobody outside the region knew what he did there. Zhukov confronted the Japanese forces, encircled them and annihilated them. The Kwantung Army concluded that war against the Soviet Union might not be advisable after all; invading China or doing something else seemed preferable. But if Japan had broken its teeth and become less eager to attack the Soviet Union, Hitler certainly intended to do so—and did, in June 1941.

## 10. Warnings of Barbarossa and the decision to strike south

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00771–U-00843 -->

Sorge warned Moscow at least a month beforehand. This is the most controversial part of the service he rendered the Soviet Union. We know Stalin received warnings not only from Sorge but through other channels as well; German Communist soldiers even crossed the frontier to report that Operation Barbarossa was about to begin. Stalin was so terrified of a war for which he knew he was unprepared that he refused to believe them. Sorge’s warning might have been vital, but it was not believed. When Barbarossa began, as you know, the Germans initially broke through.

After the war Hanako said she saw Sorge cry only once: on the day Germany actually attacked the Soviet Union. She asked why he was so shaken, and Sorge answered, “Because I am alone.” But the story was not over. Now that Germany was advancing through the Soviet Union like a knife through butter, heading toward Moscow, it became desperately important to know whether the Japanese had reconsidered and intended to attack through Siberia as well.

Japan was debating furiously. The navy wanted to strike south—to attack the American fleet at Pearl Harbor and the British and French colonies in Indochina. The army wanted to continue the invasion of China. The Kwantung Army wanted to attack the Soviet Union, but it had broken its teeth earlier and no longer possessed the same political influence. During the summer and autumn of 1941 these questions were fiercely argued in Japanese government circles.

Moving through those circles was a man who knew everyone and whose opinion everyone sought: Ozaki. He reported everything he heard to Sorge. By mid-September, Sorge was reporting that Japan was unlikely to attack the Soviet Union that year. His intelligence helped Moscow judge the danger in the east. Soviet formations were already moving west, and several of them would fight around Moscow. Sorge had not single-handedly saved the city—but his reports gave the Kremlin one more reason to take that calculated risk. [N-001]

There was perhaps something more. The magistrates who interrogated Sorge and Ozaki soon afterward realized that the pair had not merely sent information to Moscow. Ozaki was an influential government adviser, and he had contributed to that decision. In the highly confidential discussions he attended, he insisted that attacking the Soviet Union was dangerous and unprofitable, and that attacking the Americans was preferable.

The Japanese magistrates were horrified. They almost formed the impression that Japan’s war with America had been—I will not say caused by them, but that these accursed Communists had helped matters move in that direction. Their hair stood on end at the thought of what the country would say if it learned that Communists had led Japan by the nose into this mortal war. Afterward they told the Americans that they had chosen not to investigate this aspect further because they feared revolution if the country found out.

At this point Sorge clearly felt his mission was over. The Nazis were invading the Soviet Union. Japan had not yet attacked Pearl Harbor, but the decision had been made. On 15 October 1941 Sorge sent Moscow a message asking whether he and Max Clausen might now be more useful in another country—perhaps Germany, since both were Germans and could be very useful there.

## 11. The Tokyo ring is uncovered

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00844–U-00910 -->

That same day Ozaki was arrested. Three days later Sorge, Clausen and all the others were arrested. Sorge’s network fell almost by chance—or rather, it was so extensive that sooner or later the Japanese were bound to find a thread. But they found it accidentally and spent a very long time following it.

Two years earlier, in November 1939, the Tokkō—the Japanese political police—had arrested and tortured a member of the underground Communist Party. Required to name other Communists, he named a Mrs. Kitabayashi, a former emigrant who had lived in Los Angeles and returned to Japan. The Tokkō placed this elderly woman and her husband under surveillance. They watched the couple for two years, hoping something would happen, but found nothing. Eventually, in the autumn of 1941, they tired of waiting and arrested them.

Ordered to name Communists, Mrs. Kitabayashi searched her memory and recalled a Japanese Communist she had known in California named Miyagi. The secret police had never heard of him. They arrested him. Miyagi was the first Japanese whom Sorge had recruited into his network. Under torture at police headquarters, he threw himself from a window—the sort of thing that happens in every country, as you see. He failed to kill himself and broke a leg. The police brought him back upstairs. The next day he began naming names: Sorge, Ozaki, Max Clausen.

Even without this, Sorge might not have lasted much longer. The Berlin archives show that in 1940 the Nazi Party, rummaging through its old files, rediscovered information that Sorge had been a Communist in his youth and became suspicious. Walter Schellenberg of the German security service made inquiries. But Sorge appeared extraordinarily valuable: a loyal Nazi and intimate friend of Ambassador Ott, who said he needed him badly. The Germans therefore left Sorge alone, merely warning Ott, without names or commitments, to watch the people around him.

Ott worried that the embassy contained a mole. He told nobody except his best friend, Doctor Sorge. The Germans trusted Sorge so completely that when the Japanese secret police arrested him, the embassy formally protested the detention of a distinguished member of Tokyo’s German community. Ambassador Ott visited him in prison, and for quite some time the embassy pressed the Japanese to release him. Surely it had to be a mistake.

The Japanese public, from the little it learned in the newspapers, assumed Sorge had been arrested as a German spy; allies naturally spy on one another too. The Germans had complete faith in him. As Sorge had once said, “I believe I am leading them all by the nose.” When the truth emerged in Berlin, Schellenberg was summoned by Heinrich Himmler for what he later called a very unpleasant conversation. Sorge and Ozaki were arrested and brought to trial in a long proceeding.

## 12. Trial, execution, and postwar memory

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00911–U-00975 -->

The meticulous proceedings lasted for years. Sorge and Ozaki wrote extensive statements explaining their positions, material that survives and has been studied. Here too the story is extraordinary. The obvious first question is whether they were tortured. It is not easy to answer, because Japan was a very strange country. The military police, the Kempeitai, were known to torture savagely. The Tokkō tortured too, though it might treat prominent foreigners more carefully. Yet the judiciary—this is the astonishing part—was independent, exceedingly serious and punctilious about the law. For three years Japanese magistrates questioned Sorge and Ozaki with great courtesy and attention, formally recording every statement. It seems probable that those two were not tortured. Some of their agents may have been, judging by how many died in prison.

Hanako went to visit Sorge. The police quickly decided that she understood nothing about politics—Japanese sexism may have helped here—and left the poor woman alone. Sorge remained in prison for years. Initially he had meetings with Ambassador Ott, though I doubt Ott continued visiting after the truth emerged. Hanako came regularly. Sorge received newspapers and followed the course of the war.

The man who had cried when Germany attacked the Soviet Union eventually learned that Paulus’s army had surrendered at Stalingrad. That day he was seen dancing for joy in his cell. After three years, the magistrates and the entire team of prosecutors who had questioned Sorge and Ozaki reached trial and sentence. Only those two were condemned to death. All the others received long prison terms, but a few months later the Americans entered Tokyo and opened the prisons; whoever was still alive came out.

Max Clausen, for example, survived in part because he had given the Japanese prosecutor a statement saying that he had come to Japan believing it to be a country hostile to workers, but had discovered his mistake: Japan had the most sincere government and the government friendliest to the people that he had ever known. The Japanese liked such things. Clausen escaped a death sentence, was released by the Americans, and later went with his wife to live in East Germany.

Sorge and Ozaki, however, were hanged on 7 November 1944. The prosecutors who had questioned them for months and years left highly respectful accounts of both men. The Tokkō officials who questioned Ozaki for five months wrote in their report that he had agreed to work with Sorge because he regarded it as an honorable assignment.

Japan is a strange country. Among us, a man involved in such an affair would be abhorred as a traitor. The Japanese, however, admire those who die for their ideas. Ozaki remains a famous figure there and in the postwar years became almost a national hero. Immediately after the war his wife published a book containing the love letters he had written her from prison. Its title was *Love Like a Falling Star*, and it remained a Japanese bestseller for years.

As for Richard Sorge—Ika to his friends—to the very end he remained the man everyone liked. Prosecutor Yoshikawa, who conducted his interrogation, declared: “In all my life, I have never met a man of such stature.” Thank you.
