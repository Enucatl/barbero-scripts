# Come scoppiano le guerre: La prima guerra mondiale — faithful English translation

## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we started thinking about what I might come talk about here in Sarzana, the atmosphere was already much as it is now. News was already coming out of Ukraine and Iraq. Pope Francis had not yet said that Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction..., but he did say it not long ago. So the question of how wars break out is clearly becoming more and more relevant—wait, do I need to repeat that first part? No? It was just a way into the subject, so that's fine. How do wars break out? What I'm going to tell you may actually be fairly reassuring. It shows that wars—the great world wars—break out under circumstances quite different from the ones we're living through now. Today's international situation is disastrous, full of suffering and uncertainty. But it's still quite different from what was taking shape in Europe in 1914, which is where we'll begin this evening.

How does the First World War break out? Well, we all basically know the answer: it starts with an assassination, the assassination in Sarajevo. On June 28, 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists kills Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to understand about the outbreak of the First World War is that it begins with an act of terrorism. Terrorism, then as now, triggers visceral reactions in the public and, as a result, in governments. From our perspective today, killing an archduke may seem, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. But to understand the people of 1914, we need to be very clear about one thing. For them—or at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who is heir to the imperial throne is far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers would have been, if they could even have imagined such a thing.

Think about it. The Austro-Hungarian Empire is a great power. It's weakened and in crisis, but it's still one of the world's few great powers. Its ruler, Emperor Franz Joseph, is eighty-three years old, which means he's going to die very soon. No one knows when, but everyone knows it won't be long. Franz Joseph has ruled the Austrian Empire for as long as anyone can remember. He's approaching sixty-six years on the throne. And he does die on November 21, 1916, a little over two years later. We know that. [N-002] When he dies, Archduke Franz Ferdinand will become the ruler of one of the world's great powers, and he'll remain its ruler for the rest of his life. So you can see how killing him changes things. It's not like killing a president of the United States, when another one will be elected four years later anyway. They're killing someone who would have helped run the world for the rest of his life. So this assassination is extraordinarily serious: an act of terrorism of enormous consequence, carried out with help from abroad.

We need to keep something in mind here—I'll spend the whole evening telling you, “We must remember this,” so forgive me. The way a war breaks out is never that simple. There is a whole chain of connections. The first thing to focus on is Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia is a sensitive place then, just as it has been in our own time. It has a mix of ethnic groups that are hostile to one another. Austria-Hungary has occupied Bosnia for several years, and some of the people there are happy about it. The Croats are happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslims are very happy to be part of the Austrian Empire. In fact, Bosnian Muslim soldiers will fight loyally for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments in fezzes.

But part of the Serbian population—in Serbia, in Bosnia—isn't at all happy to belong to Austria. Right next door, after all, is the Kingdom of Serbia: an independent country, small but aggressive, battle-hardened, with strong military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs resent living under Austrian imperial rule. So there is discontent, and there is terrorism. The Austrians are certain that this terrorism is backed, supported, and organized in Serbia. They're certain that the assassination of the Archduke and his wife was organized there with the involvement of people in the Serbian government and army. They're certain of it from day one. And they're right: the investigation will uncover those connections. There is no doubt that people in Serbia were involved.

So Austria is determined to punish Serbia. And here you really have to make an effort. Don't think of this as Austria and Serbia, forgotten states from a forgotten age—who remembers them anymore? Think of it this way: one country is a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad. The other has suffered a terrorist attack and is determined to punish it. A wave of public outrage sweeps Austria. Crowds fill the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” It even rhymes in German: *Serbien muss sterben*.

## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there's a problem. Serbia has a great friend and protector: Tsar Nicholas II's Russia. The friendship between Serbia and Russia is simply a fact of European geography, and it's true even today. Russia is a Slavic, Orthodox power, and the Serbs are Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let's widen the picture for a moment. Serbia has its great friend, Russia. In the world of 1914, everyone is looking for friends and allies. Alliances are crucial to understanding how the world slides into war, because this isn't a world divided into blocs. Ours isn't anymore either—only the American bloc remains—but we all remember when our world was split into two blocs and nothing happened outside them. The world of 1914 is different. It's made up of powers large and small. Which are the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, and Austria-Hungary. Italy usually makes the list too, as a courtesy—the last of the great powers, if you stretch the definition a little. Then there are the smaller powers, just as aggressive, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They aren't divided into blocs. Each one looks out for itself.

Nationalism is at its peak. Every country believes its duty is to look out for itself. In those very days, the Italian prime minister, Salandra, puts it this way: “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But looking out for yourself also means finding allies, because countries that stand alone tend to come to a bad end. So every country seeks alliances for its own security. Serbia is allied with Russia.

The Austrians assume the world will understand. Serbia is clearly a rogue state that has organized terrorism, and punishing it will make everyone safer. Still, they have to wonder: if Austria attacks Serbia, might Russia step in? But Austria has a powerful friend too, one even more powerful than Austria itself. Austria is a great empire in decline—or at least nearly everyone suspects that it is. Its friend, meanwhile, is immensely powerful and on its way to becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. Kaiser Wilhelm's Germany. You remember Wilhelm, with that huge handlebar mustache and the spiked helmet? The Kaiser may be a slightly comical figure, but Germany is a major power. It has the world's strongest army and is building its strongest industry. In fact, Germany is already the world's leading industrial power. It has overtaken Britain.

Austria and Germany have an alliance that requires each country to help the other if it's attacked. Strictly speaking, Italy is part of it too. It's called the Triple Alliance for a reason. But during the crisis of 1914, it's perfectly clear that no one has any faith in Italy. The Austrians don't believe Italy will come to their aid if they go to war. They dismiss the whole idea from the start, and they're right. In the name of sacred self-interest, Italy will decide to stay out of the war for the time being.

But Austria does have Germany. And it has enough sense to ask what the Germans think before taking action against Serbia. Franz Ferdinand's funeral is on July 3. The old emperor, Franz Joseph, has signed an appeal to Kaiser Wilhelm on July 2, and it's delivered to Wilhelm on July 5 along with a memorandum. [N-005] The letter is fascinating. It isn't precise or clear. It never says, “This is what we want to do.” Instead, Franz Joseph tells Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” The letter reminds me of the arguments Western powers used to enter Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. Something has to be done. There's no clear thinking about the consequences, no thought of what we now call an exit strategy. Just: “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agrees. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reaches him, he writes on the telegram: “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.” So on July 5, he replies to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, do not worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser adds: “Really, do not worry. The Russians will not have the courage to interfere. We are certain Russia will stay out.” That's the first gamble. This evening's story is one gamble after another, and every one of them is wrong. As we'll see, wars don't break out because someone wants them. No one ever wants war—at least not a world war, a general war. No one wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

Let's pause over the promise Kaiser Wilhelm sends his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we are with you, do not worry.” Historians will later call it the blank check. So how is this blank check given? Germany is already a parliamentary country, with political parties, a parliament, and free elections. The Kaiser consults no one. Before talking to anyone, he personally writes to the Austrian emperor: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then he does consult a few people. Parliament? Not a chance. He talks to the chancellor—the prime minister, in other words. He talks to the army commander, General von Moltke, who tells him, “If war breaks out, we are ready.” And then—this is interesting—who else does he consult? Alfred Krupp, the great arms manufacturer. Krupp runs the largest arms factory in the world. The Kaiser feels the need to ask him: if war breaks out, are the Krupp works ready? Alfred Krupp assures him they are.

But they're sure war won't break out. After sending the blank check to Vienna, the Kaiser boards his yacht and takes a cruise in the Baltic. It's July. People want to go on vacation, and the Kaiser does too. This German gamble—yes, there's a risk, but you'll see, war won't come—is crucial to what we're discussing this evening, so we need to stay with it a little longer.

The gamble is even bigger than it looks because Russia isn't the only country involved. Russia is Serbia's great friend, but Russia is allied with France. Politically, it's an unnatural alliance when you think about it. On one side is France, a secular, anticlerical, radical republic. On the other is the Tsar's Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. Yet France and Russia have been allies for twenty years. Why? Just look at a map from that period. Germany seems like a large country to us today, but back then it's much larger. Poland doesn't exist. Czechoslovakia doesn't exist. The Baltic states don't exist. There's Germany, and beyond it, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany sits in the middle, bordering both France and Russia. So France and Russia clearly have every reason to be allies.

So when the Germans bet there won't be a war—a world war, that is; a small war between Austria and Serbia is perfectly fine, even necessary—they're betting they won't end up at war with Russia and France. That's the risk. Why do they take it?

## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second half of this talk—this conversation—will be a kind of countdown through the final days. But first we need the bigger picture. We need to get inside the heads of the people running the world at that moment. So what is the world like? What is Europe like?

Europe in 1914 is enjoying tremendous prosperity and economic growth. After decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy is moving again, and every country is growing. There's even a kind of globalization before the term exists. Of course, it's very easy these days to find globalization in the past. I've seen every possible version of it. Even the Roman Empire gets called globalization. Still, Europe in 1914 has the hallmarks of it. The economies and stock markets are interdependent. The currencies are linked and tied to gold. The Italian lira is worth exactly as much as the French franc. You can travel anywhere in Europe without a passport, except Russia. You need one to enter Russia. Otherwise, Europe is so interconnected that war among these powers seems almost unimaginable.

And yet every one of these countries, including Italy, is armed to the teeth. Their military spending is comparable to American military spending today. They pour enormous sums into their armies and navies. Why? Because they're afraid of one another. To understand the ruling classes of this era, you need to understand that they're steeped in a crude kind of Darwinism: survival of the fittest, the struggle to survive, the strongest coming out on top. They believe states are locked in that same struggle. The strong will triumph, and the weak will be eaten. So everyone wants to be strong, and everyone arms themselves to survive.

No country imagines that it will be the future aggressor. The aggressor is always someone else. We're under threat. We have to be strong. We need a great army. And if we're spending an enormous amount of money on that army, well, we have to expect that sooner or later we may use it. It can't be ruled out. Some even say it's quite likely.

What's striking is how calmly books from those years discuss the next war. War is a predictable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911, the German general von Bernhardi publishes *Germany and the Next War*. The First World War is three years away. The book includes chapters titled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I'm using a German example because German generals are especially inclined to spell these arguments out, but people make similar arguments in every country.

Of course, not everyone thinks this way. Some clear-eyed observers warn that with economies this interdependent, a war among the great powers would be catastrophic. Let me mention another book from those years—the kind prime ministers and army commanders read and keep on their nightstands. In 1910, the English writer Norman Angell publishes *The Great Illusion*. Remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? It later takes its title from this book, which is very famous at the time.

So what is the great illusion? Angell says it's the belief among the great powers that a huge army and preparations to win the next war will somehow keep them safe. They won't. We will all lose the next war, he says. It will destroy even the victors. Believing strength will save us is an illusion. Books like this circulate. Politicians read them. By 1914, a number of European politicians are fairly convinced that a war among the great powers really would be a catastrophe. The paradox is that these same politicians lead countries armed to the teeth.

The next war is so predictable that Sir Edward Grey, the British foreign secretary, describes it in a conversation with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. Listen to what Grey tells him: “But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.” That's the very crisis I'm telling you about tonight: Sarajevo, the summer of 1914. Except this conversation takes place two years earlier. Grey says this to the German ambassador in December 1912. There are only a few places where war is likely to break out, and the Balkans, with all their unrest, are an obvious one.

Even though everyone can see the scenario coming, politicians and military leaders respond to it differently. Politicians say, look, we have to do everything we can to prevent this. It would be a catastrophe. The military have another job. They have to ask: what if it happens anyway? If it does, we need to be ready. So during these years of rapid economic growth, the military push to use all that money to become stronger. The politicians give in.

The years before 1914 become an arms race. Every country strengthens its army, not because it wants to attack anyone, but because it fears for its own security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passes a law extending conscription from two years to three. Imagine a world where military service lasts three years. How do the Germans react? They say the French want war. The French are threatening us.

Political scientists have a name for this very understandable reaction: the security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors get scared. They feel less safe than they did before, so they arm themselves too, and on it goes.

One more point—well, the next-to-last—before we return to Austria as it decides what to do about Serbia. The military don't just ask for more weapons. Their job is to make sure they're ready if war really comes, and being ready means having plans. By 1914, the great powers haven't fought one another in a very long time. Nearly fifty years have passed since the Crimean War and the Franco-Prussian War. In all that time, the general staffs have done nothing but draw up plans. If this particular war breaks out against this particular enemy, what do we do?

They have plans for every possible war, all of them extraordinarily detailed and precise. Every year they pull them out, refine them, and adjust some detail. They've spent fifty years perfecting them. There's a plan for everything.

Here's an example. In November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium makes a state visit to Berlin. At a reception, Kaiser Wilhelm introduces him to General von Kluck with pointed emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.” The plans are so complete that they've already decided who will command every single army. If war comes, everything is ready.

Now one last point before we return to Vienna, where they're debating exactly what to demand from the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. This arms race has another feature that makes it especially dangerous: countries can predict how the balance of power will change. Building up arms takes time. Lay down a battleship today and it will be ready in five years. Extend conscription by a year and it takes two years to feel the effect. So everyone watches closely. When will we be at our strongest? And when will they?

Russia, for instance, is militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it's investing enormous sums. In a few years, it will be much stronger. In this climate, the military do their job. They say: if war comes, we need to be ready. And while we're on the subject, when would it be best for us? Soon, or later? Every military officer makes these calculations.

Then something changes. In April 1914, word gets out that Russia and Britain are negotiating a naval agreement. I've barely mentioned Britain so far. It has no alliances, and I'll come back to it in a moment. But now Britain is opening talks with Russia. Imagine the effect in Berlin. The Germans already expect to face the allied Russians and French in the next war, whenever it comes. If Britain joins them, Germany's position gets worse.

The Russian-British talks begin in April. In May 1914, the commander of the German army, von Moltke, says privately—not in public—that from this moment on, every day of delay can only reduce Germany's chances of success. A few weeks later, on June 1, 1914, he declares, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.” The Sarajevo assassination is four weeks away.

## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Does that mean von Moltke organizes the Sarajevo assassination? Obviously not. The assassination is the catalyst that brings everything simmering for years to the surface.

Let's go back to Vienna, where they're preparing the ultimatum for Serbia. It's going to be extremely harsh because the Austrians don't want the Serbs to accept it. Austria wants war, and it has a blank check from Germany. The Germans keep pushing Vienna: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli. The sooner you act, the better. The Russians won't dare step in.

But Austria does things slowly. It has two governments, Austrian and Hungarian, and they have to agree. Its army is slow and backward too, so getting ready takes time. And here's something no one today would think of: it's July, harvest season. Austria is an agricultural country, and the soldiers are all home on leave to bring in the grain. That takes time. The Austrians aren't in a hurry. In Berlin, the Germans are beside themselves: we're always being dragged behind this backward country.

Finally, on July 23, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum is ready and delivered to the Serbian government. It is extraordinarily severe. Austria accuses the Serbian government of doing nothing to stop the spread of terrorism and says Serbian officers and officials took part in the Sarajevo assassination. It demands that Serbia publish a statement rejecting every form of hostility toward Austria. Serbia must shut down every newspaper and dissolve every organization that spreads hatred of Austria. It must fire every public official and military officer connected in any way to anti-Austrian activity. It must arrest the people on a list supplied by Austria. And it must open terrorism investigations on Serbian soil with Austrian investigators taking part.

On July 24, capitals around the world wake up to the ultimatum, and the reaction is extremely negative. In St. Petersburg, the Russian foreign minister summons the Austrian ambassador. Do you want war with Serbia? he asks. Are the Germans pushing you into it? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That's number one: as far as Russia is concerned, the responsibility belongs to Vienna and the Austrians. We'll hear one version after another of this phrase, “you are the ones assuming responsibility.” This is only the first.

In London, Foreign Secretary Grey is appalled too. He calls in the German ambassador, Lichnowsky, and tells him he's never seen one state send another a document like this. The Serbs can't possibly accept it. And here's a small detail that gives you a feel for the period. Prince Lichnowsky says: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!”

So now we're in London, listening to Grey and Lichnowsky. What's happening there? For the moment, the German gamble seems to be paying off. The British public couldn't care less about Serbia. Britain isn't allied with anyone. It does have a longstanding friendship with France, to be sure. British and French military officers regularly consult one another. They've even developed plans together, and they've become fairly friendly. But there are no commitments and no treaties.

At this point Britain is isolationist. It dominates the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock market—and couldn't care less about minor disputes on the European continent, especially in the Balkans. British newspapers ask why Britain should let itself be dragged into someone else's war. On July 30, after the ultimatum, the *Manchester Guardian* writes: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017]

The cabinet minutes show just how far removed Britain is from all of this. From June 28, the day of the Sarajevo assassination, to July 24, when Austria's ultimatum to Serbia throws the world into an uproar, the British government goes almost a month without discussing foreign policy. Britain has problems of its own. Ireland isn't independent yet. There's terrorism, turmoil of every kind, a revolution about to break out. For the British government, foreign policy might as well not exist.

Still, a few people in Britain see things differently. We know from the notes exchanged in those days that some Foreign Office officials are afraid of what's happening. Once again, it's the security paradox. We're fairly safe, but Germany is growing very strong. The Germans say they're only doing it because they're afraid of us. But if they become too strong, a senior Foreign Office official argues, we have to stand with France and Russia. This fight isn't about control of Serbia. It's between a Germany seeking political dictatorship over Europe and the powers that want to remain free.

Some people in Britain agree, especially the military. British officers have come to see themselves as friends of the French, used to working alongside them. They know it will be hard to bring Britain into a war on France's side because the public doesn't want it. They've been turning the problem over for years. It's striking how long they've spent thinking, talking, and debating among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do?

There's a revealing story from 1910. The English general Wilson, a close friend of the French, is talking to the French general Foch. If war breaks out, Wilson asks, and if we come help you—I hope we will, but who knows—what is the smallest number of British soldiers who could make a real difference for France? Remember, this is 1910, four years before the war. What is the minimum? Foch answers: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is rallying British public opinion behind a cause.

It's almost midnight. Let's begin the countdown. On July 25, Serbia's reply to the ultimatum arrives.

## 5. Serbia's reply, Churchill's fleet, and mobilization logic

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00588–U-00679 -->

The Serbs accept almost every condition. Meanwhile, Serbia appeals to the tsar for assurance that Russia will support it if attacked. The tsar's answer is: you can count on us. That day he makes a symbolic decision—and symbols matter in politics. Every final-year officer cadet in Russia is commissioned on the same day, at the same hour: the moment Austria's ultimatum to Serbia expires. The message couldn't be clearer.

On July 26, Austria rejects the Serbian reply. Rejecting it means Austria is going to war. And that same day, something happens in London. It's another detail—I've packed this lecture with details, which means I'll run late and you'll have to be patient. I couldn't bring myself to cut many of them. But this one strikes me as curious in its own way.

The day Austria rejects Serbia's reply, Britain's naval minister, the First Lord of the Admiralty, makes a decision on his own. The British war fleet is in the middle of major exercises, gathered in one place with full crews. The exercises end the next day. After that, the squadrons are supposed to return to their home ports and the sailors are supposed to go on leave. Instead, the First Lord orders the fleet to stay together and move to the naval base at Scapa Flow in the Orkney Islands, where it will be safe.

I'm also telling you this because the First Lord of the Admiralty in 1914 is Winston Churchill. We usually associate him with the Second World War. He was still alive when I was a child, and many of us remember him. But few people remember how precocious Churchill was. He is still a young man, and he's already naval minister in 1914. He's the one who keeps the war fleet together and ready—and, what's more, moves it to safety at the naval base. The next day, the German stock market crashes. The news from London is enough to raise doubts. Maybe Britain won't stay out after all.

There's one more thing I need to explain: the pressures these military officers and politicians are under. It's the last interruption in our countdown, I hope. By now, war is certain—between Austria and Serbia, at least. We'll see about the rest. Before these countries can fight, they have to order mobilization. What does that mean? Their armies rely on conscription. The countries are full of young men, and older men, who completed military service in years past and can be called back if war begins—or rather, who are certain to be called back. The moment war starts, the army has to expand, double, or triple in size by calling up everyone at home. That's mobilization.

It's extraordinarily complicated. Millions of men have to be called up, put in uniform, armed, organized into units, and loaded onto trains bound for the frontier. Every step follows a plan worked out to the smallest detail. German mobilization, timed to the minute and second, requires 11,000 trains to move at once. The Germans are used to doing this kind of thing well. Everything is scheduled to the minute. Mobilization is an enormously consequential decision. It's also tremendously expensive, so no country orders it unless the situation has become truly serious.

But mobilization works differently from one country to another. Some countries are slow. Take Russia. It's enormous and has few railroads, so mobilizing its army takes months. If the situation grows serious, Russia has to start fairly quickly. It can't afford to wait. And sure enough, the first country to talk about mobilization on July 26 isn't Austria, even though Austria is about to go to war with Serbia. Serbia is small, after all; Austria doesn't need its entire army. The first is Russia, because Russia knows how long mobilization will take.

Then there's one country in Europe—let's see if you can guess which one—that's certain it can mobilize extremely fast. Its plans are so precise that mobilization itself means war. There's no gap between the two decisions, no distinction at all. Once this country mobilizes, it might as well go to war immediately, immediately, immediately. Got it? It's Germany.

So on July 26, the same day Austria rejects Serbia's very moderate reply, German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg warns the Russian government: “Preparatory military measures by Russia would compel us to take countermeasures. Mobilization, however, means war.” Surely Russia can't want to unleash a European war like this. And that's number two: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the Russians. Russia is the country that must step back and decide not to mobilize.

## 6. Austria declares war and the cousins exchange telegrams

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00680–U-00759 -->

Then comes a twist on July 28. The Kaiser returns from his cruise and reads Serbia's reply to the Austrian ultimatum. He thinks it's excellent: everything we wanted, and then some. Serbia's reply has removed every reason for war. His delight is the first sign that the Germans themselves may not be so sure they want this war after all. Two months earlier, General von Moltke had said they were ready. But saying that in theory is one thing. Now it's really happening. The Kaiser is thrilled: Serbia's reply fixes everything. No war. Except that Austria declares war on Serbia that very day.

The Germans have spent all this time urging Austria on. And once again, we have to understand the people living through this. Austria is swept up in a huge wave of popular enthusiasm. People are convinced: let's go punish these terrorists threatening the world. Let me give you an example: Sigmund Freud. Freud is Austrian, lives in Vienna, and is fifty-eight years old, a mature man who has spent his whole life in the Austrian Empire. When Austria declares war on Serbia, Freud says—or writes, I don't remember, but we know he said it—that for the first time in thirty years, he feels truly Austrian. For the first time, he thinks this empire may have a future. And this next part really is a quotation: “My entire libido belongs to Austria-Hungary.” It's July 28.

On July 29, Russia starts preparing to mobilize. That's exactly what the Germans had warned them not to do, because mobilization means war. Russia mobilizes because it's afraid. But the Russians don't want war either, as we can see from another curious episode that begins that day. And it's typical of this strange period. Europe is suspended between modernity—liberal parliamentary democracy—and the old world of dynastic empires, where emperors and kings still hold real power.

On July 29, the telegrams between Nicky and Willy begin. The first comes from the Russian tsar, Nicholas, who sends a telegram to Kaiser Wilhelm just as he is about to mobilize his army against him. Nicholas writes to the Kaiser in English. That's significant too. It shows you what England has become by this point. Fifty years earlier, a tsar writing to a king of Prussia would have used French. Now they speak and write to each other in English.

To understand the situation, remember that Tsar Nicholas, Emperor Wilhelm, and King George of England are all dynastic cousins. Wilhelm and George are first cousins through Queen Victoria—you remember Queen Victoria, the nineteenth-century queen who died in nineteen hundred and something. Nicholas and George are first cousins through the Danish royal family, and Nicholas is not Queen Victoria's grandson. They speak to each other informally and use nicknames. [N-024] So on July 29, the Russian tsar sends the Kaiser a telegram. He says he's glad Wilhelm is back from his cruise. The situation is serious, and he needs his help. “An ignoble war has been declared to a weak country. I beg you in the name of our old friendship to do what you can to stop your allies from going too far.” Signed, Nicky.

The Kaiser replies in English that same day. He tells Nicholas to look at it from the other side: Serbia is a terrorist state, Austria is within its rights, and so on. Come on, you Russians have to understand. You're the ones who need to step back. Signed, Willy. For four days, they exchange a dozen telegrams, from Nicky to Willy and Willy to Nicky. Then, on August 1, Willy declares war on Nicky. But let's not get ahead of ourselves. We still have exactly four days to cover.

July 29. The Germans are furious with these stupid Russians, who don't understand that mobilizing will cause the war. General von Moltke says Russia has sided with a criminal country, a den of terrorists. “Then the mutual destruction of the civilized states of Europe can begin.” This is still Moltke: the war will destroy the civilization of almost all Europe for decades. And yet it has to be fought, because no one is willing to back down. Here again, how can I put it, we see an almost schizophrenic streak in this ruling class. They understand perfectly well that the war will be a catastrophe. At the same time, they can't back down and lose face. The others should step back. Not us. And now another split opens up in Germany, this time between the military and the politicians.

## 7. German war plans, Belgian neutrality, and Britain's warning

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00760–U-00859 -->

The military say war is inevitable, so let's fight it now. Let's attack. Give us the order to mobilize. The politicians say, wait. For them, it's crucial to show that Germany isn't to blame for the war. The Russians and the French are entirely at fault, not Germany. Why do German politicians care so much? There are plenty of reasons: saving face, pride, honor. But they also care about German public opinion. Public opinion matters in Germany too, and Germany has a huge socialist party. What will the socialists say? If people believe Germany has been attacked, there won't be a problem. And that's exactly what happens. Germany holds off on mobilizing, and its people go to war convinced that innocent Germany has been attacked and must be defended.

They also have to keep England out of the war. If England sees Germany doing everything it can to prevent war, maybe it will stay out. By now, it's clear that the English want to stay out if they possibly can. But one more problem makes that very difficult for Germany. It's the last one I'll pull out of my hat, so to speak: Belgium. You may be wondering what Belgium has to do with any of this. Quite a lot.

Belgium is a small neutral country. All the great powers, including Germany and England, have signed a guarantee that it will remain neutral. And the English care deeply about that. In their view of Europe, Belgium, with its ports facing the Channel—Antwerp—is a vital link for England. We English may quarrel with France; that doesn't matter. As long as Belgium exists, we have a link to Europe. So the English don't care about Serbia at all. But they certainly care about Belgium.

Why on earth are the Germans worried about Belgium? Because of their war plans. The German general staff has what it considers the perfect plan for fighting France and Russia and winning in a matter of weeks. Everything is calculated. The Russians are slow, so we send the entire army—the famous 11,000 trains—against France. We invade and finish the war in a few weeks. But to invade France and outflank the French army—forgive me for getting into strategy here—the Germans have decided they must go through Belgium. This is the famous Schlieffen Plan, which some of you may remember, refined over many years. The military tell the politicians: if you want us to win this war for you, this is the plan. We have to go through Belgium. It's your job to figure out how to handle that.

On July 29, the Kaiser and the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, realize that if they're betting on England staying out, they need to deal with the Belgian problem. Germany is going to invade Belgium. That's already been decided. So the Kaiser and the chancellor make a crucial decision, later judged unanimously to be the stupidest decision Germany makes during those days. They write to the English government in London and offer guarantees.

First, if war with France breaks out, the English can rest easy: Germany has no territorial ambitions. We don't want to take any part of France, so we don't want to upset the balance of power in Europe. Second, Germany assures the English government that if it happens to be forced to invade Belgium during the war, it will leave once the war is over. And if the Belgians behave themselves and aren't hostile, Germany will even pay them damages. Finally, the Germans tell London: think about it. We Germans and you English can still come to an agreement. Together we're very strong. We can divide up the world. Why do you care about being friends with France? Why do you care about Belgium? Let's divide the world between us.

The message reaches London, and the English are stunned. A senior Foreign Office official comments: “The only comment that need be made on these astounding proposals is that they reflect discredit on the statesman who makes them.” And there's more. The Germans are telling us they're going to invade Belgium. They've actually put it in writing. Grey calls in the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, and tells him: if war breaks out, we can't stay out. We'll enter the war alongside our friends. Grey also says something everyone is repeating almost obsessively in those days: “if war did break out, it would be the greatest catastrophe that the world had ever seen” But England can't make this deal with Germany. Betraying its friends would leave an indelible stain, so Berlin had better think very carefully. And three: the English believe the responsibility for everything lies in Berlin. Berlin has to back down.

The news from London sends the Germans into a rage. The Kaiser writes on the message: “Aha! The vile deceiver! Vile blackguard! I will never make a naval agreement with such scoundrels! The vile shopkeeper rabble tried to deceive us with dinners and speeches. But he avoids saying the word and threatens us instead! Vile blackguard! England alone bears responsibility for war and peace, no longer we! That must also be made clear publicly.” And four: to the Germans, the English are responsible.

That same day, July 29, the German ambassador in Brussels, von Below-Saleske, receives a sealed packet from Berlin. His instructions are not to open it until further orders arrive by telegram. One more useless detail—we're almost there, but I'll inflict it on you anyway. Von Below-Saleske is a career diplomat who likes to joke that he brings bad luck. He was in China when the Boxer Rebellion broke out. He was in Turkey when the Young Turk Revolution broke out. But now, he says, I can relax. I'm in Belgium. Nothing will ever happen in Brussels. Let's pick up the pace, or I'll keep you here all night.

## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

No one sleeps in Berlin on the night of July 29. The news from London has sent everyone into a panic. It's becoming clear that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, calls Vienna. Have you understood nothing? Do you really want to go to war with Serbia? Take it easy. Slow down. Germany has no intention of being dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine the looks on their faces in Vienna. Until the day before, the Germans had been telling them to go ahead. So, number five: the Germans now put the blame on Vienna.

Meanwhile, we've reached July 30. Russia finishes its preparations—which alone take several days—and orders general mobilization. In Berlin, the military go to the government and say, let's mobilize too, right now. The government says, let's wait one more day. Maybe Vienna will come up with something. That day, the conflict between the military and the politicians in Berlin comes close—I won't say to a coup, but yes, to high treason.

That afternoon, a newspaper comes out announcing the mobilization order. Germany still has afternoon papers in those days. But the story isn't true. The paper is seized, and then they discover that the general staff planted the false report to present the government with a done deal. General von Moltke calls his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria, by the way, still hasn't mobilized. It does everything so slowly that it hasn't even mobilized yet. Moltke tells Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize, then we'll have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government's hand.

The government is still holding firm, but it's in a total panic. And that day, another feature of these final communications comes to the surface: everyone feels that something is happening that no one wants and no one can stop. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.”

There you have it: politics admitting defeat. The stone is rolling, and we no longer know what to do. That day, the New York Stock Exchange collapses. Because of the time difference, the news reaches London on the morning of July 31, and the London Stock Exchange collapses too. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.

## 9. 31 July–1 August: ultimatums, war, and an impossible reversal

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00891–U-00959 -->

Germany still doesn't mobilize, but it sends Russia an ultimatum: you've begun mobilizing. Stop. If you stop by midnight, fine. Otherwise, it's war. In London on July 31, the foreign secretary, Grey, is now convinced that war is inevitable and England has to enter it. But he's in the minority within the government. The rest of the government keeps saying this has nothing to do with us. Grey does what he can. He sends one telegram to Paris and another to Berlin, asking each government to guarantee that it will respect Belgian neutrality. The French answer within an hour. The Germans don't answer at all.

On August 1, Grey addresses the government. He calls Germany an aggressor as dangerous as Napoleon and makes it clear that he'll resign if England doesn't enter the war. But most of the ministers are against him. They threaten to resign if there is a war. Outside the door, the French ambassador, Cambon, is waiting, just as he has been throughout those days. Can you imagine his job in London at that moment? War is about to break out, and he has to make sure the English enter it with us. After all, we're such good friends.

Grey has to go out and tell Cambon there are no guarantees. The government won't promise anything. Cambon goes into the office of a friend at the English Foreign Office. His friend describes him as white as a sheet and on the verge of tears. The French ambassador drops into a chair and tells him, “They're going to abandon us; they're going to let us down.”

Meanwhile, everything keeps hurtling forward. On August 1, Germany declares war on Russia. It's the first declaration of war between two great powers. Why does Germany declare war? Because that's the procedure. We sent an ultimatum. We told the Russians to stop by midnight. They didn't stop, so now we have to declare war. The night before, the ministers meet at Bethmann Hollweg's house to draft the declaration of war on Russia. The navy minister, Tirpitz, says: “On 1 August I learned in the Federal Council session that we had followed the ultimatum with a declaration of war on Russia. […] We were to assume the odium of the attacking party because of the jurists of the Foreign Office. […] Presumably we did it out of formal legal conscientiousness.” That's the procedure: first the ultimatum, then the declaration of war. So at four in the afternoon on August 1, Germany declares war on Russia and orders general mobilization. To the Germans, those really are the same thing.

But there's still time for a farce, a grotesque misunderstanding. At five that afternoon, a telegram arrives in Berlin from Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador in London. Lichnowsky says: “Sir E. Grey just asked me by telephone whether I believed that I could declare that we would not attack France in a war between Germany and Russia in case France should remain neutral. I declared that I thought I could give that assurance.” The telegram throws Berlin into chaos. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg think this is fantastic. We no longer have to fight both France and Russia, only Russia. If England and France stay out, we're fine. We just need to change the mobilization orders and send everything against Russia.

They summon General von Moltke, who commands the army and is running the massive mobilization. Eleven thousand trains are already moving. At that very moment, telegrams carrying the secret orders are reaching every station and every barracks in Germany. The whole machine is in motion. They call von Moltke to the imperial palace.

I have to tell you something about von Moltke. He isn't a great general, and they will soon remove him from command. He's there mostly because he's the nephew of a great man. In the nineteenth century, there had been another von Moltke, a famous Prussian general who won all his wars. The younger von Moltke owes his position largely to that great uncle. But his nerves are rather fragile.

He arrives at the palace, and they tell him: look, there's a telegram from London. England and France will probably stay out. Fantastic. Change the mobilization orders. Send everything against Russia. Von Moltke nearly has a fit. He starts crying and says it can't be done. Everything is already moving: 11,000 trains, millions of people, every movement timed to the second. It can't be done. They keep pushing him. Moltke—who, remember, is on the verge of collapse and will go home that evening and have a heart attack—holds his ground. It can't be done. The Kaiser tells him: “Your uncle would have given me a different answer!” Moltke is shattered. But the mobilization can't be changed, so everything continues according to plan. That evening, the Germans invade Luxembourg. It's the first step toward invading Belgium.

## 10. 2–4 August: Belgium brings Britain into the war

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01015 -->

Before invading Belgium, though, the Germans have to observe the formalities. They have to deliver an ultimatum. On August 2, von Below-Saleske in Brussels receives a telegram telling him to open the sealed packet and deliver the ultimatum to Belgium, while pretending it has just arrived. He's had it for several days.

What the ultimatum tells the Belgians is a pack of lies. And here we come to one last, slightly grotesque feature of this whole situation—I really am almost finished. You've seen how the road to war is both incredibly complicated and terrifyingly simple. Yet the Germans remain convinced that to issue an ultimatum or declare war, you need a pretext, some kind of excuse. So they tell the Belgians: “The German Government has received reliable information according to which French forces intend to march on the Meuse by way of Givet and Namur.” We Germans have to enter Belgium too, you understand, for our own security. But we don't want the Belgians to see that as a hostile act. If Belgium remains neutral and behaves itself, we promise to leave at the end of the war. The Belgians decide to resist.

That evening in Berlin, they draft the declaration of war on France at Bethmann Hollweg's house. Admiral von Tirpitz asks why they have to declare war on France too. Why not wait for the French to declare war on us? They explain that these are the rules. This is how it has to be done. The declaration of war on France is another pack of lies. French planes have flown over Germany. They've dropped bombs. They've killed civilians. France has therefore placed itself at war, and so on. Apparently, even when you're practicing power politics, you still need something to point to for the sake of public opinion. It reminds me, perhaps, of Colin Powell at the UN with that vial of Saddam Hussein's biological weapons. But anyway, never mind.

That evening, August 2, Grey learns about the German ultimatum to Belgium. He finally gets the English government to issue the mobilization order. Several ministers resign. That same evening, an American diplomat in Brussels writes in his diary: “Nobody seems to remember that a few days ago Serbia was playing a star rôle in this affair. She seems to have faded away behind the scenes.”

August 3. Now we really are at the end. Germany declares war on France. Grey turns to the House of Commons. Finally, a parliament steps in and does something. Until now, governments and monarchs have handled the entire crisis. Grey secures authorization to send Germany an ultimatum: invade Belgium, and it's war. On August 4, the Germans invade Belgium. The English ambassador goes to see Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg with the ultimatum, unaware that the Germans began invading Belgium that morning.

And the whole affair ends—and so does my story—with two famous lines. One is very famous, the other a little less so. The very famous one comes from the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg. When the English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum, the chancellor makes a scene and says: “just for a word—'neutrality,' a word which in war time had so often been disregarded—just for a scrap of paper Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation” The line goes down in history as the emblem, you might say, of a kind of politics that signs treaties and then, whenever it wants, pretends they don't exist. So much for treaties.

The other historic line is a little less famous. It comes from the Kaiser. Remember, he's the cousin of both the king of England and the tsar. They all use nicknames and speak to each other informally. And now it's clear that Germany is at war with Russia, France, and England. They're all Queen Victoria's little grandsons, remember? The Kaiser says: who would have thought that Georgie and Nicky were about to betray me? If my grandmother had been alive, she would never have allowed it.
