## 10. 2–4 August: Belgium brings Britain into the war

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01015 -->

Before invading Belgium, though, the Germans have to observe the formalities. They have to deliver an ultimatum. On August 2, von Below-Saleske in Brussels receives a telegram telling him to open the sealed packet and deliver the ultimatum to Belgium, while pretending it has just arrived. He's had it for several days.

What the ultimatum tells the Belgians is a pack of lies. And here we come to one last, slightly grotesque feature of this whole situation—I really am almost finished. You've seen how the road to war is both incredibly complicated and terrifyingly simple. Yet the Germans remain convinced that to issue an ultimatum or declare war, you need a pretext, some kind of excuse. So they tell the Belgians: “The German Government has received reliable information according to which French forces intend to march on the Meuse by way of Givet and Namur.” We Germans have to enter Belgium too, you understand, for our own security. But we don't want the Belgians to see that as a hostile act. If Belgium remains neutral and behaves itself, we promise to leave at the end of the war. The Belgians decide to resist.

That evening in Berlin, they draft the declaration of war on France at Bethmann Hollweg's house. Admiral von Tirpitz asks why they have to declare war on France too. Why not wait for the French to declare war on us? They explain that these are the rules. This is how it has to be done. The declaration of war on France is another pack of lies. French planes have flown over Germany. They've dropped bombs. They've killed civilians. France has therefore placed itself at war, and so on. Apparently, even when you're practicing power politics, you still need something to point to for the sake of public opinion. It reminds me, perhaps, of Colin Powell at the UN with that vial of Saddam Hussein's biological weapons. But anyway, never mind.

That evening, August 2, Grey learns about the German ultimatum to Belgium. He finally gets the English government to issue the mobilization order. Several ministers resign. That same evening, an American diplomat in Brussels writes in his diary: “Nobody seems to remember that a few days ago Serbia was playing a star rôle in this affair. She seems to have faded away behind the scenes.”

August 3. Now we really are at the end. Germany declares war on France. Grey turns to the House of Commons. Finally, a parliament steps in and does something. Until now, governments and monarchs have handled the entire crisis. Grey secures authorization to send Germany an ultimatum: invade Belgium, and it's war. On August 4, the Germans invade Belgium. The English ambassador goes to see Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg with the ultimatum, unaware that the Germans began invading Belgium that morning.

And the whole affair ends—and so does my story—with two famous lines. One is very famous, the other a little less so. The very famous one comes from the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg. When the English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum, the chancellor makes a scene and says: “just for a word—'neutrality,' a word which in war time had so often been disregarded—just for a scrap of paper Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation” The line goes down in history as the emblem, you might say, of a kind of politics that signs treaties and then, whenever it wants, pretends they don't exist. So much for treaties.

The other historic line is a little less famous. It comes from the Kaiser. Remember, he's the cousin of both the king of England and the tsar. They all use nicknames and speak to each other informally. And now it's clear that Germany is at war with Russia, France, and England. They're all Queen Victoria's little grandsons, remember? The Kaiser says: who would have thought that Georgie and Nicky were about to betray me? If my grandmother had been alive, she would never have allowed it.
