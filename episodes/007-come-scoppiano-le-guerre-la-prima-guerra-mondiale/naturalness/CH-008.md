## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

On the night of July 29–30, no one in Berlin goes to bed. The news from London has thrown them into a panic. It's becoming clear that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, calls Vienna and says: have you understood nothing? Do you really want to go to war with Serbia? Take it easy. Slow down. Germany has no intention of being dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine the faces in Vienna. Until the day before, the Germans had been telling them to press ahead. So, and five: for the Germans, responsibility now lies in Vienna.

Meanwhile, we've reached July 30. Russia finishes its preparations—and the preparations alone take several days—and orders general mobilization. In Berlin, the military go to the government and say, let's mobilize too, immediately. The government says, let's wait one more day. Perhaps they'll manage to come up with something in Vienna. On this day, July 30, the crisis between the military and the politicians in Berlin comes close—I won't say to a coup d'état, but yes, to high treason. That afternoon, an evening paper comes out—Germany had evening papers in those days—announcing the mobilization order. It isn't true. The paper is seized. Then it emerges that the general staff leaked the false report to present the government with a fait accompli. General von Moltke telephones his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria hasn't mobilized yet, incidentally, because Austria does things so slowly that it still hasn't mobilized. Moltke tells Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize, we'll have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government to mobilize.

The government is still holding firm, but it's in a state of total panic. That day, another characteristic feature of the communications from these very last days emerges: everyone feels that something is happening which no one wanted and which can no longer be stopped. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.” There you have it: politics admitting defeat. The stone is rolling, and we no longer know what to do. That day, the New York Stock Exchange collapses. Because of the time difference, the news from New York arrives on the morning of July 31. The London Stock Exchange collapses. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.
