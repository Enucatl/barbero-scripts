## 9. 31 July–1 August: ultimatums, war, and an impossible reversal

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00891–U-00959 -->

Germany still doesn't mobilize, but it sends Russia an ultimatum: you've begun mobilizing. Stop. If you stop by midnight, fine. Otherwise, it's war. In London on July 31, the foreign secretary, Grey, is now convinced that war is inevitable and England has to enter it. But he's in the minority within the government. The rest of the government keeps saying this has nothing to do with us. Grey does what he can. He sends one telegram to Paris and another to Berlin, asking each government to guarantee that it will respect Belgian neutrality. The French answer within an hour. The Germans don't answer at all.

On August 1, Grey addresses the government. He calls Germany an aggressor as dangerous as Napoleon and makes it clear that he'll resign if England doesn't enter the war. But most of the ministers are against him. They threaten to resign if there is a war. Outside the door, the French ambassador, Cambon, is waiting, just as he has been throughout those days. Can you imagine his job in London at that moment? War is about to break out, and he has to make sure the English enter it with us. After all, we're such good friends.

Grey has to go out and tell Cambon there are no guarantees. The government won't promise anything. Cambon goes into the office of a friend at the English Foreign Office. His friend describes him as white as a sheet and on the verge of tears. The French ambassador drops into a chair and tells him, “They're going to abandon us; they're going to let us down.”

Meanwhile, everything keeps hurtling forward. On August 1, Germany declares war on Russia. It's the first declaration of war between two great powers. Why does Germany declare war? Because that's the procedure. We sent an ultimatum. We told the Russians to stop by midnight. They didn't stop, so now we have to declare war. The night before, the ministers meet at Bethmann Hollweg's house to draft the declaration of war on Russia. The navy minister, Tirpitz, says: “On 1 August I learned in the Federal Council session that we had followed the ultimatum with a declaration of war on Russia. […] We were to assume the odium of the attacking party because of the jurists of the Foreign Office. […] Presumably we did it out of formal legal conscientiousness.” That's the procedure: first the ultimatum, then the declaration of war. So at four in the afternoon on August 1, Germany declares war on Russia and orders general mobilization. To the Germans, those really are the same thing.

But there's still time for a farce, a grotesque misunderstanding. At five that afternoon, a telegram arrives in Berlin from Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador in London. Lichnowsky says: “Sir E. Grey just asked me by telephone whether I believed that I could declare that we would not attack France in a war between Germany and Russia in case France should remain neutral. I declared that I thought I could give that assurance.” The telegram throws Berlin into chaos. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg think this is fantastic. We no longer have to fight both France and Russia, only Russia. If England and France stay out, we're fine. We just need to change the mobilization orders and send everything against Russia.

They summon General von Moltke, who commands the army and is running the massive mobilization. Eleven thousand trains are already moving. At that very moment, telegrams carrying the secret orders are reaching every station and every barracks in Germany. The whole machine is in motion. They call von Moltke to the imperial palace.

I have to tell you something about von Moltke. He isn't a great general, and they will soon remove him from command. He's there mostly because he's the nephew of a great man. In the nineteenth century, there had been another von Moltke, a famous Prussian general who won all his wars. The younger von Moltke owes his position largely to that great uncle. But his nerves are rather fragile.

He arrives at the palace, and they tell him: look, there's a telegram from London. England and France will probably stay out. Fantastic. Change the mobilization orders. Send everything against Russia. Von Moltke nearly has a fit. He starts crying and says it can't be done. Everything is already moving: 11,000 trains, millions of people, every movement timed to the second. It can't be done. They keep pushing him. Moltke—who, remember, is on the verge of collapse and will go home that evening and have a heart attack—holds his ground. It can't be done. The Kaiser tells him: “Your uncle would have given me a different answer!” Moltke is shattered. But the mobilization can't be changed, so everything continues according to plan. That evening, the Germans invade Luxembourg. It's the first step toward invading Belgium.
