## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there was a problem: Serbia had a great friend, a great protector—Russia. The Russia of Tsar Nicholas II. The friendship between Serbia and Russia was an obvious fact of European geography; it's true even today. Russia was a Slavic, Orthodox power; the Serbs were Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let's broaden the picture for a moment. Serbia had a great friend, Russia. Everyone in that world of 1914 tried to have friends and allies. The weight of alliances is a fundamental factor in understanding how the world slid into world war, because that wasn't a world of blocs. Well, ours is no longer one either by now—only the American bloc remains—but in any case we all remember when our world was divided into two blocs and nothing happened outside those two blocs. The world of 1914 wasn't like that. It was a world of great and small powers. Which ones were the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and then, as a courtesy, Italy usually makes the list too—the last of the great powers, with a little effort. And then there were the small powers, but they too were bellicose, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They weren't divided into blocs; each looked after itself.

This was the age in which nationalism was at its height. Every country was convinced that it had a duty to look after itself. It's what an Italian politician, Prime Minister Salandra, in those very days would call “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But if you had to look after yourself, you also had to find allies, because on your own you'd come to a bad end. So each country sought alliances for its own security. Serbia was allied with Russia.

The Austrians thought the world would understand: Serbia was clearly a rogue state that had organized terrorism; it had to be punished for everyone's security. But they began to wonder whether, if Austria attacked Serbia, the Russians might intervene. But Austria also had a powerful friend, one more powerful than itself. Austria was a great empire in decline—almost everyone suspected as much—whereas its immensely powerful friend that was becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. The Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm—you remember Wilhelm with the great handlebar moustache and the spiked helmet? But beyond the Kaiser, who was a somewhat comical figure, Germany was a very great power. It had the strongest army in the world and was building the strongest industry in the world. In fact, Germany had already become the greatest industrial power; it had already overtaken Britain.

Austria was allied with Germany in an alliance that bound the two countries to help one another if either was attacked. To tell the truth, the alliance also included Italy. It was called the Triple Alliance for a reason. But during those days of crisis in 1914 it was perfectly clear that no one had any faith in Italy. The Austrians didn't believe that, if they went to war, Italy would come to their aid. They ruled the whole idea out from the start, and in fact they were right. In the name of sacred egoism, Italy would decide to stay out for the time being.

But there was Germany. Austria had enough good sense to know that, before doing anything against Serbia, it had to ask what the Germans thought. Franz Ferdinand's funeral took place on 3 July. The Emperor, the old Emperor Franz Joseph, had signed his appeal to Kaiser Wilhelm on 2 July; it was delivered to Wilhelm on 5 July with the accompanying memorandum. [N-005] Now, this letter from the Emperor of Austria is very interesting. It'sn't precise or lucid; it doesn't say, “This is what we want to do.” It's a letter more or less along these lines. Franz Joseph says to Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” This is a letter that makes me think of the reasons with which the Western powers went into Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. In other words, something must be done. There's no lucid reasoning about the consequences, no consideration of what today is called an exit strategy. “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agreed. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reached him, Kaiser Wilhelm wrote on the telegram: “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.” Consequently, on 5 July Kaiser Wilhelm replied to his Austrian counterpart, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser also added: “Really, don't worry. The Russians won't have the courage to interfere. We're certain Russia will stay out.” That was the first gamble. This evening's story is a story of one gamble after another, all of them wrong. As we'll see, wars break out not because someone wants them. No one ever wants war, at least not world war, general war; no one ever wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

I want to pause for another moment over this assurance Kaiser Wilhelm sent to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we're with you, don't worry.” It's a reassurance that historians later called the blank cheque. How was this blank cheque given? Germany was already a parliamentary country; it had political parties, a parliament, free elections. The Kaiser consulted no one. Before consulting anyone at all, he himself wrote to his counterpart, the Emperor of Austria, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then the Kaiser did consult someone. Whom? Parliament? Not a chance. He consulted the Chancellor—the prime minister, that's. He consulted the commander of the army, General von Moltke, and General von Moltke told him, “If war breaks out, we're ready.” And then—this is interesting—whom else did Kaiser Wilhelm consult? He consulted Alfred Krupp, the great arms industrialist. Krupp was the great—the world's largest—arms factory. The Kaiser felt the need to consult Alfred Krupp. If war should ever break out, were the Krupp works ready? And Alfred Krupp reassured him: the Krupp works were ready.

But they were certain that war would not break out. Indeed, after sending the blank cheque to Vienna, the Kaiser boarded his yacht and went on a cruise in the Baltic. It was July. People wanted their holidays, and the Kaiser went on holiday too. This German gamble—that yes, there was a risk of war, but you would see that war would not come—is a crucial aspect of the argument we must discuss this episode, so I must dwell on it a little longer.

The gamble was even greater than it appeared because of the question of alliances, since Russia wasn't the only party involved. Russia was Serbia's great friend, but Russia had an alliance with France. Politically speaking it was an unnatural alliance, if you think about it: France, the secular, anticlerical, radical republic, and the Tsar's empire. The Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. It was an unnatural alliance, and yet France and Russia had been allies for twenty years. Why? One need only look at the map—the map of that time. Today Germany seems to us a great power. The Germany of that time was much larger. Poland didn't exist; Czechoslovakia didn't exist; the Baltic states didn't exist. There was only Germany and then, at a certain point, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany stood in the middle, sharing borders with France and Russia. And so it's clear that France and Russia had every interest in being allies.

And when the Germans gambled that there would be no war—that there would be no world war; the small war between Austria and Serbia, yes, certainly, that was perfectly fine, it was necessary—but when they gambled that there would be no war, the Germans were gambling that there would be no war against Russia and France, because that was the risk. Why did they take that risk?
