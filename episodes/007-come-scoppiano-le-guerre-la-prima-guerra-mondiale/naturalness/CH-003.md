## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second part of this talk, this chat, will be a kind of countdown through the final days. But first we need the broader picture. We need to get inside the heads of the people governing the world at that moment. What was the world like? What was Europe like at that moment? The Europe of 1914 was experiencing a period of great prosperity, of great economic expansion. After the decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy had started moving again; everyone was growing. There was even a kind of globalization avant la lettre. Well, today it's easy to find globalization everywhere in the past; I've seen every kind. Even the Roman Empire was a globalization. But in any case this one in 1914 had the features of globalization: the economies were interdependent, the stock exchanges were interdependent, the currencies were linked, anchored to gold. The Italian lira was worth exactly as much as the French franc. You could travel throughout Europe without a passport, except in Russia. You needed a passport to enter Russia. But otherwise this Europe was so interdependent that it seemed incredible that a war could break out among these powers. And yet at the same time all these countries, Italy included, were armed to the teeth. They all had military spending at a level comparable to American military spending today. They spent enormous sums on the army and the navy. Why? They did it because they were afraid of one another. One important thing in understanding the mentality of the ruling classes of that era is that they were all steeped in a kind of Darwinian ideology, but a very simplified one, right? The survival of the fittest, the struggle for survival, the survival of the strongest. They were convinced that states, too, were struggling with one another in this way. The strongest would triumph, the weakest would be eaten. And because they were convinced of this, everyone wanted to be strong and they all armed themselves in order to survive. No one imagined that they themselves would be the aggressor in the future; it was always the others who were the aggressors. We're threatened, we must be strong, we must have a great army, and since we're spending an enormous amount of money on a great army, well, we have to expect that perhaps sooner or later we'll use it. It can't be ruled out; in fact it's quite likely, some said. Those years are striking, because books came out that talked quite calmly about the next war. It was a foreseeable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911 a book came out in Germany by a German general, General von Bernhardi, entitled *Germany and the Next War*. Three years remained before the First World War. In this book there were chapters entitled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I have given a German example because German generals were particularly inclined to state these arguments explicitly, but in reality similar arguments were made in every country. And of course not everyone reasoned that way. There were also lucid people who said: look, with the interdependent economies we have, a war among the great powers would be a catastrophe. Let me give you another book from those years. These were the books prime ministers and army commanders read. They kept them on their bedside tables. In 1910 a book came out by an English writer, Norman Angell, entitled *The Great Illusion*. Do you remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? Well, it later copied its title from this book, which was very famous in its day. What was the great illusion? The author said that the great illusion was precisely that of our great powers, which believed that having a great army and preparing to win the next war was a guarantee. It wasn't. We will all lose the next war, this author said. The next war will destroy even the victors; it's an illusion to believe that strength will save us. Now, books like this circulated, politicians read them, and in the Europe of 1914 various politicians had become fairly convinced that a war among the great powers would indeed be a catastrophe. But the paradox was that these politicians were at the head of countries armed to the teeth. The scenario of the next war was so foreseeable that at a certain point this happened. Let me tell you this detail. In London, at a certain point, the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, was speaking with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. At one point Grey said to the German ambassador—listen to this—“But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.” That's the description of what I'm telling you about in this episode, the Sarajevo crisis, the crisis of the summer of 1914, except that this conversation took place two years earlier. In December 1912 Grey said these things to the German ambassador. Those were the scenarios in which a war could break out, and there were only a few places. The Balkans were a typical place, with all the unrest there. But precisely because the next war was so foreseeable, a split emerged between the politicians and the military, because the politicians said: look, we must do everything we can to avoid this scenario; it would be a catastrophe if it came to pass. But the military had a different job. The military’s job was to ask: what do we do if it happens anyway? If it happens anyway, we must be ready. So in those years of great economic growth the military pressed: let us use all this money, let us use it to become stronger. And the politicians gave in. The years preceding 1914 were years of an arms race in which every country strengthened its army, not because it wanted to attack anyone but because it was concerned about its security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passed a law under which military service, conscription, was increased from two to three years. Imagine a world in which conscription lasted three years. What did the Germans say when the French parliament passed the law increasing military service? The Germans said: the French want to make war, they're threatening us. It's a very understandable reaction that political scientists have studied. It's a reaction called the security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors become frightened, they feel less safe than before, so they arm themselves too, and so on and so on. The next-to-last thing before we return to Austria, which was deciding what to do against Serbia. It's the next-to-last piece we need to make that moment clear. The military didn't merely ask for more weapons; they also did something else. Their job was to say: if war really happens, we must be ready. Being ready meant making plans. In 1914 the great powers had not fought one another for a very long time. Almost fifty years had passed since the Crimean War, since the Franco-Prussian War. In those fifty years the general staffs had done nothing but prepare plans: if war breaks out—that particular war, against that particular enemy—what do we do? Plans were ready for every possible war, extremely detailed, extremely precise. Every year they reopened them and refined them, adjusted a detail. They had perfected them for fifty years. Plans were ready for everything. Here's an anecdote: in November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium was on a state visit to Berlin. There was a reception. The Emperor introduced General von Kluck to him, saying with a distinctly deliberate emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.” The plans were so complete that everything had already been decided, even who would command each individual army, and so on. Everything was ready, should war come. Finally, one last thing before we return to Vienna, where they were deciding exactly what to demand from the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. One last thing: this arms race of those years had another characteristic that made it very dangerous, one that made it possible to predict how the balance of power would evolve. Armaments were slow things. You laid down a battleship and it would be ready in five years; you increased conscription by one year and two years passed before the effects were felt, and so on and so on. So everyone paid close attention: when will we be at our strongest? And when will they? Russia, for example, was militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it was making enormous investments. In a few years it would be much stronger. So in this climate, you understand, the military did their job. Their job was to say: if there's a war, we must be ready. And by the way, if there's a war, when would it suit us for it to happen? Would it suit us for it to happen soon, or would it suit us for it to happen later? All military officers made these calculations. And then every so often something changed. In April 1914 it became known that Russia and Britain were negotiating a naval agreement. I have hardly mentioned Britain so far; it had no alliances. I'll come back to Britain in a moment, but it was now beginning talks with Russia. You can imagine the effect this had in Berlin. In Berlin they said: we already have Russia and France allied with each other against us in the next war, when it comes. If Britain were to ally with them too, our position would become worse. These talks between the Russians and the British began in April. In May 1914 the commander of the German army, von Moltke, stated in private, not in public: from this moment on, every day of delay can only diminish our probability of success. A few weeks later: on 1 June 1914 von Moltke declared, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.” Four weeks remained before the Sarajevo assassination.
