## 7. German war plans, Belgian neutrality, and Britain's warning

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00760–U-00859 -->

The military say war is inevitable, so let's fight it now. Let's attack. Give us the order to mobilize. The politicians say, wait a moment. From their point of view, it's essential to show that Germany isn't to blame for the war: it's all the fault of the Russians and the French, not Germany. So why do the German politicians care so much about that? There are many reasons. Perhaps it's partly about saving face, pride, a sense of honor. But they also care about German public opinion. Public opinion matters in Germany too. Germany has a huge Socialist Party. What will the socialists say? If people think Germany has been attacked, there'll be no problem—and that's exactly what happens. Germany delays mobilization, and the German people go to war convinced that innocent Germany has been attacked and must be defended.

And then they have to prevent England from entering the war. If England sees Germany doing everything it can to avoid war, perhaps it'll stay out. After all, by now it's clear that the English would like to stay out if they possibly can. But there's another problem that makes it very difficult for the Germans to keep England out. It's the last problem, so to speak, that I'll pull out of my hat: Belgium. You might ask, what does Belgium have to do with it? Quite a lot. Belgium is a small neutral country whose neutrality is guaranteed by all the great powers, including Germany and England. And the English care a great deal about this. In their view of Europe, Belgium, with Antwerp and its other ports facing the Channel, is a vital link to the continent. We English might quarrel with France, but that doesn't matter. As long as Belgium is there, we have a link to Europe. So the English don't care at all about Serbia, but they certainly would care about Belgium.

And why on earth should the Germans be worried about Belgium? Because of their plans. The German general staff has a perfect plan for fighting France and Russia and winning in a matter of weeks. Everything is calculated. The Russians are slow, so we send the whole army—the famous 11,000 trains—against France. We invade and finish the war in a few weeks. But to invade France and outflank the French army—forgive me for getting into these strategic questions—the German military have decided they need to go through Belgium. It's the famous Schlieffen Plan, which some of you may remember, refined over years and years. The military tell the politicians: if you want us to win the war for you, this is the plan. We have to go through Belgium. It's up to you to work out how to handle it.

On July 29, the Kaiser and the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, realize that if they're gambling on England staying out of the war, they need to work out what to do about Belgium. After all, the Germans are going to invade it. That's already been decided. So the Kaiser and the chancellor make a crucial decision, one that everyone will later judge the stupidest decision the Germans make in those days. They write to the English government in London and offer guarantees. First: if war breaks out against France, the English can rest easy. Germany has no territorial ambitions. We don't want to take any pieces of France, so we don't want to upset the European balance. Second: the Germans assure the English government that if they're forced to invade Belgium during the war, they'll evacuate it when the war is over. And if the Belgians behave themselves and don't prove hostile, they'll even be paid damages. Finally, the Germans write to London: after all, think about this carefully. We Germans and you English could still come to an agreement. Together we're tremendously strong. We could divide up the world. Why do you care about being friends with France? Why do you care about Belgium? Let's divide the world between us.

In London, they see this message arrive and are stunned. A senior Foreign Office official comments: “The only comment that need be made on these astounding proposals is that they reflect discredit on the statesman who makes them.” And on top of that, the English say, they're telling us they're going to invade Belgium. They've actually put it in writing. Grey calls the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, and tells him: look, if war breaks out, we can't stay out. At that point, we'll enter the war too, alongside our friends. Grey also says something that everyone is repeating almost obsessively in those days: “if war did break out, it would be the greatest catastrophe that the world had ever seen” But England can't make this agreement with Germany. Betraying its friends would be an indelible disgrace, so think very carefully in Berlin. And three: as the English see it, the responsibility for everything lies in Berlin. Berlin is where they must take a step back.

At this news from London, the German reaction is uncontrollable. The Kaiser writes on the message: “Aha! The vile deceiver! Vile blackguard! I will never make a naval agreement with such scoundrels! The vile shopkeeper rabble tried to deceive us with dinners and speeches. But he avoids saying the word and threatens us instead! Vile blackguard! England alone bears responsibility for war and peace, no longer we! That must also be made clear publicly.” And four: as the Germans see it, responsibility lies with the English. That same day, July 29, the German ambassador in Brussels, von Below-Saleske, receives a sealed packet from Berlin with orders not to open it until further instructions arrive by telegram. Another useless detail—we're nearly there, but I'll inflict it on you anyway. Von Below-Saleske is a career diplomat who likes to joke that he brings bad luck. He was in China, and the Boxer Rebellion broke out. He was in Turkey, and the Young Turk Revolution broke out. But now, he says, I can relax. I'm in Belgium. Nothing will ever happen in Brussels. Let's pick up the pace, or I'll keep you here all night.
