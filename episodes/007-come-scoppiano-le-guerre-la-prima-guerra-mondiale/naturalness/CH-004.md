## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Did von Moltke organize the Sarajevo assassination? No, obviously not. It was the catalyst that brought all these forces, simmering for years, to the surface. So let's return to Vienna, where they were preparing the ultimatum to Serbia. It would be extremely harsh because the Austrians didn't want the Serbs to accept it. The Austrians wanted war, and they had a blank cheque from Germany.

The Germans kept pressing Vienna: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli. The sooner you act, the better. The Russians won't dare intervene. But Austria was a country where things moved slowly. There were two governments, Austrian and Hungarian, and they had to agree. The Austrian army was slow and backward too. It needed a long time to prepare. And then there was something no one would think about today: it was July, harvest time. Austria was an agricultural country, so the soldiers were all home on leave to bring in the grain. All this took time. The Austrians weren't in a hurry. In Berlin, they were champing at the bit: we're always being dragged along behind this backward country.

Finally, on July 23, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum was ready. It was delivered to the Serbian government, and it was extremely harsh. The Austrians told Serbia: your government has done nothing to stop terrorism from spreading. Serbian officers and officials are involved in the Sarajevo assassination. So we Austrians wanted the Serbian government to publish a declaration renouncing every form of hostility toward Austria. It would have to close every newspaper and dissolve every association that sows hatred against Austria. It would have to dismiss every public official and officer involved in any kind of hostility toward Austria, and arrest the people on a list supplied by the Austrians. Finally, it would have to open investigations into terrorism on Serbian soil, with Austrian investigators taking part.

On July 24, capitals around the world woke to news of the ultimatum, and the reaction was extremely negative. In St. Petersburg, the Russian foreign minister summoned the Austrian ambassador and asked him: do you want to make war on Serbia? Are the Germans inciting you? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That was number one: for the Russians, responsibility lay with Vienna and the Austrians. We'll hear a whole series of these statements—*you are the ones assuming responsibility*. This was just the first.

In London, Foreign Secretary Grey was aghast too. He called in the German ambassador, Lichnowsky, and told him: I've never seen one state send another a document like this. It's impossible for the Serbs to accept it. And here we get a small period detail, something that gives us a sense of the age. Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador, said: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!”

So now we're in London, listening to this exchange between Grey and Lichnowsky. What was happening there? For the moment, the German gamble seemed to be holding. British public opinion didn't give the slightest damn about Serbia. Britain had no alliances. It did have a long-standing friendship with France, it must be said. British and French military officers regularly consulted one another. They had even prepared plans together and become fairly friendly. But there were no obligations, no treaties.

And at this point Britain was an isolationist country. It dominated the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock exchange—and it didn't give a damn about minor disputes on the continent, especially in the Balkans. British newspapers were asking: why should we let ourselves be dragged into somebody else's war? The *Manchester Guardian* wrote on July 30, after the ultimatum: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017]

The proof that Britain had so far stayed completely outside this kind of thinking comes from the records of British cabinet meetings. From the Sarajevo assassination on June 28 until the Austrian ultimatum throws the whole world into an uproar on July 24, the British government never discussed foreign policy for almost a month. It had problems of its own. Ireland wasn't yet independent. There was terrorism, there was everything—a revolution was about to break out. For the British, foreign policy didn't exist.

Only a few people in Britain thought differently. Some Foreign Office officials were frightened by what was happening; we know this from the notes they exchanged during those days. And here again is the security paradox. We're fairly safe, but Germany is becoming very strong. The Germans say they're only building their strength because they're afraid of us. But what if they become too strong? A senior Foreign Office official argued that Britain must stand with France and Russia, because this struggle isn't about possession of Serbia. It's a struggle between a Germany aspiring to political dictatorship in Europe and the powers that want to remain free.

There were others in Britain who thought this way: the military. British officers were used to seeing themselves as friends of the French and doing things alongside them. They knew it would be difficult for Britain to enter a war alongside France because public opinion didn't want it, and they'd been brooding over that problem for years. It's striking to see how long they'd been thinking, talking, and arguing among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do?

There's a revealing anecdote from 1910. Wilson, an English general who was very friendly with the French, was talking to the French general Foch. Wilson asked: if war breaks out, assuming we come to help you—I hope we do, but who knows—what's the smallest number of British soldiers who could really help France? Remember, this is four years earlier, in 1910. What's the smallest number of British soldiers who could help France? Foch replied: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is mobilizing British public opinion behind a cause.

It's almost midnight. Let's begin the countdown. On July 25, the Serbian reply to the ultimatum arrived.
