## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Does that mean von Moltke organizes the Sarajevo assassination? Obviously not. The assassination is the catalyst that brings everything simmering for years to the surface.

Let's go back to Vienna, where they're preparing the ultimatum for Serbia. It's going to be extremely harsh because the Austrians don't want the Serbs to accept it. Austria wants war, and it has a blank check from Germany. The Germans keep pushing Vienna: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli. The sooner you act, the better. The Russians won't dare step in.

But Austria does things slowly. It has two governments, Austrian and Hungarian, and they have to agree. Its army is slow and backward too, so getting ready takes time. And here's something no one today would think of: it's July, harvest season. Austria is an agricultural country, and the soldiers are all home on leave to bring in the grain. That takes time. The Austrians aren't in a hurry. In Berlin, the Germans are beside themselves: we're always being dragged behind this backward country.

Finally, on July 23, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum is ready and delivered to the Serbian government. It is extraordinarily severe. Austria accuses the Serbian government of doing nothing to stop the spread of terrorism and says Serbian officers and officials took part in the Sarajevo assassination. It demands that Serbia publish a statement rejecting every form of hostility toward Austria. Serbia must shut down every newspaper and dissolve every organization that spreads hatred of Austria. It must fire every public official and military officer connected in any way to anti-Austrian activity. It must arrest the people on a list supplied by Austria. And it must open terrorism investigations on Serbian soil with Austrian investigators taking part.

On July 24, capitals around the world wake up to the ultimatum, and the reaction is extremely negative. In St. Petersburg, the Russian foreign minister summons the Austrian ambassador. Do you want war with Serbia? he asks. Are the Germans pushing you into it? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That's number one: as far as Russia is concerned, the responsibility belongs to Vienna and the Austrians. We'll hear one version after another of this phrase, “you are the ones assuming responsibility.” This is only the first.

In London, Foreign Secretary Grey is appalled too. He calls in the German ambassador, Lichnowsky, and tells him he's never seen one state send another a document like this. The Serbs can't possibly accept it. And here's a small detail that gives you a feel for the period. Prince Lichnowsky says: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!”

So now we're in London, listening to Grey and Lichnowsky. What's happening there? For the moment, the German gamble seems to be paying off. The British public couldn't care less about Serbia. Britain isn't allied with anyone. It does have a longstanding friendship with France, to be sure. British and French military officers regularly consult one another. They've even developed plans together, and they've become fairly friendly. But there are no commitments and no treaties.

At this point Britain is isolationist. It dominates the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock market—and couldn't care less about minor disputes on the European continent, especially in the Balkans. British newspapers ask why Britain should let itself be dragged into someone else's war. On July 30, after the ultimatum, the *Manchester Guardian* writes: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017]

The cabinet minutes show just how far removed Britain is from all of this. From June 28, the day of the Sarajevo assassination, to July 24, when Austria's ultimatum to Serbia throws the world into an uproar, the British government goes almost a month without discussing foreign policy. Britain has problems of its own. Ireland isn't independent yet. There's terrorism, turmoil of every kind, a revolution about to break out. For the British government, foreign policy might as well not exist.

Still, a few people in Britain see things differently. We know from the notes exchanged in those days that some Foreign Office officials are afraid of what's happening. Once again, it's the security paradox. We're fairly safe, but Germany is growing very strong. The Germans say they're only doing it because they're afraid of us. But if they become too strong, a senior Foreign Office official argues, we have to stand with France and Russia. This fight isn't about control of Serbia. It's between a Germany seeking political dictatorship over Europe and the powers that want to remain free.

Some people in Britain agree, especially the military. British officers have come to see themselves as friends of the French, used to working alongside them. They know it will be hard to bring Britain into a war on France's side because the public doesn't want it. They've been turning the problem over for years. It's striking how long they've spent thinking, talking, and debating among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do?

There's a revealing story from 1910. The English general Wilson, a close friend of the French, is talking to the French general Foch. If war breaks out, Wilson asks, and if we come help you—I hope we will, but who knows—what is the smallest number of British soldiers who could make a real difference for France? Remember, this is 1910, four years before the war. What is the minimum? Foch answers: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is rallying British public opinion behind a cause.

It's almost midnight. Let's begin the countdown. On July 25, Serbia's reply to the ultimatum arrives.
