## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we started thinking about what I might come and talk about here in Sarzana, the atmosphere was already much as it is now. There was news from Ukraine and from Iraq. Pope Francis hadn't yet said: “Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction...” But he did say it a short while ago. So clearly, asking how wars break out is becoming more timely all the time. There—do I need to repeat the first part? No, it was only a way into the subject. This works just as well. How do wars break out? I think what I'm going to tell you is fairly reassuring, because it shows that wars break out in situations—the great world wars break out in situations—quite different from the one we're experiencing now. Today we're experiencing a disastrous international situation, full of suffering, full of uncertainties, but still quite different from the scenarios that were taking shape in Europe, for example in the Europe of 1914, which is where we begin this episode.

How did the First World War break out? Well, deep down we all know: it began with an assassination, the Sarajevo assassination. On 28 June 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to say about the outbreak of the First World War is that it arose from an act of terrorism. And terrorism, then as now, provoked visceral reactions in public opinion and therefore in governments as well. To us today, it may seem that killing an archduke is perhaps, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. But one thing we have to understand very clearly about the people of 1914 is that, for them—at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who was heir to the imperial throne was far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers would have been, if they could ever have imagined such a thing.

Just think: the Austro-Hungarian Empire was a great power. It was still a great power—weakened, in crisis—but it was one of the few great powers in the world. Emperor Franz Joseph ruled the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Emperor Franz Joseph was eighty-three years old, which meant he was going to die very soon. No one knew when, but he was certain to die very soon. Franz Joseph had reigned over the Austrian Empire since time immemorial; he was nearing sixty-six years on the throne. He would die very soon, everyone knew that. In fact, he would die on 21 November 1916, a little over two years later; we know that. [N-002] And when Franz Joseph died, Archduke Franz Ferdinand would become the sovereign of one of the world's great powers for the rest of his life. You see how killing him changes the situation. It wasn't like killing a president of the United States, because another one is chosen four years later anyway. Here they were killing someone who would have helped govern the world for the rest of his life. So the assassination was extremely serious: an act of terrorism of enormous gravity, with complicity abroad.

Here we have to remember—and I'll spend the whole episode telling you, “We have to remember this,” so forgive me—that the way a war breaks out isn't so simple. There's a whole series of connections. The first—the first thing to focus on—is Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia was then, just as it has been in our own time, a sensitive place. Bosnia was also a place where hostile ethnic groups lived side by side. Bosnia had been occupied by Austria-Hungary for some years, and some of its inhabitants were happy about it. The Croat population was happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslim population was very happy to belong to the Austrian Empire. Indeed, Bosnian Muslim soldiers would fight faithfully for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments wearing fezzes.

But in Serbia—in Serbia, in Bosnia—there was a section of the Serbian population that wasn't at all happy to belong to Austria. Because right next door was Serbia, the Kingdom of Serbia: independent Serbia, small but bellicose, a battle-hardened state with military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs suffered under Austrian imperial rule. And so there was discontent, and there was terrorism. And the Austrians were certain that this terrorism was backed, supported, and organized in Serbia; that this assassination, in which the Archduke and his wife had been killed, had been organized in Serbia with complicity within the Serbian government and the Serbian army. The Austrians were certain of it from the very first day. Among other things, it was true: the investigations would bring it to light; there's no doubt about it. There was such complicity in Serbia.

And so Austria was determined to punish Serbia. And here you really have to make an effort. Don't think of Austria and Serbia as forgotten states from another age—who remembers them now? No. Here there's a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad, and there's the country that suffered the terrorist attack, which is determined to punish the rogue state. In Austria there was a wave of mass indignation. Crowds were in the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” In German it even has assonance: *Serbien muss sterben*.
