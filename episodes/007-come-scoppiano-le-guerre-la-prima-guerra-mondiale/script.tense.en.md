# Come scoppiano le guerre: La prima guerra mondiale — faithful English translation

## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we started thinking about what I might come and talk about here in Sarzana, the atmosphere was already much as it is now. There was news from Ukraine and from Iraq. Pope Francis hadn't yet said: “Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction...” But he does say it a short while ago. So clearly, asking how wars break out is becoming more timely all the time. There—do I need to repeat the first part? No, it is only a way into the subject. This works just as well. How do wars break out? I think what I'm going to tell you is fairly reassuring, because it shows that wars break out in situations—the great world wars break out in situations—quite different from the one we're experiencing now. Today we're experiencing a disastrous international situation, full of suffering, full of uncertainties, but still quite different from the scenarios that are taking shape in Europe, for example in the Europe of 1914, which is where we begin this episode.

How does the First World War break out? Well, deep down we all know: it begins with an assassination, the Sarajevo assassination. On 28 June 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to say about the outbreak of the First World War is that it arose from an act of terrorism. And terrorism, then as now, provoked visceral reactions in public opinion and therefore in governments as well. To us today, it may seem that killing an archduke is perhaps, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. But one thing we have to understand very clearly about the people of 1914 is that, for them—at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who is heir to the imperial throne is far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers will have been, if they can ever have imagine such a thing.

Just think: the Austro-Hungarian Empire is a great power. It is still a great power—weakened, in crisis—but it is one of the few great powers in the world. Emperor Franz Joseph rules the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Emperor Franz Joseph is eighty-three years old, which meant he is going to die very soon. No one knew when, but he is certain to die very soon. Franz Joseph has reigned over the Austrian Empire since time immemorial; he is nearing sixty-six years on the throne. He will die very soon, everyone knew that. In fact, he will die on 21 November 1916, a little over two years later; we know that. [N-002] And when Franz Joseph died, Archduke Franz Ferdinand will become the sovereign of one of the world's great powers for the rest of his life. You see how killing him changes the situation. It wasn't like killing a president of the United States, because another one is chosen four years later anyway. Here they are killing someone who will have helped govern the world for the rest of his life. So the assassination is extremely serious: an act of terrorism of enormous gravity, with complicity abroad.

Here we have to remember—and I'll spend the whole episode telling you, “We have to remember this,” so forgive me—that the way a war breaks out isn't so simple. There's a whole series of connections. The first—the first thing to focus on—is Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia is then, just as it has been in our own time, a sensitive place. Bosnia is also a place where hostile ethnic groups lived side by side. Bosnia has been occupied by Austria-Hungary for some years, and some of its inhabitants are happy about it. The Croat population is happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslim population is very happy to belong to the Austrian Empire. Indeed, Bosnian Muslim soldiers will fight faithfully for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments wearing fezzes.

But in Serbia—in Serbia, in Bosnia—there is a section of the Serbian population that wasn't at all happy to belong to Austria. Because right next door is Serbia, the Kingdom of Serbia: independent Serbia, small but bellicose, a battle-hardened state with military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs suffered under Austrian imperial rule. And so there is discontent, and there is terrorism. And the Austrians are certain that this terrorism is backed, supported, and organized in Serbia; that this assassination, in which the Archduke and his wife has been killed, has been organized in Serbia with complicity within the Serbian government and the Serbian army. The Austrians are certain of it from the very first day. Among other things, it is true: the investigations will bring it to light; there's no doubt about it. There is such complicity in Serbia.

And so Austria is determined to punish Serbia. And here you really have to make an effort. Don't think of Austria and Serbia as forgotten states from another age—who remembers them now? No. Here there's a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad, and there's the country that suffered the terrorist attack, which is determined to punish the rogue state. In Austria there is a wave of mass indignation. Crowds are in the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” In German it even has assonance: *Serbien muss sterben*.

## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there is a problem: Serbia has a great friend, a great protector—Russia. The Russia of Tsar Nicholas II. The friendship between Serbia and Russia is an obvious fact of European geography; it's true even today. Russia is a Slavic, Orthodox power; the Serbs are Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let's broaden the picture for a moment. Serbia has a great friend, Russia. Everyone in that world of 1914 tried to have friends and allies. The weight of alliances is a fundamental factor in understanding how the world slid into world war, because that wasn't a world of blocs. Well, ours is no longer one either by now—only the American bloc remains—but in any case we all remember when our world is divided into two blocs and nothing happened outside those two blocs. The world of 1914 wasn't like that. It is a world of great and small powers. Which ones are the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and then, as a courtesy, Italy usually makes the list too—the last of the great powers, with a little effort. And then there are the small powers, but they too are bellicose, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They weren't divided into blocs; each looks after itself.

This is the age in which nationalism is at its height. Every country is convinced that it has a duty to look after itself. It's what an Italian politician, Prime Minister Salandra, in those very days will call “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But if you have to look after yourself, you also has to find allies, because on your own you'd come to a bad end. So each country seek alliances for its own security. Serbia is allied with Russia.

The Austrians think the world will understand: Serbia is clearly a rogue state that has organized terrorism; it has to be punished for everyone's security. But they begins to wonder whether, if Austria attacked Serbia, the Russians might intervene. But Austria also has a powerful friend, one more powerful than itself. Austria is a great empire in decline—almost everyone suspected as much—whereas its immensely powerful friend that is becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. The Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm—you remember Wilhelm with the great handlebar moustache and the spiked helmet? But beyond the Kaiser, who is a somewhat comical figure, Germany is a very great power. It has the strongest army in the world and is building the strongest industry in the world. In fact, Germany has already become the greatest industrial power; it has already overtaken Britain.

Austria is allied with Germany in an alliance that binds the two countries to help one another if either is attacked. To tell the truth, the alliance also includes Italy. It iscallsthe Triple Alliance for a reason. But during those days of crisis in 1914 it is perfectly clear that no one has any faith in Italy. The Austrians didn't believe that, if they goes to war, Italy will come to their aid. They rules the whole idea out from the start, and in fact they are right. In the name of sacred egoism, Italy will decide to stay out for the time being.

But there is Germany. Austria has enough good sense to know that, before doing anything against Serbia, it has to ask what the Germans think. Franz Ferdinand's funeral takes place on 3 July. The Emperor, the old Emperor Franz Joseph, has signed his appeal to Kaiser Wilhelm on 2 July; it is delivered to Wilhelm on 5 July with the accompanying memorandum. [N-005] Now, this letter from the Emperor of Austria is very interesting. It'sn't precise or lucid; it doesn't say, “This is what we want to do.” It's a letter more or less along these lines. Franz Joseph says to Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” This is a letter that makes me think of the reasons with which the Western powers goes into Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. In other words, something must be done. There's no lucid reasoning about the consequences, no consideration of what today iscallsan exit strategy. “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agreed. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reached him, Kaiser Wilhelm writes on the telegram: “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.” Consequently, on 5 July Kaiser Wilhelm replies to his Austrian counterpart, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser also added: “Really, don't worry. The Russians won't have the courage to interfere. We're certain Russia will stay out.” That is the first gamble. This evening's story is a story of one gamble after another, all of them wrong. As we'll see, wars break out not because someone wants them. No one ever wants war, at least not world war, general war; no one ever wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

I want to pause for another moment over this assurance Kaiser Wilhelm sent to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we're with you, don't worry.” It's a reassurance that historians latercallsthe blank cheque. How is this blank cheque given? Germany is already a parliamentary country; it has political parties, a parliament, free elections. The Kaiser consulted no one. Before consulting anyone at all, he himself writes to his counterpart, the Emperor of Austria, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then the Kaiser does consult someone. Whom? Parliament? Not a chance. He consulted the Chancellor—the prime minister, that's. He consulted the commander of the army, General von Moltke, and General von Moltke tells him, “If war breaks out, we're ready.” And then—this is interesting—whom else does Kaiser Wilhelm consult? He consulted Alfred Krupp, the great arms industrialist. Krupp is the great—the world's largest—arms factory. The Kaiser feels the need to consult Alfred Krupp. If war should ever break out, are the Krupp works ready? And Alfred Krupp reassured him: the Krupp works are ready.

But they are certain that war will not break out. Indeed, after sending the blank cheque to Vienna, the Kaiser boarded his yacht and goes on a cruise in the Baltic. It is July. People want their holidays, and the Kaiser goes on holiday too. This German gamble—that yes, there is a risk of war, but you will see that war will not come—is a crucial aspect of the argument we must discuss this episode, so I must dwell on it a little longer.

The gamble is even greater than it appeared because of the question of alliances, since Russia wasn't the only party involved. Russia is Serbia's great friend, but Russia has an alliance with France. Politically speaking it is an unnatural alliance, if you think about it: France, the secular, anticlerical, radical republic, and the Tsar's empire. The Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. It is an unnatural alliance, and yet France and Russia have been allies for twenty years. Why? One need only look at the map—the map of that time. Today Germany seems to us a great power. The Germany of that time is much larger. Poland didn't exist; Czechoslovakia didn't exist; the Baltic states didn't exist. There is only Germany and then, at a certain point, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany stands in the middle, sharing borders with France and Russia. And so it's clear that France and Russia have every interest in being allies.

And when the Germans gambled that there will be no war—that there will be no world war; the small war between Austria and Serbia, yes, certainly, that is perfectly fine, it is necessary—but when they gambled that there will be no war, the Germans are gambling that there will be no war against Russia and France, because that is the risk. Why do they take that risk?

## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second part of this talk, this chat, will be a kind of countdown through the final days. But first we need the broader picture. We need to get inside the heads of the people governing the world at that moment. What is the world like? What is Europe like at that moment? The Europe of 1914 is experiencing a period of great prosperity, of great economic expansion. After the decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy has started moving again; everyone is growing. There is even a kind of globalization avant la lettre. Well, today it's easy to find globalization everywhere in the past; I've seen every kind. Even the Roman Empire is a globalization. But in any case this one in 1914 has the features of globalization: the economies are interdependent, the stock exchanges are interdependent, the currencies are linked, anchored to gold. The Italian lira is worth exactly as much as the French franc. You can travel throughout Europe without a passport, except in Russia. You needed a passport to enter Russia. But otherwise this Europe is so interdependent that it seems incredible that a war can break out among these powers. And yet at the same time all these countries, Italy includes, are armed to the teeth. They all has military spending at a level comparable to American military spending today. They spend enormous sums on the army and the navy. Why? They does it because they are afraid of one another. One important thing in understanding the mentality of the ruling classes of that era is that they are all steeped in a kind of Darwinian ideology, but a very simplified one, right? The survival of the fittest, the struggle for survival, the survival of the strongest. They are convinced that states, too, are struggling with one another in this way. The strongest will triumph, the weakest will be eaten. And because they are convinced of this, everyone want to be strong and they all armed themselves in order to survive. No one imagine that they themselves will be the aggressor in the future; it is always the others who are the aggressors. We're threatened, we must be strong, we must have a great army, and since we're spending an enormous amount of money on a great army, well, we have to expect that perhaps sooner or later we'll use it. It can't be rules out; in fact it's quite likely, some says. Those years are striking, because books come out that talked quite calmly about the next war. It is a foreseeable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911 a book comes out in Germany by a German general, General von Bernhardi, entitled *Germany and the Next War*. Three years remain before the First World War. In this book there are chapters entitled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I have given a German example because German generals are particularly inclined to state these arguments explicitly, but in reality similar arguments are makes in every country. And of course not everyone reason that way. There are also lucid people who says: look, with the interdependent economies we have, a war among the great powers will be a catastrophe. Let me give you another book from those years. These are the books prime ministers and army commanders read. They keep them on their bedside tables. In 1910 a book comes out by an English writer, Norman Angell, entitled *The Great Illusion*. Do you remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? Well, it later copied its title from this book, which is very famous in its day. What is the great illusion? The author says that the great illusion is precisely that of our great powers, which believed that having a great army and preparing to win the next war is a guarantee. It wasn't. We will all lose the next war, this author says. The next war will destroy even the victors; it's an illusion to believe that strength will save us. Now, books like this circulated, politicians read them, and in the Europe of 1914 various politicians have become fairly convinced that a war among the great powers will indeed be a catastrophe. But the paradox is that these politicians are at the head of countries armed to the teeth. The scenario of the next war is so foreseeable that at a certain point this happened. Let me tell you this detail. In London, at a certain point, the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, is speaking with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. At one point Grey says to the German ambassador—listen to this—“But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.” That's the description of what I'm telling you about in this episode, the Sarajevo crisis, the crisis of the summer of 1914, except that this conversation takes place two years earlier. In December 1912 Grey says these things to the German ambassador. Those are the scenarios in which a war can break out, and there are only a few places. The Balkans are a typical place, with all the unrest there. But precisely because the next war is so foreseeable, a split emerges between the politicians and the military, because the politicians says: look, we must do everything we can to avoid this scenario; it will be a catastrophe if it comes to pass. But the military has a different job. The military’s job is to ask: what do we do if it happens anyway? If it happens anyway, we must be ready. So in those years of great economic growth the military press: let us use all this money, let us use it to become stronger. And the politicians give in. The years preceding 1914 are years of an arms race in which every country strengthened its army, not because it want to attack anyone but because it is concerned about its security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passes a law under which military service, conscription, is increased from two to three years. Imagine a world in which conscription lasted three years. What does the Germans say when the French parliament passes the law increasing military service? The Germans says: the French want to make war, they're threatening us. It's a very understandable reaction that political scientists have studied. It's a reactioncallsthe security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors become frightened, they feel less safe than before, so they arm themselves too, and so on and so on. The next-to-last thing before we return to Austria, which is deciding what to do against Serbia. It's the next-to-last piece we need to make that moment clear. The military didn't merely ask for more weapons; they also does something else. Their job is to say: if war really happens, we must be ready. Being ready meant making plans. In 1914 the great powers have not fought one another for a very long time. Almost fifty years has passes since the Crimean War, since the Franco-Prussian War. In those fifty years the general staffs have done nothing but prepare plans: if war breaks out—that particular war, against that particular enemy—what do we do? Plans are ready for every possible war, extremely detailed, extremely precise. Every year they reopened them and refined them, adjusted a detail. They have perfected them for fifty years. Plans are ready for everything. Here's an anecdote: in November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium is on a state visit to Berlin. There is a reception. The Emperor introduced General von Kluck to him, saying with a distinctly deliberate emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.” The plans are so complete that everything has already been decided, even who will command each individual army, and so on. Everything is ready, should war come. Finally, one last thing before we return to Vienna, where they are deciding exactly what to demand from the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. One last thing: this arms race of those years has another characteristic that makes it very dangerous, one that makes it possible to predict how the balance of power will evolve. Armaments are slow things. You laid down a battleship and it will be ready in five years; you increased conscription by one year and two years passes before the effects are felt, and so on and so on. So everyone paid close attention: when will we be at our strongest? And when will they? Russia, for example, is militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it is making enormous investments. In a few years it will be much stronger. So in this climate, you understand, the military do their job. Their job is to say: if there's a war, we must be ready. And by the way, if there's a war, when will it suit us for it to happen? Would it suit us for it to happen soon, or will it suit us for it to happen later? All military officers makes these calculations. And then every so often something changed. In April 1914 it becomes known that Russia and Britain are negotiating a naval agreement. I have hardly mentioned Britain so far; it has no alliances. I'll come back to Britain in a moment, but it is now beginning talks with Russia. You can imagine the effect this has in Berlin. In Berlin they says: we already have Russia and France allied with each other against us in the next war, when it comes. If Britain are to ally with them too, our position will become worse. These talks between the Russians and the British begins in April. In May 1914 the commander of the German army, von Moltke, states in private, not in public: from this moment on, every day of delay can only diminish our probability of success. A few weeks later: on 1 June 1914 von Moltke declares, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.” Four weeks remain before the Sarajevo assassination.

## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Did von Moltke organize the Sarajevo assassination? No, obviously not. It is the catalyst that brings all these forces, simmering for years, to the surface. So let's return to Vienna, where they are preparing the ultimatum to Serbia. It will be extremely harsh because the Austrians don't want the Serbs to accept it. The Austrians want war, and they have a blank cheque from Germany.

The Germans keep pressing Vienna: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli. The sooner you act, the better. The Russians won't dare intervene. But Austria is a country where things move slowly. There are two governments, Austrian and Hungarian, and they have to agree. The Austrian army is slow and backward too. It needs a long time to prepare. And then there is something no one would think about today: it is July, harvest time. Austria is an agricultural country, so the soldiers are all home on leave to bring in the grain. All this takes time. The Austrians aren't in a hurry. In Berlin, they are champing at the bit: we're always being dragged along behind this backward country.

Finally, on July 23, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum is ready. It is delivered to the Serbian government, and it is extremely harsh. The Austrians tell Serbia: your government has done nothing to stop terrorism from spreading. Serbian officers and officials are involved in the Sarajevo assassination. So we Austrians want the Serbian government to publish a declaration renouncing every form of hostility toward Austria. It will have to close every newspaper and dissolve every association that sows hatred against Austria. It will have to dismiss every public official and officer involved in any kind of hostility toward Austria, and arrest the people on a list supplied by the Austrians. Finally, it will have to open investigations into terrorism on Serbian soil, with Austrian investigators taking part.

On July 24, capitals around the world wake to news of the ultimatum, and the reaction is extremely negative. In St. Petersburg, the Russian foreign minister summons the Austrian ambassador and asks him: do you want to make war on Serbia? Are the Germans inciting you? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That is number one: for the Russians, responsibility lies with Vienna and the Austrians. We'll hear a whole series of these statements—*you are the ones assuming responsibility*. This is just the first.

In London, Foreign Secretary Grey is aghast too. He calls in the German ambassador, Lichnowsky, and tells him: I've never seen one state send another a document like this. It's impossible for the Serbs to accept it. And here we get a small period detail, something that gives us a sense of the age. Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador, says: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!”

So now we're in London, listening to this exchange between Grey and Lichnowsky. What is happening there? For the moment, the German gamble seems to be holding. British public opinion doesn't give the slightest damn about Serbia. Britain has no alliances. It does have a long-standing friendship with France, it must be said. British and French military officers regularly consult one another. They have even prepared plans together and become fairly friendly. But there are no obligations, no treaties.

And at this point Britain is an isolationist country. It dominates the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock exchange—and it doesn't give a damn about minor disputes on the continent, especially in the Balkans. British newspapers are asking: why should we let ourselves be dragged into somebody else's war? The *Manchester Guardian* writes on July 30, after the ultimatum: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017]

The proof that Britain has so far stayed completely outside this kind of thinking comes from the records of British cabinet meetings. From the Sarajevo assassination on June 28 until the Austrian ultimatum throws the whole world into an uproar on July 24, the British government never discusses foreign policy for almost a month. It has problems of its own. Ireland isn't yet independent. There is terrorism, there is everything—a revolution is about to break out. For the British, foreign policy doesn't exist.

Only a few people in Britain think differently. Some Foreign Office officials are frightened by what is happening; we know this from the notes they exchange during those days. And here again is the security paradox. We're fairly safe, but Germany is becoming very strong. The Germans say they're only building their strength because they're afraid of us. But what if they become too strong? A senior Foreign Office official argues that Britain must stand with France and Russia, because this struggle isn't about possession of Serbia. It's a struggle between a Germany aspiring to political dictatorship in Europe and the powers that want to remain free.

There are others in Britain who think this way: the military. British officers are used to seeing themselves as friends of the French and doing things alongside them. They know it will be difficult for Britain to enter a war alongside France because public opinion doesn't want it, and they have been brooding over that problem for years. It's striking to see how long they have been thinking, talking, and arguing among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do?

There's a revealing anecdote from 1910. Wilson, an English general who is very friendly with the French, is talking to the French general Foch. Wilson asks: if war breaks out, assuming we come to help you—I hope we do, but who knows—what's the smallest number of British soldiers who could really help France? Remember, this is four years earlier, in 1910. What's the smallest number of British soldiers who could help France? Foch replies: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is mobilizing British public opinion behind a cause.

It's almost midnight. Let's begin the countdown. On July 25, the Serbian reply to the ultimatum arrives.

## 5. Serbia's reply, Churchill's fleet, and mobilization logic

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00588–U-00679 -->

The Serbs accept almost all the conditions. In the meantime, Serbia appeals to the tsar, seeking assurance that Russia will support it if it's attacked. The tsar replies: you can count on us. That day, he makes a symbolic decision—symbolic gestures matter in politics too. Every final-year officer cadet in Russia is commissioned at the same hour: the moment the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia expires. The message couldn't be clearer.

July 26: Austria rejects the Serbian reply, which means it's going to war. That day, something happens in London. It's a detail—I've filled this episode with details, so I'm going to run late and you'll have to be patient. I just couldn't bring myself to cut many of them. And this one seems to me, it seems to me, another curious detail in its own way.

On the day Austria rejects the Serbian reply, the First Lord of the Admiralty, Britain's navy minister, makes a decision on his own initiative. The British battle fleet is taking part in major maneuvers. It's assembled in one place with full crews. The maneuvers end tomorrow, when each squadron will return to its home port and the sailors will go on leave. Instead, the First Lord gives an order: tomorrow the fleet isn't to disperse. It will remain together and move to the naval base at Scapa Flow in the Orkneys, where it will be safe.

I'm telling you this because the First Lord of the Admiralty in 1914 is Winston Churchill, whom we tend to associate with the Second World War. He was still alive when I was a child, and many of us—well, we remember him. Yet few people remember that Churchill was precocious. He is a precocious young man, already navy minister in 1914. He's the one who decides that the battle fleet won't disperse. It will remain ready and take shelter at the naval base. The next day, the German stock market collapses. The news from London is enough to create doubts. Perhaps Britain won't stay out after all.

There's still one thing I have to explain: the constraints acting on these soldiers and politicians. It's the last thing, I hope, that interrupts our countdown. At this point, war is certain—at least between Austria and Serbia. We'll see what happens beyond that. To wage war, the first thing these countries have to do is order mobilization. What does that mean? They have conscript armies. Their countries are full of young men, and older men, who completed military service in previous years and can always be called back if war breaks out—or rather, who are certain to be called back. If war begins, the army must immediately expand, double, or triple in size by calling up everyone at home. That's mobilization.

It's an extremely complicated process. It means calling up millions of men, putting them in uniform, arming them, organizing their units, and loading them onto trains bound for the frontier. All of it follows plans calculated down to the smallest detail. German mobilization, timed to the minute and second, involves the simultaneous movement of 11,000 trains. The Germans are used to doing these things well. Everything is scheduled to the minute. Mobilization is an enormously consequential decision. Among other things, it costs a tremendous amount of money, so nobody does it unless the situation has become truly serious.

But some countries mobilize more slowly than others. Take Russia. Russia is immense and has few railways. It takes months to mobilize its army. So when a situation becomes serious, Russia has to start mobilizing fairly quickly. It can't afford to wait. And indeed, on July 26, the first country that starts talking about mobilization isn't Austria, even though Austria is about to make war on Serbia. Serbia is small; Austria doesn't need its entire army to fight Serbia. The first country to talk about mobilization is Russia, because it knows the process will take a long time.

Then there's another country in Europe—let's see if you can guess which one—that's certain it can mobilize extremely quickly. Its plans are so precise that its leaders have already decided that if they mobilize, it means there's a war. There's no interval, no distinction between the two decisions. If they mobilize, they might as well go to war immediately, immediately, immediately. Has everyone got it? It's Germany.

So on July 26, the same day Austria rejects Serbia's very moderate reply, German Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg warns the Russian government: “Preparatory military measures by Russia would compel us to take countermeasures. Mobilization, however, means war.” But we can't believe that Russia wants to unleash a European war like this. And that's number two: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the Russians. The Russians are the ones who must step back and not mobilize.

## 6. Austria declares war and the cousins exchange telegrams

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00680–U-00759 -->

July 28 brings a twist. The Kaiser returns from his cruise and reads Serbia's reply to the Austrian ultimatum. He says: but this is excellent. It's everything we wanted and more. The Serbian reply removes every reason for war. The Kaiser's joy is the first sign that the Germans, despite General von Moltke's declaration two months earlier that they were ready—well, saying it in theory is one thing. Now that it's really happening, signs begin to appear that even the Germans aren't so sure they want this war after all. The Kaiser is delighted: Serbia's reply puts everything right. No war.

Unfortunately, Austria declares war on Serbia that very day. The Germans had done nothing but urge Austria forward. And once again, we have to understand the people of that time. Austria is swept by popular mobilization. People are convinced: let's go and punish these terrorists who threaten the world.

I'll give you an example: Sigmund Freud. Freud is Austrian, lives in Vienna, and is fifty-eight years old, already a mature man who's spent his entire life in the Austrian Empire. When Austria declares war on Serbia, Freud says—or writes, I don't remember, but anyway—that for the first time in thirty years he feels truly Austrian. For the first time, he thinks this empire might have a future. And then there is this direct quotation: “My entire libido belongs to Austria-Hungary.” It is July 28.

On July 29, Russia begins mobilization preparations—exactly what the Germans have warned it not to do, because otherwise it means war. Russia mobilizes because it's afraid, and the Russians don't want war either. We discover that through another story beginning on July 29, a very curious story that's typical of this strange age. The Europe of these years is suspended between modernity—liberal parliamentary democracy—and the archaism of dynastic empires where emperors and kings still wield real power.

On July 29, the telegrams begin: Nicky to Willy, Willy to Nicky. The first comes from Tsar Nicholas of Russia, who sends a telegram to Kaiser Wilhelm just as Russia is preparing to mobilize against him. Nicholas writes in English. This too is significant. It shows us what Britain has become. Fifty years earlier, if a tsar had written to a king of Prussia, he'd have used French. Now they speak and write to one another in English.

To understand the situation, remember that Tsar Nicholas, Emperor Wilhelm, and King George of England are dynastic cousins. Wilhelm and George are first cousins through Queen Victoria—the nineteenth-century Queen Victoria, who died in nineteen hundred and something—while Nicholas and George are first cousins through the Danish royal family, and Nicholas isn't Queen Victoria's grandson. They address one another informally and use nicknames. [N-024]

So on July 29, the Russian tsar sends the Kaiser a telegram: I'm glad you're back from your cruise. The moment is serious, and I need your help. “An ignoble war has been declared to a weak country. I beg you in the name of our old friendship to do what you can to stop your allies from going too far.” Signed, Nicky.

The Kaiser replies in English that same day: but look at it from the other side. Serbia is a terrorist state. Austria has the right, and so on. Come on, you Russians have to understand. You take a step back. Signed, Willy.

They write to each other for four days, exchanging a dozen telegrams from Nicky to Willy and Willy to Nicky, until August 1, when Willy declares war on Nicky. But let's not get ahead of ourselves. We still have those four days to deal with.

July 29: the Germans are furious with these stupid Russians who haven't understood that mobilizing will cause the war. General von Moltke says Russia has sided with a criminal country, a den of terrorists. “Then the mutual destruction of the civilized states of Europe can begin.” A war—this is still General von Moltke—that will annihilate the civilization of almost all Europe for decades.

And yet the war must still be fought, because no one wants to back down. Here we see, how can I put it, an almost schizophrenic trait in this ruling class. They understand perfectly well that the war will be a catastrophe, but at the same time they can't back down and lose face. Let the others step back. Not us. At this point, another split opens in Germany between the military and the politicians.

## 7. German war plans, Belgian neutrality, and Britain's warning

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00760–U-00859 -->

The military say war is inevitable, so let's fight it now. Let's attack. Give us the order to mobilize. The politicians say, wait a moment. From their point of view, it's essential to show that Germany isn't to blame for the war: it's all the fault of the Russians and the French, not Germany. So why do the German politicians care so much about that? There are many reasons. Perhaps it's partly about saving face, pride, a sense of honor. But they also care about German public opinion. Public opinion matters in Germany too. Germany has a huge Socialist Party. What will the socialists say? If people think Germany has been attacked, there'll be no problem—and that's exactly what happens. Germany delays mobilization, and the German people go to war convinced that innocent Germany has been attacked and must be defended.

And then they have to prevent England from entering the war. If England sees Germany doing everything it can to avoid war, perhaps it'll stay out. After all, by now it's clear that the English want to stay out if they possibly can. But there's another problem that makes it very difficult for the Germans to keep England out. It's the last problem, so to speak, that I'll pull out of my hat: Belgium. You might ask, what does Belgium have to do with it? Quite a lot. Belgium is a small neutral country whose neutrality is guaranteed by all the great powers, including Germany and England. And the English care a great deal about this. In their view of Europe, Belgium, with Antwerp and its other ports facing the Channel, is a vital link to the continent. We English might quarrel with France, but that doesn't matter. As long as Belgium is there, we have a link to Europe. So the English don't care at all about Serbia, but they certainly would care about Belgium.

And why on earth should the Germans be worried about Belgium? Because of their plans. The German general staff has a perfect plan for fighting France and Russia and winning in a matter of weeks. Everything is calculated. The Russians are slow, so we send the whole army—the famous 11,000 trains—against France. We invade and finish the war in a few weeks. But to invade France and outflank the French army—forgive me for getting into these strategic questions—the German military have decided they need to go through Belgium. It's the famous Schlieffen Plan, which some of you may remember, refined over years and years. The military tell the politicians: if you want us to win the war for you, this is the plan. We have to go through Belgium. It's up to you to work out how to handle it.

On July 29, the Kaiser and the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, realize that if they're gambling on England staying out of the war, they need to work out what to do about Belgium. After all, the Germans are going to invade it. That's already been decided. So the Kaiser and the chancellor make a crucial decision, one that everyone will later judge the stupidest decision the Germans make in those days. They write to the English government in London and offer guarantees. First: if war breaks out against France, the English can rest easy. Germany has no territorial ambitions. We don't want to take any pieces of France, so we don't want to upset the European balance. Second: the Germans assure the English government that if they're forced to invade Belgium during the war, they'll evacuate it when the war is over. And if the Belgians behave themselves and don't prove hostile, they'll even be paid damages. Finally, the Germans write to London: after all, think about this carefully. We Germans and you English could still come to an agreement. Together we're tremendously strong. We could divide up the world. Why do you care about being friends with France? Why do you care about Belgium? Let's divide the world between us.

In London, they see this message arrive and are stunned. A senior Foreign Office official comments: “The only comment that need be made on these astounding proposals is that they reflect discredit on the statesman who makes them.” And on top of that, the English say, they're telling us they're going to invade Belgium. They've actually put it in writing. Grey calls the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, and tells him: look, if war breaks out, we can't stay out. At that point, we'll enter the war too, alongside our friends. Grey also says something that everyone is repeating almost obsessively in those days: “if war did break out, it would be the greatest catastrophe that the world had ever seen” But England can't make this agreement with Germany. Betraying its friends would be an indelible disgrace, so think very carefully in Berlin. And three: as the English see it, the responsibility for everything lies in Berlin. Berlin is where they must take a step back.

At this news from London, the German reaction is uncontrollable. The Kaiser writes on the message: “Aha! The vile deceiver! Vile blackguard! I will never make a naval agreement with such scoundrels! The vile shopkeeper rabble tried to deceive us with dinners and speeches. But he avoids saying the word and threatens us instead! Vile blackguard! England alone bears responsibility for war and peace, no longer we! That must also be made clear publicly.” And four: as the Germans see it, responsibility lies with the English. That same day, July 29, the German ambassador in Brussels, von Below-Saleske, receives a sealed packet from Berlin with orders not to open it until further instructions arrive by telegram. Another useless detail—we're nearly there, but I'll inflict it on you anyway. Von Below-Saleske is a career diplomat who likes to joke that he brings bad luck. He was in China, and the Boxer Rebellion broke out. He was in Turkey, and the Young Turk Revolution broke out. But now, he says, I can relax. I'm in Belgium. Nothing will ever happen in Brussels. Let's pick up the pace, or I'll keep you here all night.

## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

On the night of July 29–30, no one in Berlin goes to bed. The news from London has thrown them into a panic. It's becoming clear that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, calls Vienna and says: have you understood nothing? Do you really want to go to war with Serbia? Take it easy. Slow down. Germany has no intention of being dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine the faces in Vienna. Until the day before, the Germans had been telling them to press ahead. So, and five: for the Germans, responsibility now lies in Vienna.

Meanwhile, we've reached July 30. Russia finishes its preparations—and the preparations alone take several days—and orders general mobilization. In Berlin, the military go to the government and say, let's mobilize too, immediately. The government says, let's wait one more day. Perhaps they'll manage to come up with something in Vienna. On this day, July 30, the crisis between the military and the politicians in Berlin comes close—I won't say to a coup d'état, but yes, to high treason. That afternoon, an evening paper comes out—Germany has evening papers in those days—announcing the mobilization order. It isn't true. The paper is seized. Then it emerges that the general staff has leaked the false report to present the government with a fait accompli. General von Moltke telephones his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria hasn't mobilized yet, incidentally, because Austria does things so slowly that it still hasn't mobilized. Moltke tells Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize, we'll have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government to mobilize.

The government is still holding firm, but it's in a state of total panic. That day, another characteristic feature of the communications from these very last days emerges: everyone feels that something is happening which no one wants and which can no longer be stopped. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.” There you have it: politics admitting defeat. The stone is rolling, and we no longer know what to do. That day, the New York Stock Exchange collapses. Because of the time difference, the news from New York arrives on the morning of July 31. The London Stock Exchange collapses. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.

## 9. 31 July–1 August: ultimatums, war, and an impossible reversal

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00891–U-00959 -->

Germany still doesn't mobilize, but it sends Russia an ultimatum: you've begun mobilizing. Stop. If you stop by midnight, fine. Otherwise, it's war. In London on July 31, the foreign secretary, Grey, is now convinced that war is inevitable and that England must enter it. But he's in the minority in the government. The government keeps saying, this has nothing to do with us. Grey does what he can. He sends one telegram to Paris and another to Berlin, asking for a guarantee that Belgian neutrality will be respected. The French reply within an hour. The Germans don't reply.

On August 1, Grey speaks to the government. He calls Germany an aggressor as dangerous as Napoleon and makes it clear that he'll resign if England doesn't enter the war. But most of the ministers are against it. Most of them threaten to resign if Britain goes to war. Outside the door, the French ambassador, Cambon, is waiting, as he's been doing throughout these days. Do you understand the job of the French ambassador in London at this moment? War is about to break out, and he has to make sure the English enter it alongside us. We're such good friends. Grey has to go out and tell Cambon there's no guarantee. The government is offering nothing. Cambon goes into the office of a friend at the English Foreign Office. The friend describes him as white as a sheet, on the verge of tears. The French ambassador collapses into a chair and says, “They're going to abandon us; they're going to let us down.”

Meanwhile, though, everything is hurtling forward. On August 1, Germany declares war on Russia. It's the first declaration of war between two great powers. Why does Germany declare war? Well, because that's the procedure. We've sent the ultimatum. We've told the Russians to stop by midnight. They haven't stopped, so we have to declare war. The ministers had met at Bethmann Hollweg's house the previous evening to draft the declaration of war on Russia. The navy minister, Tirpitz, says: “On 1 August I learned in the Federal Council session that we had followed the ultimatum with a declaration of war on Russia. […] We were to assume the odium of the attacking party because of the jurists of the Foreign Office. […] Presumably we did it out of formal legal conscientiousness.” That's the procedure: once you've sent an ultimatum, you have to follow it with a declaration of war. That same day, August 1, at four in the afternoon, Germany declares war on Russia and orders general mobilization. To the Germans, they really are the same thing.

But there's still room for a farce, a grotesque misunderstanding. At five in the afternoon, a telegram arrives in Berlin from the ambassador in London, Prince Lichnowsky. Lichnowsky says: “Sir E. Grey just asked me by telephone whether I believed that I could declare that we would not attack France in a war between Germany and Russia in case France should remain neutral. I declared that I thought I could give that assurance.” The telegram causes pandemonium in Berlin. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg say, fantastic! We no longer have to fight both France and Russia, only Russia. If England and France stay out, we're fine. We just need to change the mobilization orders and send everything against Russia.

They summon General von Moltke, who commands the army and is organizing the great mobilization. Eleven thousand trains have already started moving. At that very moment, telegrams carrying the secret orders are arriving at every station and barracks in Germany. Everything is in motion. They call von Moltke to the imperial palace. I have to tell you something about him. He isn't a great general, and he'll soon be removed from command. He's there mainly because he's the nephew of a great man. In the nineteenth century there had been another von Moltke, a famous Prussian general who won all his wars. The younger von Moltke is there mainly because he's that great man's nephew. But he's a man with rather fragile nerves.

He arrives at the imperial palace, and they tell him: look, a telegram from London. England and France will probably stay out. Fantastic! Change the mobilization orders. Send everything against Russia. Von Moltke nearly has a fit. He starts crying and says it can't be done. Everything is already moving: 11,000 trains, millions of people, everything calculated to the second. It can't be done. They press him. Moltke, who, I repeat, is on the verge of collapse—that evening he'll go home and have a heart attack—stands firm. It can't be done. The Kaiser tells him: “Your uncle would have given me a different answer!” Moltke is a broken man. But the mobilization can't be changed, so everything continues according to plan. That evening, the Germans invade Luxembourg. It's the first step toward the invasion of Belgium.

## 10. 2–4 August: Belgium brings Britain into the war

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01015 -->

Before invading Belgium, though, the Germans have to observe the formalities. They have to deliver an ultimatum. On August 2, von Below-Saleske in Brussels receives a telegram: open the sealed packet and deliver the ultimatum to Belgium, pretending it has only just arrived. He's had it for several days. But what does the ultimatum say to the Belgians? It's a heap of lies. Here we come to another aspect—I'm nearly finished, but this is the last slightly grotesque feature of the situation. You've seen how complicated, and at the same time terrifyingly simple, the road to war is. Despite all that, the Germans remain convinced that to send someone an ultimatum or declare war on them, you need pretexts. You need excuses. So they tell the Belgians: “The German Government has received reliable information according to which French forces intend to march on the Meuse by way of Givet and Namur.” We Germans, you understand, have to enter Belgium too for our own security. But we wouldn't want the Belgians to interpret that as a hostile act. If Belgium remains neutral and behaves itself, we'll evacuate it at the end of the war. We promise. The Belgians decide to resist.

That evening in Berlin, at Bethmann Hollweg's house, they prepare the declaration of war on France. Admiral von Tirpitz asks, but why do we have to declare war on France too? Let's wait for them to declare war on us. They explain that the rules require it. The declaration of war on France is also a heap of lies. French aircraft have flown over Germany. They've dropped bombs. They've killed civilians. France has therefore gone to war all by itself, and so on. Evidently, even when you're practicing power politics, you still need something to cling to for public opinion. It reminds me, perhaps, of Colin Powell at the UN with his vial of Saddam Hussein's biological weapons. But anyway, never mind.

That evening, August 2, Grey learns of the German ultimatum to Belgium and finally gets the English government to order mobilization. Several ministers resign. That same evening, an American diplomat in Brussels writes in his diary: “Nobody seems to remember that a few days ago Serbia was playing a star rôle in this affair. She seems to have faded away behind the scenes.”

August 3: now we really are at the end. Germany declares war on France. Grey secures authorization from the House of Commons to send the Germans an ultimatum. Finally, a parliament comes into play and does something in this crisis, which until now has been managed entirely by governments and sovereigns. If the Germans invade Belgium, it's war. August 4: the Germans invade Belgium. The English ambassador arrives to deliver the ultimatum to Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, unaware that Germany has begun invading Belgium that morning.

And the whole affair ends—my story ends too—with two famous phrases, one very famous and the other a little less so. The very famous one comes from the German chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg. When the English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum, he makes a scene and says: “just for a word—'neutrality,' a word which in war time had so often been disregarded—just for a scrap of paper Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation” The phrase passes into history as the emblem, let's say, of a kind of politics that thinks it can sign treaties and then, if it wants, simply pretend they don't exist. So much for treaties.

The other historic phrase is a little less historic, but anyway, it comes from the Kaiser. Remember, he's the cousin of the king of England and of the tsar. They all call one another by nicknames and address one another informally. And now it's clear that Germany is at war with Russia, with France, and with England too. They're all Queen Victoria's little grandsons, remember? The Kaiser declares: who would have thought that Georgie and Nicky were about to betray me? If my grandmother had been alive, she would never have allowed it.
