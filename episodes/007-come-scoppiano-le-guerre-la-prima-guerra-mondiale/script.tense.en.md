# Come scoppiano le guerre: La prima guerra mondiale — faithful English translation

## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we began thinking about what I might come and talk about here in Sarzana, there was already something of the atmosphere that, after all, is still with us now: there was already news from Ukraine, there was news from Iraq. Pope Francis had not yet said, let us say, that Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction..., whereas he did say it a short while ago. And so, clearly, it is increasingly timely to ask how—there, do I have to repeat the first part? No, it was only a way of introducing the subject anyway; this is fine too. How do wars break out? In fact, I think what I am going to tell you is also fairly reassuring, in the sense that it shows that wars break out in situations—the great world wars break out in situations—quite different from the one we are experiencing now. Today we are experiencing a disastrous international situation, full of suffering, full of uncertainties, but still quite different from the scenarios that were taking shape in Europe, for example in the Europe of 1914, which is where we begin this evening.

How does the First World War break out? Well, deep down we all know: it begins with an assassination, the Sarajevo assassination. On 28 June 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists kills Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to say about the outbreak of the First World War is that it arises from an act of terrorism. And terrorism, then as now, provokes visceral reactions in public opinion and therefore in governments as well. To us today, in considering an act of terrorism, it may seem that killing an archduke is perhaps, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. Instead, one thing we must understand very clearly if we are to grasp what the people of 1914 are like is that, for them—at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who is heir to the imperial throne is far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers would have been, if they could ever have imagined such a thing.

Just think: the Austro-Hungarian Empire is a great power. It is still a great power—weakened, in crisis—but it is one of the few great powers in the world. Emperor Franz Joseph rules the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Emperor Franz Joseph is eighty-three years old, which means he is going to die very soon. No one knows when, but he is certain to die very soon. Franz Joseph has reigned over the Austrian Empire since time immemorial; he is nearing sixty-six years on the throne. He will die very soon, everyone knows that. In fact, he will die on 21 November 1916, a little over two years later; we know that. [N-002] And when Franz Joseph dies, Archduke Franz Ferdinand will become the sovereign of one of the world's great powers for the rest of his life. You understand that killing him changes the situation somewhat. It is not like killing a president of the United States, because another one is chosen four years later anyway. Here they are killing someone who would have helped govern the world for the rest of his life. So the assassination is extremely serious: an act of terrorism of enormous gravity, with complicity abroad.

Here we must remember how—I will spend the whole evening telling you, “We must remember this,” and you will forgive me—the way a war breaks out is not so simple. There is a whole series of connections. The first, the first point to bring into focus: Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia is then, just as it has been in our own time, a sensitive place. Bosnia is also then a place with an ethnic mixture, with hostile ethnic groups. Bosnia has been occupied by Austria-Hungary for some years, and some of its inhabitants are happy about it. The Croat population is happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslim population is very happy to belong to the Austrian Empire. Indeed, Bosnian Muslim soldiers will fight faithfully for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments wearing fezzes.

But in Serbia—in Serbia, in Bosnia—there is a proportion of the Serbian population that is not at all happy to belong to Austria. Because right next door is Serbia, the Kingdom of Serbia: independent Serbia, small but bellicose, a battle-hardened state with military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs suffer under Austrian imperial rule. And so there is discontent, and there is terrorism. And the Austrians are certain that this terrorism is backed, supported, and organized in Serbia; that this assassination, in which the Archduke and his wife have been killed, has been organized in Serbia with complicity within the Serbian government and the Serbian army. The Austrians are certain of it from the very first day. Among other things, it is true: the investigations will bring it to light; there is no doubt about it. There is such complicity in Serbia.

And so Austria is determined to punish Serbia. And here you really must make the effort to think of them not as Austria and Serbia, forgotten states as they are then—who remembers anymore? No. Here there is a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad, and there is the country that has suffered the terrorist attack, which is determined to punish the rogue state. In Austria there is a wave of mass indignation. Crowds are in the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” In German it even has assonance: *Serbien muss sterben*.

## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there is a problem: Serbia has a great friend, a great protector—Russia. The Russia of Tsar Nicholas II. The friendship between Serbia and Russia is something—it is an obvious fact of European geography; it is true even today. Russia is a Slavic, Orthodox power; the Serbs are Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let us broaden the discussion for a moment. Serbia has a great friend, Russia. Everyone in that world of 1914 tries to have friends and allies. The weight of alliances is a fundamental factor in understanding how the world slides into world war, because that is not a world of blocs. Well, ours is no longer one either by now—only the American bloc remains—but in any case we all remember when our world was divided into two blocs and nothing happened outside those two blocs. The world of 1914 is not like that. It is a world of great and small powers. Which are the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and then Italy is also usually included as a courtesy, the last of the great powers with a little effort. And then there are the small powers, but they too are bellicose, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They are not divided into blocs; each looks after itself.

This is the age in which nationalism is at its height. Every country is convinced that it has a duty to look after itself. It is what an Italian politician, Prime Minister Salandra, in those very days calls “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But if one has to look after oneself, one also has to try to have allies, because alone one comes to a bad end. So each country seeks alliances for its own security. Serbia is allied with Russia.

The Austrians think that, well, the world will understand: Serbia is clearly a rogue state that has organized terrorism; it has to be punished for everyone's security. But they begin to wonder whether, if Austria attacks Serbia, the Russians might intervene. Yet Austria too has a powerful friend, more powerful than itself, because Austria is a great empire in decline—all things considered, almost everyone suspects it is in decline—whereas Austria has an immensely powerful friend that is becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. The Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm—you remember Wilhelm with the great handlebar moustache and the spiked helmet? But beyond the Kaiser, who is a somewhat comical figure, Germany is a very great power. It has the strongest army in the world and is building the strongest industry in the world. In fact, Germany has already become the greatest industrial power; it has already overtaken Britain.

Austria is allied with Germany in an alliance that binds the two countries to help one another if either is attacked. To tell the truth, the alliance also includes Italy. It is called the Triple Alliance for a reason. But during those days of crisis in 1914 it is perfectly clear that no one has any faith in Italy. The Austrians do not believe that, if they go to war, Italy will come to their aid. They rule it out, excluding the whole idea from the outset, and in fact they are right. In the name of sacred egoism, Italy will decide to stay out for the time being.

But there is Germany. Austria has enough good sense to know that, before doing anything against Serbia, it has to ask what the Germans think. Franz Ferdinand's funeral takes place on 3 July. The Emperor, the old Emperor Franz Joseph, has signed his appeal to Kaiser Wilhelm on 2 July; it is delivered to Wilhelm on 5 July with the accompanying memorandum. [N-005] Now, this letter from the Emperor of Austria is very interesting. It is not precise or lucid; it does not say, “This is what we want to do.” It is a letter more or less along these lines. Franz Joseph says to Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” This is a letter that makes me think of the reasons with which the Western powers went into Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. In other words, something must be done. There is no lucid reasoning about the consequences, no consideration of what today is called an exit strategy. “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agrees. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reaches him, Kaiser Wilhelm notes on the telegram that “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.”. Consequently, on 5 July Kaiser Wilhelm replies to his Austrian counterpart, telling him: “Go ahead, do not worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser also adds: “Really, do not worry. The Russians will not have the courage to interfere. We are certain Russia will stay out.” That is the first gamble. This evening's story is a story of one gamble after another, all of them wrong. It is as though—as we shall see—wars break out not because someone wants them. No one ever wants war, at least not world war, general war; no one ever wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

I want to pause for another moment over this assurance Kaiser Wilhelm sends to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we are with you, do not worry.” It is a reassurance that historians later call the blank cheque. How is this blank cheque given? Germany is already a parliamentary country; it has political parties, a parliament, free elections. The Kaiser consults no one. Before consulting anyone at all, he himself writes to his counterpart, the Emperor of Austria, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then the Kaiser does consult someone. Whom? Parliament? Not a chance. He consults the Chancellor—the prime minister, that is. He consults the commander of the army, General von Moltke, and General von Moltke tells him, “If war breaks out, we are ready.” And then—this is interesting—whom else does Kaiser Wilhelm consult? He consults Alfred Krupp, the great arms industrialist. Krupp is the great—the largest arms factory in the world. The Kaiser feels the need to consult Alfred Krupp. If war should ever break out, are the Krupp works ready? And Alfred Krupp reassures him: the Krupp works are ready.

But they are certain that war will not break out. Indeed, after sending the blank cheque to Vienna, the Kaiser boards his yacht and goes on a cruise in the Baltic. It is July, people want to take their holidays, and the Kaiser goes on holiday too. This German gamble—that yes, there is a risk of war, but you will see that war will not come—is a crucial aspect of the argument we must discuss this evening, so I must dwell on it a little longer.

The gamble is even greater than it appears because of the question of alliances, since Russia is not the only party involved. Russia is Serbia's great friend, but Russia has an alliance with France. Politically speaking it is an unnatural alliance, if you think about it: France, the secular, anticlerical, radical republic, and the Tsar's empire. The Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. It is an unnatural alliance, and yet France and Russia have been allies for twenty years. Why? One need only look at the map—the map of that time. Today Germany seems to us a great power. The Germany of that time is much larger. Poland does not exist; Czechoslovakia does not exist; the Baltic states do not exist. There is only Germany and then, at a certain point, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany stands in the middle, sharing borders with France and Russia. And so it is clear that France and Russia have every interest in being allies.

And when the Germans gamble that there will be no war—that there will be no world war; the small war between Austria and Serbia, yes, certainly, that is perfectly fine, it is necessary—but when they gamble that there will be no war, the Germans are gambling that there will be no war against Russia and France, because that is the risk. Why do they take that risk?

## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second part of this talk, of this chat, will be a kind of countdown through the final days, but first we have to sketch a broader picture, get you inside the heads of the people governing the world at that moment. What is the world like, what is Europe like at that moment? The Europe of 1914 is experiencing a period of great prosperity, of great economic expansion. After the decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy has started moving again; everyone is growing. There is even a kind of globalization avant la lettre. Well, today it is very easy to find globalizations in the past; I have seen every kind of them. Even the Roman Empire was a globalization. But in any case this one in 1914 has the features of globalization: the economies are interdependent, the stock exchanges are interdependent, the currencies are linked, anchored to gold. The Italian lira is worth exactly as much as the French franc. You can travel throughout Europe without a passport, except in Russia. You need a passport to enter Russia. But otherwise this Europe is so interdependent that it seems incredible that a war could break out among these powers. And yet at the same time all these countries, Italy included, are armed to the teeth. They all have military spending at a level comparable to American military spending today. They spend enormous sums on the army, on the navy, and why do they do it? They do it because they are afraid of one another. An important aspect in understanding the mentality of the ruling classes of that era is that they are all steeped in a kind of Darwinian ideology, but a very simplified one, right? The survival of the fittest, the struggle for survival, the survival of the strongest. They are convinced that states, too, are struggling with one another in this way. The strongest will triumph, the weakest will be eaten. And because they are convinced of this, everyone wants to be strong and they all arm themselves in order to survive. No one imagines that they themselves will be the aggressor in the future; it is always the others who are the aggressors. We are threatened, we must be strong, we must have a great army, and since we are spending an enormous amount of money on a great army, well, we have to expect that perhaps sooner or later we will use it. It cannot be ruled out; in fact it is quite likely, some say. Those years—it is striking, because in those years books come out that talk quite calmly about the next war. It is a foreseeable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911 a book comes out in Germany by a German general, General von Bernhardi, entitled *Germany and the Next War*. Three years remain before the First World War. In this book there are chapters entitled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I have given a German example because German generals are particularly inclined to state these arguments explicitly, but in reality similar arguments are made in every country. And of course not everyone reasons that way. There are also lucid people who say: look, with the interdependent economies we have, a war among the great powers would be a catastrophe. Let me mention another book that comes out in those years. These are the books that prime ministers and army commanders read; they keep them on their bedside tables. In 1910 a book comes out by an English writer, Norman Angell, entitled *The Great Illusion*. Do you remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? Well, it later copies its title from this book, which is very famous in its day. What is the great illusion? The author says that the great illusion is precisely that of our great powers, which believe that having a great army and preparing to win the next war is a guarantee. It is not. We will all lose the next war, this author says. The next war will destroy even the victors; it is an illusion to believe that strength will save us. Now, books like this circulate, politicians read them, and in the Europe of 1914 various politicians have become fairly convinced that a war among the great powers would indeed be a catastrophe. But the paradox is that these politicians are at the head of countries armed to the teeth. The scenario of the next war is so foreseeable that at a certain point this happens. Let me tell you this detail. In London, at a certain point, the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, is speaking with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. At a certain point the British foreign secretary Grey says to the German ambassador—listen to this conversation—“But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.”. That is the description of what I am telling you about tonight, the Sarajevo crisis, the crisis of the summer of 1914, except that this conversation takes place two years earlier. In December 1912 Grey says these things to the German ambassador. Those are the scenarios in which a war can break out, and there are only a few places. The Balkans are a typical place, with all the unrest there. Now, starting from the fact that the scenario of the next war is foreseeable, a split nevertheless emerges between the politicians and the military, because the politicians say: look, we must do everything we can to avoid this scenario; it would be a catastrophe if it came to pass. But the military have a different job. The military’s job is to say: and what do we do if it happens anyway? If it happens anyway, we must be ready. So in those years of great economic growth the military press: let us use all this money, let us use it to become stronger. And the politicians give in. The years preceding 1914 are years of an arms race in which every country strengthens its army, not because it wants to attack anyone but because it is concerned about its security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passes a law under which military service, conscription, is increased from two to three years. Imagine a world in which conscription lasts three years. What do the Germans say when the French parliament passes the law increasing military service? The Germans say: the French want to make war, they are threatening us. It is a very understandable reaction that political scientists have studied. It is a reaction called the security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors become frightened, they feel less safe than before, so they arm themselves too, and so on and so on. The penultimate thing before we reach—return to—Austria, which is deciding what to do against Serbia. The penultimate thing needed to make the picture of that moment clear. The military do not merely ask for more weapons; they also do something else. Their job is to say: if war really happens, we must be ready. Being ready means making plans. In 1914 the great powers have not fought one another for a very long time. Almost fifty years have passed since the Crimean War, since the Franco-Prussian War. In those fifty years the general staffs have done nothing but prepare plans: if war breaks out—that particular war, against that particular enemy—what do we do? Plans are ready for every possible war, extremely detailed, extremely precise. Every year they reopen them and refine them, adjust a detail. They have perfected them for fifty years. Plans are ready for everything. An anecdote about this: in November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium is on a state visit to Berlin. There is a reception, and during the reception Kaiser Wilhelm points out a general to the king of Belgium and tells him, The Emperor introduces General von Kluck to him, saying with a distinctly deliberate emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.”. The plans are so complete that everything has already been decided, even who will command each individual army, and so on. Everything is ready, should war come. Finally, one last thing before returning to Vienna, where they are discussing exactly what to demand of the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. One last thing: this arms race of those years has another characteristic that makes it very dangerous, one that makes it possible to predict how the balance of power will evolve. Armaments are slow things. You lay down a battleship and it will be ready in five years; you increase conscription by one year and two years pass before the effects are felt, and so on and so on. So everyone pays close attention: when will we be at our strongest? And when will they? Russia, for example, is militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it is making enormous investments. In a few years it will be much stronger. So in this climate, you understand, the military do their job. Their job is to say: if there is a war, we must be ready. And by the way, if there is a war, when would it suit us for it to happen? Would it suit us for it to happen soon, or would it suit us for it to happen later? All military officers make these calculations. And then every so often something changes. In April 1914 it becomes known that Russia and Britain are negotiating a naval agreement. I have hardly mentioned Britain so far; it has no alliances. I will come back to Britain in a moment, but it is now beginning talks with Russia. You understand what effect this has in Berlin? In Berlin they say: we already have Russia and France allied with each other against us in the next war, when it comes. If Britain were to ally with them too, our position would become worse. These talks between the Russians and the British begin in April. In May 1914 the commander of the German army, von Moltke, states in private, not in public: from this moment on, every day of delay can only diminish our probability of success. A few more weeks: on 1 June 1914 von Moltke declares, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.”. Four weeks remain before the Sarajevo assassination.

## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Does von Moltke organize the Sarajevo assassination? No, obviously. It is the catalyst that brings out all these things that have been simmering there for years. Let us return to Vienna, where they are preparing the ultimatum to send to Serbia. They will send an extremely harsh ultimatum because the Austrians do not want the Serbs to accept it. The Austrians want war and they have a blank cheque from Germany. It will be an extremely severe ultimatum. The Germans press, the Germans press Vienna, saying: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli; the sooner you act the better; the Russians will not dare to intervene. But Austria is a country where things are done calmly. It is a country where things are done slowly. There are two governments, the Austrian one and the Hungarian one; they have to reach agreement. And then the Austrian army is a slow, backward army; it takes a long time to prepare. What is more, consider something no one today would think of: it is July, harvest time. The soldiers are all at home on leave—Austria is an agricultural country—to harvest the grain. It takes time. The Austrians are in no hurry; in Berlin they are champing at the bit: we are always being dragged along behind this backward country. Finally, on 23 July, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum is ready. It is delivered to the Serbian government. It is an extremely harsh ultimatum. The Austrians say to Serbia: the Serbian government has done nothing to prevent the proliferation of terrorism; Serbian officers and officials are involved in the Sarajevo assassination. Therefore we Austrians want the Serbian government to publish a declaration in which it dissociates itself from every form of hostility toward Austria. The Serbian government must close all the newspapers and dissolve all the associations that sow hatred against Austria. The Serbian government must dismiss all public officials and officers who are in some way involved with forms of hostility toward Austria. It must have these people arrested—the Austrians present the list—and it must open investigations into terrorism on Serbian territory in which Austrian investigators will take part. On 24 July the capitals of the whole world awake to the news of this ultimatum, and the ultimatum is received very badly. In St. Petersburg the Russian foreign minister summons the Austrian ambassador and tells him: but do you want to make war on Serbia? Are the Germans inciting you? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That is number one: for the Russians the responsibility lies with Vienna and the Austrians. We will see a whole series of these phrases, “you are the ones assuming responsibility.” This is only the first. In London too, the foreign secretary Grey is aghast. He calls the German ambassador Lichnowsky and tells him: I have never seen one state send another state a document like this. It is impossible for the Serbs to accept it. Here there is a small period detail, right, to give a sense of the era. Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador, says: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!” Now we are in London, then, in this exchange between Grey and Lichnowsky. What is happening in London? In London the German gamble does indeed appear to be holding for the moment. British public opinion does not give the slightest damn about Serbia. Britain has alliances with no one. It has a friendship with France that has lasted for years, this must be said. British and French military officers are accustomed to consulting each other; they have even prepared plans together, they have become fairly friendly. But there are no obligations, there are no treaties. And Britain is an isolationist country at that moment. Britain is a country that dominates the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock exchange. It does not give a damn about minor continental disputes, and especially about the Balkans. British newspapers write in those days: why should we let ourselves be dragged into someone else’s war? The *Manchester Guardian* writes on 30 July, after the ultimatum: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017] The proof that until that moment Britain is truly outside, outside these thoughts, is in the minutes of the British government, of cabinet meetings. From the day of the Sarajevo assassination, 28 June, to the day when the whole world is thrown into an uproar because the Austrians have delivered the ultimatum to Serbia, 24 July, for almost a month the British government never discusses foreign policy. They have problems of their own. They have Ireland, which is not yet independent, where there is terrorism, there is everything, a revolution about to break out. Foreign policy does not exist for the British. There are only some people in Britain who reason differently. There are some officials in the Foreign Office; we know from the notes they exchange in those days. There are some officials in the Foreign Office who are instead afraid of what is happening. And again, the security paradox. We are fairly safe; Germany is becoming very strong. The Germans say they are doing it only because they are afraid of us, but if they become too strong, then—some senior Foreign Office official in London says in those days—we must stand with the French and the Russians, because this struggle is not for the possession of Serbia but between a Germany that aspires to political dictatorship in Europe and the powers that want to remain free. And then there are people in Britain who think that way, and they are the military officers. By then the military are accustomed to considering themselves friends of the French, to doing things together with them. They know it will be difficult for Britain to enter the war alongside France because public opinion does not want it, and for years they have been brooding over this matter. Here too it is striking to see how, for years and years, they have thought and discussed and reasoned among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do? There is a revealing anecdote, from 1910. An English general who is very friendly with the French, Wilson, is talking with a French general, Foch, and Wilson says to him: well, if war breaks out, assuming that we come to help you—I hope so, but who knows, Wilson says—what would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could really help France? We are four years earlier, in 1910. What would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could help France? And Foch replies: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is that British public opinion should be mobilized for a cause. It is almost midnight; let us begin the countdown. On 25 July the Serbian reply to the ultimatum arrives.

## 5. Serbia's reply, Churchill's fleet, and mobilization logic

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00588–U-00679 -->

The Serbs accept almost all the conditions. In the meantime, however, Serbia appeals to the tsar to make sure that the Russian tsar will support them if they are attacked. The tsar replies to the Serbs: you can count on us. That day the tsar makes a symbolic decision—symbolic things matter in politics too. All the officer cadets in their final year who are in Russia are commissioned as officers on the same day, at the same hour: the hour when the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia expires. It could not be any clearer than that.

July 26: Austria rejects the Serbian reply. Having rejected the reply means it will go to war. That day something happens in London. It is a detail—I have filled this lecture with details, which means I will run late and you will have to be patient; I did not have the courage to cut many things. This detail seems to me, it seems to me, in its own way another curious detail. On the day Austria rejects the Serbian reply, the English navy minister, the First Lord of the Admiralty, makes a decision on his own initiative. The English war fleet is engaged in major maneuvers; it is all gathered together with full crews. The major maneuvers end tomorrow, after which the squadrons will each return to their own port and the sailors will go on leave. The First Lord of the Admiralty, on his own initiative, gives the order: tomorrow the fleet is not to disperse, it is to remain all together and move to the naval base at Scapa Flow in the Orkneys, where it will be safe.

I am telling you this also because the First Lord of the Admiralty in 1914 is Winston Churchill, whom we are accustomed to associating more with the Second World War, and he was still alive when I was a child, and many of you, well, we remember him. Yet few people remember that Churchill is precocious; he is a precocious young man and is already navy minister in 1914. He is the one who decides that the war fleet will not disperse but remain ready and, indeed, take shelter in the naval base. The next day the German stock market collapses. The news from London is enough to raise doubts. Perhaps England will not stay out after all.

There is still one thing I have to explain to you, to explain what constraints are acting upon these soldiers and these politicians. It is the last thing, I hope, that interrupts our countdown. At this point war is certain—at least between Austria and Serbia it is certain; then we will see. To wage war, the first thing these countries must do is order mobilization. What does that mean? They have conscript armies, which means that the country is full of young men and men who did their military service in previous years and who can always be called back if war breaks out—or rather, who are certain to be called back. If war breaks out, the army must immediately be expanded, doubled, tripled, by calling up everyone who is at home. This is mobilization.

It is an extremely complicated thing because, as you understand, it means calling up millions of men, putting them in uniform, arming them, organizing the units, and putting them on trains that will take them to the frontier. All of it according to plans, themselves calculated down to the smallest detail. German mobilization, calculated to the very minute and second, calls for the simultaneous movement of 11,000 trains. The Germans are accustomed to doing these things well; everything is scheduled to the minute. Mobilization, as you understand, is an enormously consequential decision. Among other things it costs a tremendous amount of money, so no one does it unless the situation has really become very serious.

But there are differences from one country to another. There are countries that are slow to mobilize. One at random: Russia. Russia, which is immense, has few railways. Russia takes months to mobilize its army. So if the situation becomes serious, Russia has to mobilize somewhat quickly; it cannot afford to wait. Indeed, the first country that begins talking about mobilization on July 26 is not Austria, which is about to wage war on Serbia—but Serbia is small, there is no need to mobilize the whole army against Serbia. The first country that begins talking about mobilization is Russia, which knows it will take a long time.

On the other hand, there is a country in Europe—now let us see whether you can guess which one—that is certain it can mobilize extremely quickly. Its plans are extremely precise, so much so that they have already decided that if they mobilize, it is because there is a war. There is no interval, no distinction between the two decisions. If they mobilize, they might as well go to war immediately, immediately, immediately. Has everyone understood? It is the Germans.

So on that same July 26 when Austria rejects Serbia's very moderate reply to the ultimatum, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg warns the Russian government and tells it: “Preparatory military measures by Russia would compel us to take countermeasures. Mobilization, however, means war.” But we cannot believe that Russia wants to unleash such a European war. And two: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the Russians; it is the Russians who must decide to take a step back and not mobilize.

## 6. Austria declares war and the cousins exchange telegrams

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00680–U-00759 -->

July 28, a twist: the Kaiser returns from his cruise and they have him read the Serbian reply to the Austrian ultimatum, and the Kaiser says, but excellent, it is everything we wanted, even more than that. The Serbian reply removes every reason for war. The Kaiser's joy when he discovers this is the first sign that the Germans, even though General von Moltke had said two months earlier, now we are ready—well, it is one thing to say it in theory; now that it is really happening, signs begin to appear that even the Germans are not so sure they want to fight this war after all. The Kaiser is happy: the Serbian reply puts everything right, no war. Too bad that on that very day Austria declares war on Serbia. The Germans have done nothing but insist—once again, we have to understand the people of that time. There is such popular mobilization in Austria. People are convinced: let's go punish these terrorists who threaten the world. There is such a—I'll give you an example—Sigmund Freud. Freud is Austrian, lives in Vienna, is fifty-eight years old, already a mature man who has lived his whole life in the Austrian Empire. When Austria declares war on Serbia, Freud says—writes, I no longer remember, but in any case it is established—for the first time in thirty years I feel truly Austrian. For the first time I think that perhaps this empire has a future. And then this really is a quotation: “My entire libido belongs to Austria-Hungary.” It is July 28.

On July 29 Russia begins mobilization preparations, which is exactly the thing the Germans had told them: don't do it, because otherwise it means war. Russia mobilizes because it is afraid, and the Russians do not want to go to war either, as we discover from another episode that begins on this July 29, which is very curious and is itself typical of this strange age, because the Europe of these years is truly strange, suspended between modernity—liberal parliamentary democracy—and the archaism of these dynastic empires where emperors and kings still have power. On July 29 begins the story of the telegrams from Nicky to Willy and from Willy to Nicky. It begins with a telegram from the Russian tsar Nicholas, who on that July 29 sends a telegram to Kaiser Wilhelm, against whom he is about to mobilize the army. Tsar Nicholas sends a telegram to the Kaiser in English. This too is very significant; it gives you an idea of what England has now become. Fifty years earlier, if a tsar had to write to a king of Prussia, he wrote to him in French; now instead they speak and write to one another in English.

To appreciate the situation, bear in mind that Tsar Nicholas, Emperor Wilhelm, and King George of England are dynastic cousins. Wilhelm and George are first cousins through Queen Victoria—you remember the nineteenth-century Queen Victoria, who died in nineteen hundred and something—while Nicholas and George are first cousins through the Danish royal family, and Nicholas is not Queen Victoria's grandson. They address one another informally, they call one another by their nicknames. [N-024] So on July 29 the Russian tsar sends the Kaiser a telegram: I am glad you have returned from your cruise. The moment is serious and I need your help. “An ignoble war has been declared to a weak country. I beg you in the name of our old friendship to do what you can to stop your allies from going too far.” Signed, Nicky. The Kaiser replies the same day in English, saying: but look at it from the other point of view, Serbia is a terrorist state, Austria has the right, and so on; come on, you Russians have to understand, you take a step back. Signed, Willy. They write to one another for four days, a dozen telegrams from Nicky to Willy and Willy to Nicky, until August 1, when Willy declares war on Nicky. In any event, let us not get ahead of ourselves; we still have precisely four days to deal with.

July 29: the Germans are furious with these stupid Russians who have not understood that by mobilizing they are causing the war. General von Moltke: Russia has sided with a criminal country, a den of terrorists. “Then the mutual destruction of the civilized states of Europe can begin.” A war—this is still General von Moltke—that will annihilate the civilization of almost all Europe for decades. Yet this war must be fought anyway, because no one wants to back down. Here too there emerges, how can I put it, an almost schizophrenic trait in this ruling class, which understands perfectly clearly that this war will be a catastrophe, but at the same time cannot back down and lose face. Let the others take a step back. Not us. At this point, a further split opens in Germany between the military and the politicians.

## 7. German war plans, Belgian neutrality, and Britain's warning

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00760–U-00859 -->

The military say war is inevitable, so let us fight it immediately, let us attack, give us the order to mobilize. The politicians instead say, wait a moment, because from the politicians' point of view it is essential to show that Germany is not to blame for the war: it is entirely the fault of the Russians and the French, not Germany. So why do the German politicians care so much about this? For many reasons, perhaps also to save face, out of pride, out of a sense of honor, but then they care because of German public opinion. Public opinion matters in Germany too; there is a huge socialist party in Germany. What will the socialists say? If people think we have been attacked, then there will be no problems—and in fact that is exactly what will happen. Germany delays mobilization, and the German population will go to war convinced that innocent Germany has been attacked and therefore must be defended.

And then they must prevent England from entering the war. If England sees that Germany is doing everything it can to avoid it, perhaps it will stay out of the war. After all, the English, well, it has become clear, want to stay out if only they can. But there is another problem that makes it very difficult for the Germans to keep England out of the war. It is the last problem, so to speak, that I pull out of my hat: Belgium. You will say, what does Belgium have to do with it? It does. Belgium is a small neutral country that enjoys a guarantee signed by all the great powers, including Germany and England, guaranteeing that Belgium will always be neutral. And this is something the English care a great deal about, because in their vision of Europe, Belgium, with its ports facing the Channel—Antwerp—is a fundamental link for England. We English might quarrel with France, it does not matter; as long as Belgium exists, we have a link with Europe. So the English do not care at all about Serbia, but they certainly would care about Belgium.

And why on earth should the Germans be concerned about Belgium? Because of the plans. Because the German general staff has a perfect plan for fighting a war against France and Russia and winning it in a few weeks. Everything has been calculated. The Russians are slow, so we send the entire army, the famous 11,000 trains, against France. We invade it and finish the war in a few weeks. But in order to invade France and outflank the French army—forgive me for going into these strategic considerations—the German military have decided that they must pass through Belgium. It is the famous Schlieffen Plan, which some of you may remember, perfected over years and years. The German military say: if you want us to win the war for you, this is the plan, we have to pass through Belgium; it is up to you politicians to work out how to manage the matter.

On July 29, the Kaiser and the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg realize that if the gamble is that England stays out of the war, they must nevertheless work out what to do about this matter of Belgium, because we Germans will invade Belgium; it has already been decided. So on July 29 the Kaiser and the chancellor make a crucial decision, which will later be judged unanimously the stupidest decision the Germans made in those days. They write to London, to the English government, giving guarantees. First: if in this war there is a war against France, the English can rest easy, Germany has no territorial ambitions. We do not want to take pieces of France, and therefore we do not want to upset the European balance. Second: the Germans assure the English government that if by chance during the war they should be forced to invade Belgium, they will nevertheless evacuate it at the end of the war. And if the Belgians behave themselves and do not prove hostile, they will even pay them damages. And finally the Germans write to London that, after all, let us think about it carefully, because we Germans and you English could still come to an agreement; together we are very strong, we can divide up the world. What do you care about being friends with France? What do you care about Belgium? Let us divide the world between us.

In London they see this message arrive and are stunned. A senior Foreign Office official comments: “The only comment that need be made on these astounding proposals is that they reflect discredit on the statesman who makes them.” On top of that, the English say, what, they are telling us that they are going to invade Belgium; they have actually put it in writing. Grey calls the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, and tells him: look, if war breaks out, we cannot stay out, and at that point we too will enter the war alongside our friends. Grey also says something that everyone is repeating almost obsessively in those days: “if war did break out, it would be the greatest catastrophe that the world had ever seen” But England cannot make this agreement with Germany; betraying its friends would be an indelible disgrace, so think very carefully in Berlin. And three: for the English, responsibility for everything lies in Berlin. Berlin is where they must take a step back.

At this news from London, the German reaction is uncontrollable. The Kaiser writes on the communication: “Aha! The vile deceiver! Vile blackguard! I will never make a naval agreement with such scoundrels! The vile shopkeeper rabble tried to deceive us with dinners and speeches. But he avoids saying the word and threatens us instead! Vile blackguard! England alone bears responsibility for war and peace, no longer we! That must also be made clear publicly.” And four: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the English. That same day, July 29, the German ambassador in Brussels, von Below-Saleske, receives a sealed packet from Berlin with instructions not to open it until further telegraphic communications arrive. Another useless detail—we are nearly there, I will inflict it on you anyway. Von Below-Saleske is a career diplomat who is in the habit of joking that he brings bad luck. He was in China, and the Boxer Rebellion broke out. He was in Turkey, and the Young Turk Revolution broke out. But then he says, now, though, I can relax: I am in Belgium, nothing will ever happen in Brussels. Let us pick up the pace or I will keep you here all night.

## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

During the night between July 29 and 30, they do not go to sleep in Berlin, because the news from London has thrown them into a panic. It is becoming apparent that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, Bethmann Hollweg, the German chancellor, calls Vienna and says, have you understood nothing, do you really want to wage war on Serbia? Take it easy, slow down. Germany does not intend to be dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine their faces in Vienna, where until the previous day the Germans had been telling them to go ahead. So, and five: for the Germans, responsibility now lies in Vienna.

In the meantime, however, we have now reached July 30. Russia, after finishing the preparations—and the preparations alone already take several days—orders general mobilization on July 30. In Berlin the military go to the government and say, let us mobilize immediately too. The government says, let us wait one more day, because perhaps in Vienna they will manage to come up with something. On this day, July 30, in Berlin the crisis between the military and the politicians comes close—I will not say to a coup d'état, but yes, to high treason. That afternoon an afternoon newspaper comes out—in those days there are afternoon newspapers in Germany—announcing the mobilization order. It is not true. The newspaper is seized, and then it is discovered that the general staff leaked the false news to present the government with a fait accompli. General von Moltke telephones his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria has not mobilized yet, incidentally, because Austria does things so slowly that it has not mobilized yet. And Moltke says to Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize then we will have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government to mobilize.

The government is still holding firm, but it is in a state of total panic. That day another characteristic aspect of the communications of those very last days surfaces: the feeling everyone has that something is happening which no one wants and which can no longer be prevented. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.” There, that is a fine declaration of the surrender of politics. The stone is rolling and we no longer know what to do. That day the New York Stock Exchange collapses. July 31—there is the time difference—in the morning the news arrives from New York, the London Stock Exchange collapses. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.

## 9. 31 July–1 August: ultimatums, war, and an impossible reversal

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00891–U-00959 -->

Germany still does not mobilize, but it sends Russia an ultimatum: you have begun mobilization; stop it. If you stop it by midnight, fine, otherwise it is war. In London on that July 31, Grey, the foreign secretary, is now convinced that war is inevitable and that England must enter it, but he is in the minority in the government. The government continues to say, this has nothing to do with us. Grey does what he can: he sends a telegram to Paris and one to Berlin asking for a guarantee that Belgian neutrality will be respected. The French reply within an hour; the Germans do not reply.

On August 1 Grey speaks to the government, calls Germany an aggressor as dangerous as Napoleon, and lets it be understood that he will resign if England does not enter the war. But the majority of the ministers are opposed. The majority of the ministers threaten to resign if there is a war. Outside the door the French ambassador, Cambon, is waiting, as he has done throughout all those days. Do you understand the job of the French ambassador in London in those days? To make sure that, since war is about to break out, the English enter the war with us—we are such good friends. Grey is forced to go out and tell Cambon that there is no guarantee, the government is granting nothing. Cambon enters the office of a friend there at the English Foreign Office. His friend describes him as white as a sheet, on the verge of bursting into tears. The French ambassador collapses onto a chair and says to his friend, “They're going to abandon us; they're going to let us down.”

Meanwhile, however, everything is plunging onward. On that August 1 Germany declares war on Russia. It is the first declaration of war between two great powers. Why does Germany declare war? Well, because that is the procedure. We have sent the ultimatum, we have told the Russians to stop by midnight, the Russians have not stopped, therefore we have to declare war. The previous evening the ministers had met at Bethmann Hollweg's house to draft the declaration of war on Russia. The navy minister Tirpitz says, “On 1 August I learned in the Federal Council session that we had followed the ultimatum with a declaration of war on Russia. […] We were to assume the odium of the attacking party because of the jurists of the Foreign Office. […] Presumably we did it out of formal legal conscientiousness.” That is the procedure: you send the ultimatum, after the ultimatum you must declare war. That same day, August 1 at four in the afternoon, Germany declares war on Russia and orders general mobilization. For them it really is the same thing.

But there is still room for a farce, a grotesque misunderstanding. At five in the afternoon, a telegram arrives in Berlin from the ambassador in London, Prince Lichnowsky. Lichnowsky says: “Sir E. Grey just asked me by telephone whether I believed that I could declare that we would not attack France in a war between Germany and Russia in case France should remain neutral. I declared that I thought I could give that assurance.” The telegram arrives in Berlin and causes pandemonium. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg say, fantastic, we no longer have to fight France and Russia, only Russia. If England and France stay out, we are fine; all we have to do is change the mobilization orders, everything against Russia.

They summon General von Moltke, who commands the army and is organizing the great mobilization. Eleven thousand trains have already begun moving. At that precise moment, the telegram containing the secret orders is arriving at all the stations and all the barracks in Germany. Everything has been set in motion. They summon von Moltke to the imperial palace. I have to tell you this about von Moltke: he is not a great general; they will soon remove him from command. He is there above all because he is the nephew of a great man. In the nineteenth century there had been another von Moltke, a very famous Prussian general who won all his wars. So, the younger von Moltke is there mainly because he is the nephew of that great uncle, but he is a man with rather fragile nerves.

He arrives at the imperial palace and they tell him: look, a telegram from London, England and France will probably stay out, fantastic, change the mobilization orders, everything against Russia. Von Moltke nearly has a fit. He starts to cry and declares that it cannot be done. Everything is already moving: 11,000 trains, millions of people, everything calculated to the second. It cannot be done. They insist. Moltke, who, I repeat, is on the verge of collapse—that evening he will return home and have a heart attack—stands firm and says it cannot be done. The Kaiser tells him: “Your uncle would have given me a different answer!” Moltke is a broken man. In the meantime, however, mobilization cannot be changed, and therefore everything continues according to plan. That evening the Germans invade Luxembourg. The first step toward the invasion of Belgium.

## 10. 2–4 August: Belgium brings Britain into the war

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01015 -->

Before invading Belgium, however, the formalities have to be observed: they must give it the ultimatum. On August 2, von Below-Saleske in Brussels receives the telegram: open the sealed packet, deliver the ultimatum to Belgium while pretending it has just arrived—he has had it there for several days. But the ultimatum to Belgium, what it says to the Belgians, is a heap of lies. Here another aspect begins—I am finishing, but it is the last slightly grotesque aspect of this situation. You have seen how complicated and at the same time terrifyingly simple the path leading to war is. Despite all this, the Germans remain convinced that in order to send someone an ultimatum or declare war on them, one needs pretexts, excuses. And so they tell the Belgians: “The German Government has received reliable information according to which French forces intend to march on the Meuse by way of Givet and Namur.”; we Germans, you understand, must enter Belgium too for our own security, but we do not want the Belgians to interpret it as a hostile act. If Belgium remains neutral and behaves itself, the Germans will evacuate it at the end of the war, we promise. The Belgians decide to resist.

That evening in Berlin, at Bethmann Hollweg's house, they prepare the declaration of war on France. Admiral von Tirpitz says, but why do we have to declare war on France too? Let us wait for them to declare war on us. They explain to him that according to the rules, this is how it must be done. The declaration of war on France too is a heap of lies. French aircraft have flown over Germany, they have dropped bombs, they have killed civilians, and therefore France has put itself at war on its own, and so on. Evidently, even when one practices power politics, there is this need to cling to something for public opinion. Perhaps it reminds me of Colin Powell at the UN with the vial of Saddam Hussein's biological weapons, but anyway, never mind.

That evening, August 2, in London, Grey learns of the German ultimatum to Belgium and finally wrests the mobilization order from the English government. Several ministers resign. That evening an American diplomat in Brussels notes in his diary: “Nobody seems to remember that a few days ago Serbia was playing a star rôle in this affair. She seems to have faded away behind the scenes.”

August 3, we really are at the end. Germany declares war on France. Grey wrests from the House of Commons—finally, it is the first parliament to come into play and do something in this crisis, which has been managed entirely by governments and sovereigns—Grey wrests from the House of Commons authorization to send the Germans an ultimatum. If they invade Belgium, it is war. August 4, the Germans invade Belgium. The English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum to see Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, not knowing that the Germans have already begun the invasion of Belgium that morning.

And the whole affair ends—this story of mine too ends—with two famous phrases, one very famous, the other a little less so. The very famous phrase is the one from the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, who, when the English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum, makes a scene and says: “just for a word—'neutrality,' a word which in war time had so often been disregarded—just for a scrap of paper Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation” It is a phrase that has passed into history as the emblem, let us say, of this politics that thinks it can sign treaties and then, well, if it wants, pretend they do not exist—there you have treaties.

The other historic phrase is a little less historic, but anyway it is the Kaiser's. Remember, he is the cousin of the king of England and of the tsar, and they all call one another by nicknames and address one another informally. And when it is obvious at this point that there is war against Russia, against France, and also against England, and they are all little grandsons of Queen Victoria, remember? The Kaiser declares: who would have thought that Georgie and Nicky were about to betray me? If my grandmother had been alive, she would never have allowed it.
