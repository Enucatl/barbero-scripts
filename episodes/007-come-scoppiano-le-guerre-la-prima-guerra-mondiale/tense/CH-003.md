## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second part of this talk, of this chat, will be a kind of countdown through the final days, but first we have to sketch a broader picture, get you inside the heads of the people governing the world at that moment. What is the world like, what is Europe like at that moment? The Europe of 1914 is experiencing a period of great prosperity, of great economic expansion. After the decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy has started moving again; everyone is growing. There is even a kind of globalization avant la lettre. Well, today it is very easy to find globalizations in the past; I have seen every kind of them. Even the Roman Empire was a globalization. But in any case this one in 1914 has the features of globalization: the economies are interdependent, the stock exchanges are interdependent, the currencies are linked, anchored to gold. The Italian lira is worth exactly as much as the French franc. You can travel throughout Europe without a passport, except in Russia. You need a passport to enter Russia. But otherwise this Europe is so interdependent that it seems incredible that a war could break out among these powers. And yet at the same time all these countries, Italy included, are armed to the teeth. They all have military spending at a level comparable to American military spending today. They spend enormous sums on the army, on the navy, and why do they do it? They do it because they are afraid of one another. An important aspect in understanding the mentality of the ruling classes of that era is that they are all steeped in a kind of Darwinian ideology, but a very simplified one, right? The survival of the fittest, the struggle for survival, the survival of the strongest. They are convinced that states, too, are struggling with one another in this way. The strongest will triumph, the weakest will be eaten. And because they are convinced of this, everyone wants to be strong and they all arm themselves in order to survive. No one imagines that they themselves will be the aggressor in the future; it is always the others who are the aggressors. We are threatened, we must be strong, we must have a great army, and since we are spending an enormous amount of money on a great army, well, we have to expect that perhaps sooner or later we will use it. It cannot be ruled out; in fact it is quite likely, some say. Those years—it is striking, because in those years books come out that talk quite calmly about the next war. It is a foreseeable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911 a book comes out in Germany by a German general, General von Bernhardi, entitled *Germany and the Next War*. Three years remain before the First World War. In this book there are chapters entitled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I have given a German example because German generals are particularly inclined to state these arguments explicitly, but in reality similar arguments are made in every country. And of course not everyone reasons that way. There are also lucid people who say: look, with the interdependent economies we have, a war among the great powers would be a catastrophe. Let me mention another book that comes out in those years. These are the books that prime ministers and army commanders read; they keep them on their bedside tables. In 1910 a book comes out by an English writer, Norman Angell, entitled *The Great Illusion*. Do you remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? Well, it later copies its title from this book, which is very famous in its day. What is the great illusion? The author says that the great illusion is precisely that of our great powers, which believe that having a great army and preparing to win the next war is a guarantee. It is not. We will all lose the next war, this author says. The next war will destroy even the victors; it is an illusion to believe that strength will save us. Now, books like this circulate, politicians read them, and in the Europe of 1914 various politicians have become fairly convinced that a war among the great powers would indeed be a catastrophe. But the paradox is that these politicians are at the head of countries armed to the teeth. The scenario of the next war is so foreseeable that at a certain point this happens. Let me tell you this detail. In London, at a certain point, the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, is speaking with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. At a certain point the British foreign secretary Grey says to the German ambassador—listen to this conversation—“But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.”. That is the description of what I am telling you about tonight, the Sarajevo crisis, the crisis of the summer of 1914, except that this conversation takes place two years earlier. In December 1912 Grey says these things to the German ambassador. Those are the scenarios in which a war can break out, and there are only a few places. The Balkans are a typical place, with all the unrest there. Now, starting from the fact that the scenario of the next war is foreseeable, a split nevertheless emerges between the politicians and the military, because the politicians say: look, we must do everything we can to avoid this scenario; it would be a catastrophe if it came to pass. But the military have a different job. The military’s job is to say: and what do we do if it happens anyway? If it happens anyway, we must be ready. So in those years of great economic growth the military press: let us use all this money, let us use it to become stronger. And the politicians give in. The years preceding 1914 are years of an arms race in which every country strengthens its army, not because it wants to attack anyone but because it is concerned about its security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passes a law under which military service, conscription, is increased from two to three years. Imagine a world in which conscription lasts three years. What do the Germans say when the French parliament passes the law increasing military service? The Germans say: the French want to make war, they are threatening us. It is a very understandable reaction that political scientists have studied. It is a reaction called the security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors become frightened, they feel less safe than before, so they arm themselves too, and so on and so on. The penultimate thing before we reach—return to—Austria, which is deciding what to do against Serbia. The penultimate thing needed to make the picture of that moment clear. The military do not merely ask for more weapons; they also do something else. Their job is to say: if war really happens, we must be ready. Being ready means making plans. In 1914 the great powers have not fought one another for a very long time. Almost fifty years have passed since the Crimean War, since the Franco-Prussian War. In those fifty years the general staffs have done nothing but prepare plans: if war breaks out—that particular war, against that particular enemy—what do we do? Plans are ready for every possible war, extremely detailed, extremely precise. Every year they reopen them and refine them, adjust a detail. They have perfected them for fifty years. Plans are ready for everything. An anecdote about this: in November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium is on a state visit to Berlin. There is a reception, and during the reception Kaiser Wilhelm points out a general to the king of Belgium and tells him, The Emperor introduces General von Kluck to him, saying with a distinctly deliberate emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.”. The plans are so complete that everything has already been decided, even who will command each individual army, and so on. Everything is ready, should war come. Finally, one last thing before returning to Vienna, where they are discussing exactly what to demand of the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. One last thing: this arms race of those years has another characteristic that makes it very dangerous, one that makes it possible to predict how the balance of power will evolve. Armaments are slow things. You lay down a battleship and it will be ready in five years; you increase conscription by one year and two years pass before the effects are felt, and so on and so on. So everyone pays close attention: when will we be at our strongest? And when will they? Russia, for example, is militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it is making enormous investments. In a few years it will be much stronger. So in this climate, you understand, the military do their job. Their job is to say: if there is a war, we must be ready. And by the way, if there is a war, when would it suit us for it to happen? Would it suit us for it to happen soon, or would it suit us for it to happen later? All military officers make these calculations. And then every so often something changes. In April 1914 it becomes known that Russia and Britain are negotiating a naval agreement. I have hardly mentioned Britain so far; it has no alliances. I will come back to Britain in a moment, but it is now beginning talks with Russia. You understand what effect this has in Berlin? In Berlin they say: we already have Russia and France allied with each other against us in the next war, when it comes. If Britain were to ally with them too, our position would become worse. These talks between the Russians and the British begin in April. In May 1914 the commander of the German army, von Moltke, states in private, not in public: from this moment on, every day of delay can only diminish our probability of success. A few more weeks: on 1 June 1914 von Moltke declares, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.”. Four weeks remain before the Sarajevo assassination.
