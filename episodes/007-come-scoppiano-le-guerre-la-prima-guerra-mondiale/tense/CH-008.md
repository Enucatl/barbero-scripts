## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

During the night between July 29 and 30, they do not go to sleep in Berlin, because the news from London has thrown them into a panic. It is becoming apparent that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, Bethmann Hollweg, the German chancellor, calls Vienna and says, have you understood nothing, do you really want to wage war on Serbia? Take it easy, slow down. Germany does not intend to be dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine their faces in Vienna, where until the previous day the Germans had been telling them to go ahead. So, and five: for the Germans, responsibility now lies in Vienna.

In the meantime, however, we have now reached July 30. Russia, after finishing the preparations—and the preparations alone already take several days—orders general mobilization on July 30. In Berlin the military go to the government and say, let us mobilize immediately too. The government says, let us wait one more day, because perhaps in Vienna they will manage to come up with something. On this day, July 30, in Berlin the crisis between the military and the politicians comes close—I will not say to a coup d'état, but yes, to high treason. That afternoon an afternoon newspaper comes out—in those days there are afternoon newspapers in Germany—announcing the mobilization order. It is not true. The newspaper is seized, and then it is discovered that the general staff leaked the false news to present the government with a fait accompli. General von Moltke telephones his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria has not mobilized yet, incidentally, because Austria does things so slowly that it has not mobilized yet. And Moltke says to Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize then we will have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government to mobilize.

The government is still holding firm, but it is in a state of total panic. That day another characteristic aspect of the communications of those very last days surfaces: the feeling everyone has that something is happening which no one wants and which can no longer be prevented. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.” There, that is a fine declaration of the surrender of politics. The stone is rolling and we no longer know what to do. That day the New York Stock Exchange collapses. July 31—there is the time difference—in the morning the news arrives from New York, the London Stock Exchange collapses. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.
