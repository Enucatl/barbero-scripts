## 6. Austria declares war and the cousins exchange telegrams

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00680–U-00759 -->

July 28, a twist: the Kaiser returns from his cruise and they have him read the Serbian reply to the Austrian ultimatum, and the Kaiser says, but excellent, it is everything we wanted, even more than that. The Serbian reply removes every reason for war. The Kaiser's joy when he discovers this is the first sign that the Germans, even though General von Moltke had said two months earlier, now we are ready—well, it is one thing to say it in theory; now that it is really happening, signs begin to appear that even the Germans are not so sure they want to fight this war after all. The Kaiser is happy: the Serbian reply puts everything right, no war. Too bad that on that very day Austria declares war on Serbia. The Germans have done nothing but insist—once again, we have to understand the people of that time. There is such popular mobilization in Austria. People are convinced: let's go punish these terrorists who threaten the world. There is such a—I'll give you an example—Sigmund Freud. Freud is Austrian, lives in Vienna, is fifty-eight years old, already a mature man who has lived his whole life in the Austrian Empire. When Austria declares war on Serbia, Freud says—writes, I no longer remember, but in any case it is established—for the first time in thirty years I feel truly Austrian. For the first time I think that perhaps this empire has a future. And then this really is a quotation: “My entire libido belongs to Austria-Hungary.” It is July 28.

On July 29 Russia begins mobilization preparations, which is exactly the thing the Germans had told them: don't do it, because otherwise it means war. Russia mobilizes because it is afraid, and the Russians do not want to go to war either, as we discover from another episode that begins on this July 29, which is very curious and is itself typical of this strange age, because the Europe of these years is truly strange, suspended between modernity—liberal parliamentary democracy—and the archaism of these dynastic empires where emperors and kings still have power. On July 29 begins the story of the telegrams from Nicky to Willy and from Willy to Nicky. It begins with a telegram from the Russian tsar Nicholas, who on that July 29 sends a telegram to Kaiser Wilhelm, against whom he is about to mobilize the army. Tsar Nicholas sends a telegram to the Kaiser in English. This too is very significant; it gives you an idea of what England has now become. Fifty years earlier, if a tsar had to write to a king of Prussia, he wrote to him in French; now instead they speak and write to one another in English.

To appreciate the situation, bear in mind that Tsar Nicholas, Emperor Wilhelm, and King George of England are dynastic cousins. Wilhelm and George are first cousins through Queen Victoria—you remember the nineteenth-century Queen Victoria, who died in nineteen hundred and something—while Nicholas and George are first cousins through the Danish royal family, and Nicholas is not Queen Victoria's grandson. They address one another informally, they call one another by their nicknames. [N-024] So on July 29 the Russian tsar sends the Kaiser a telegram: I am glad you have returned from your cruise. The moment is serious and I need your help. “An ignoble war has been declared to a weak country. I beg you in the name of our old friendship to do what you can to stop your allies from going too far.” Signed, Nicky. The Kaiser replies the same day in English, saying: but look at it from the other point of view, Serbia is a terrorist state, Austria has the right, and so on; come on, you Russians have to understand, you take a step back. Signed, Willy. They write to one another for four days, a dozen telegrams from Nicky to Willy and Willy to Nicky, until August 1, when Willy declares war on Nicky. In any event, let us not get ahead of ourselves; we still have precisely four days to deal with.

July 29: the Germans are furious with these stupid Russians who have not understood that by mobilizing they are causing the war. General von Moltke: Russia has sided with a criminal country, a den of terrorists. “Then the mutual destruction of the civilized states of Europe can begin.” A war—this is still General von Moltke—that will annihilate the civilization of almost all Europe for decades. Yet this war must be fought anyway, because no one wants to back down. Here too there emerges, how can I put it, an almost schizophrenic trait in this ruling class, which understands perfectly clearly that this war will be a catastrophe, but at the same time cannot back down and lose face. Let the others take a step back. Not us. At this point, a further split opens in Germany between the military and the politicians.
