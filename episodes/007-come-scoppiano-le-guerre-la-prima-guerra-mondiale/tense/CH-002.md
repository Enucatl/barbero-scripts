## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there is a problem: Serbia has a great friend, a great protector—Russia. The Russia of Tsar Nicholas II. The friendship between Serbia and Russia is something—it is an obvious fact of European geography; it is true even today. Russia is a Slavic, Orthodox power; the Serbs are Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let us broaden the discussion for a moment. Serbia has a great friend, Russia. Everyone in that world of 1914 tries to have friends and allies. The weight of alliances is a fundamental factor in understanding how the world slides into world war, because that is not a world of blocs. Well, ours is no longer one either by now—only the American bloc remains—but in any case we all remember when our world was divided into two blocs and nothing happened outside those two blocs. The world of 1914 is not like that. It is a world of great and small powers. Which are the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and then Italy is also usually included as a courtesy, the last of the great powers with a little effort. And then there are the small powers, but they too are bellicose, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They are not divided into blocs; each looks after itself.

This is the age in which nationalism is at its height. Every country is convinced that it has a duty to look after itself. It is what an Italian politician, Prime Minister Salandra, in those very days calls “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But if one has to look after oneself, one also has to try to have allies, because alone one comes to a bad end. So each country seeks alliances for its own security. Serbia is allied with Russia.

The Austrians think that, well, the world will understand: Serbia is clearly a rogue state that has organized terrorism; it has to be punished for everyone's security. But they begin to wonder whether, if Austria attacks Serbia, the Russians might intervene. Yet Austria too has a powerful friend, more powerful than itself, because Austria is a great empire in decline—all things considered, almost everyone suspects it is in decline—whereas Austria has an immensely powerful friend that is becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. The Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm—you remember Wilhelm with the great handlebar moustache and the spiked helmet? But beyond the Kaiser, who is a somewhat comical figure, Germany is a very great power. It has the strongest army in the world and is building the strongest industry in the world. In fact, Germany has already become the greatest industrial power; it has already overtaken Britain.

Austria is allied with Germany in an alliance that binds the two countries to help one another if either is attacked. To tell the truth, the alliance also includes Italy. It is called the Triple Alliance for a reason. But during those days of crisis in 1914 it is perfectly clear that no one has any faith in Italy. The Austrians do not believe that, if they go to war, Italy will come to their aid. They rule it out, excluding the whole idea from the outset, and in fact they are right. In the name of sacred egoism, Italy will decide to stay out for the time being.

But there is Germany. Austria has enough good sense to know that, before doing anything against Serbia, it has to ask what the Germans think. Franz Ferdinand's funeral takes place on 3 July. The Emperor, the old Emperor Franz Joseph, has signed his appeal to Kaiser Wilhelm on 2 July; it is delivered to Wilhelm on 5 July with the accompanying memorandum. [N-005] Now, this letter from the Emperor of Austria is very interesting. It is not precise or lucid; it does not say, “This is what we want to do.” It is a letter more or less along these lines. Franz Joseph says to Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” This is a letter that makes me think of the reasons with which the Western powers went into Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. In other words, something must be done. There is no lucid reasoning about the consequences, no consideration of what today is called an exit strategy. “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agrees. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reaches him, Kaiser Wilhelm notes on the telegram that “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.”. Consequently, on 5 July Kaiser Wilhelm replies to his Austrian counterpart, telling him: “Go ahead, do not worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser also adds: “Really, do not worry. The Russians will not have the courage to interfere. We are certain Russia will stay out.” That is the first gamble. This evening's story is a story of one gamble after another, all of them wrong. It is as though—as we shall see—wars break out not because someone wants them. No one ever wants war, at least not world war, general war; no one ever wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

I want to pause for another moment over this assurance Kaiser Wilhelm sends to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we are with you, do not worry.” It is a reassurance that historians later call the blank cheque. How is this blank cheque given? Germany is already a parliamentary country; it has political parties, a parliament, free elections. The Kaiser consults no one. Before consulting anyone at all, he himself writes to his counterpart, the Emperor of Austria, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then the Kaiser does consult someone. Whom? Parliament? Not a chance. He consults the Chancellor—the prime minister, that is. He consults the commander of the army, General von Moltke, and General von Moltke tells him, “If war breaks out, we are ready.” And then—this is interesting—whom else does Kaiser Wilhelm consult? He consults Alfred Krupp, the great arms industrialist. Krupp is the great—the largest arms factory in the world. The Kaiser feels the need to consult Alfred Krupp. If war should ever break out, are the Krupp works ready? And Alfred Krupp reassures him: the Krupp works are ready.

But they are certain that war will not break out. Indeed, after sending the blank cheque to Vienna, the Kaiser boards his yacht and goes on a cruise in the Baltic. It is July, people want to take their holidays, and the Kaiser goes on holiday too. This German gamble—that yes, there is a risk of war, but you will see that war will not come—is a crucial aspect of the argument we must discuss this evening, so I must dwell on it a little longer.

The gamble is even greater than it appears because of the question of alliances, since Russia is not the only party involved. Russia is Serbia's great friend, but Russia has an alliance with France. Politically speaking it is an unnatural alliance, if you think about it: France, the secular, anticlerical, radical republic, and the Tsar's empire. The Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. It is an unnatural alliance, and yet France and Russia have been allies for twenty years. Why? One need only look at the map—the map of that time. Today Germany seems to us a great power. The Germany of that time is much larger. Poland does not exist; Czechoslovakia does not exist; the Baltic states do not exist. There is only Germany and then, at a certain point, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany stands in the middle, sharing borders with France and Russia. And so it is clear that France and Russia have every interest in being allies.

And when the Germans gamble that there will be no war—that there will be no world war; the small war between Austria and Serbia, yes, certainly, that is perfectly fine, it is necessary—but when they gamble that there will be no war, the Germans are gambling that there will be no war against Russia and France, because that is the risk. Why do they take that risk?
