## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Does von Moltke organize the Sarajevo assassination? No, obviously. It is the catalyst that brings out all these things that have been simmering there for years. Let us return to Vienna, where they are preparing the ultimatum to send to Serbia. They will send an extremely harsh ultimatum because the Austrians do not want the Serbs to accept it. The Austrians want war and they have a blank cheque from Germany. It will be an extremely severe ultimatum. The Germans press, the Germans press Vienna, saying: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli; the sooner you act the better; the Russians will not dare to intervene. But Austria is a country where things are done calmly. It is a country where things are done slowly. There are two governments, the Austrian one and the Hungarian one; they have to reach agreement. And then the Austrian army is a slow, backward army; it takes a long time to prepare. What is more, consider something no one today would think of: it is July, harvest time. The soldiers are all at home on leave—Austria is an agricultural country—to harvest the grain. It takes time. The Austrians are in no hurry; in Berlin they are champing at the bit: we are always being dragged along behind this backward country. Finally, on 23 July, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum is ready. It is delivered to the Serbian government. It is an extremely harsh ultimatum. The Austrians say to Serbia: the Serbian government has done nothing to prevent the proliferation of terrorism; Serbian officers and officials are involved in the Sarajevo assassination. Therefore we Austrians want the Serbian government to publish a declaration in which it dissociates itself from every form of hostility toward Austria. The Serbian government must close all the newspapers and dissolve all the associations that sow hatred against Austria. The Serbian government must dismiss all public officials and officers who are in some way involved with forms of hostility toward Austria. It must have these people arrested—the Austrians present the list—and it must open investigations into terrorism on Serbian territory in which Austrian investigators will take part. On 24 July the capitals of the whole world awake to the news of this ultimatum, and the ultimatum is received very badly. In St. Petersburg the Russian foreign minister summons the Austrian ambassador and tells him: but do you want to make war on Serbia? Are the Germans inciting you? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That is number one: for the Russians the responsibility lies with Vienna and the Austrians. We will see a whole series of these phrases, “you are the ones assuming responsibility.” This is only the first. In London too, the foreign secretary Grey is aghast. He calls the German ambassador Lichnowsky and tells him: I have never seen one state send another state a document like this. It is impossible for the Serbs to accept it. Here there is a small period detail, right, to give a sense of the era. Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador, says: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!” Now we are in London, then, in this exchange between Grey and Lichnowsky. What is happening in London? In London the German gamble does indeed appear to be holding for the moment. British public opinion does not give the slightest damn about Serbia. Britain has alliances with no one. It has a friendship with France that has lasted for years, this must be said. British and French military officers are accustomed to consulting each other; they have even prepared plans together, they have become fairly friendly. But there are no obligations, there are no treaties. And Britain is an isolationist country at that moment. Britain is a country that dominates the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock exchange. It does not give a damn about minor continental disputes, and especially about the Balkans. British newspapers write in those days: why should we let ourselves be dragged into someone else’s war? The *Manchester Guardian* writes on 30 July, after the ultimatum: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” [N-017] The proof that until that moment Britain is truly outside, outside these thoughts, is in the minutes of the British government, of cabinet meetings. From the day of the Sarajevo assassination, 28 June, to the day when the whole world is thrown into an uproar because the Austrians have delivered the ultimatum to Serbia, 24 July, for almost a month the British government never discusses foreign policy. They have problems of their own. They have Ireland, which is not yet independent, where there is terrorism, there is everything, a revolution about to break out. Foreign policy does not exist for the British. There are only some people in Britain who reason differently. There are some officials in the Foreign Office; we know from the notes they exchange in those days. There are some officials in the Foreign Office who are instead afraid of what is happening. And again, the security paradox. We are fairly safe; Germany is becoming very strong. The Germans say they are doing it only because they are afraid of us, but if they become too strong, then—some senior Foreign Office official in London says in those days—we must stand with the French and the Russians, because this struggle is not for the possession of Serbia but between a Germany that aspires to political dictatorship in Europe and the powers that want to remain free. And then there are people in Britain who think that way, and they are the military officers. By then the military are accustomed to considering themselves friends of the French, to doing things together with them. They know it will be difficult for Britain to enter the war alongside France because public opinion does not want it, and for years they have been brooding over this matter. Here too it is striking to see how, for years and years, they have thought and discussed and reasoned among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do? There is a revealing anecdote, from 1910. An English general who is very friendly with the French, Wilson, is talking with a French general, Foch, and Wilson says to him: well, if war breaks out, assuming that we come to help you—I hope so, but who knows, Wilson says—what would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could really help France? We are four years earlier, in 1910. What would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could help France? And Foch replies: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is that British public opinion should be mobilized for a cause. It is almost midnight; let us begin the countdown. On 25 July the Serbian reply to the ultimatum arrives.
