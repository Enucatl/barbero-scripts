## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we began thinking about what I might come and talk about here in Sarzana, there was already something of the atmosphere that, after all, is still with us now: there was already news from Ukraine, there was news from Iraq. Pope Francis had not yet said, let us say, that Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction..., whereas he did say it a short while ago. And so, clearly, it is increasingly timely to ask how—there, do I have to repeat the first part? No, it was only a way of introducing the subject anyway; this is fine too. How do wars break out? In fact, I think what I am going to tell you is also fairly reassuring, in the sense that it shows that wars break out in situations—the great world wars break out in situations—quite different from the one we are experiencing now. Today we are experiencing a disastrous international situation, full of suffering, full of uncertainties, but still quite different from the scenarios that were taking shape in Europe, for example in the Europe of 1914, which is where we begin this evening.

How does the First World War break out? Well, deep down we all know: it begins with an assassination, the Sarajevo assassination. On 28 June 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists kills Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to say about the outbreak of the First World War is that it arises from an act of terrorism. And terrorism, then as now, provokes visceral reactions in public opinion and therefore in governments as well. To us today, in considering an act of terrorism, it may seem that killing an archduke is perhaps, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. Instead, one thing we must understand very clearly if we are to grasp what the people of 1914 are like is that, for them—at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who is heir to the imperial throne is far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers would have been, if they could ever have imagined such a thing.

Just think: the Austro-Hungarian Empire is a great power. It is still a great power—weakened, in crisis—but it is one of the few great powers in the world. Emperor Franz Joseph rules the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Emperor Franz Joseph is eighty-three years old, which means he is going to die very soon. No one knows when, but he is certain to die very soon. Franz Joseph has reigned over the Austrian Empire since time immemorial; he is nearing sixty-six years on the throne. He will die very soon, everyone knows that. In fact, he will die on 21 November 1916, a little over two years later; we know that. [N-002] And when Franz Joseph dies, Archduke Franz Ferdinand will become the sovereign of one of the world's great powers for the rest of his life. You understand that killing him changes the situation somewhat. It is not like killing a president of the United States, because another one is chosen four years later anyway. Here they are killing someone who would have helped govern the world for the rest of his life. So the assassination is extremely serious: an act of terrorism of enormous gravity, with complicity abroad.

Here we must remember how—I will spend the whole evening telling you, “We must remember this,” and you will forgive me—the way a war breaks out is not so simple. There is a whole series of connections. The first, the first point to bring into focus: Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia is then, just as it has been in our own time, a sensitive place. Bosnia is also then a place with an ethnic mixture, with hostile ethnic groups. Bosnia has been occupied by Austria-Hungary for some years, and some of its inhabitants are happy about it. The Croat population is happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslim population is very happy to belong to the Austrian Empire. Indeed, Bosnian Muslim soldiers will fight faithfully for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments wearing fezzes.

But in Serbia—in Serbia, in Bosnia—there is a proportion of the Serbian population that is not at all happy to belong to Austria. Because right next door is Serbia, the Kingdom of Serbia: independent Serbia, small but bellicose, a battle-hardened state with military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs suffer under Austrian imperial rule. And so there is discontent, and there is terrorism. And the Austrians are certain that this terrorism is backed, supported, and organized in Serbia; that this assassination, in which the Archduke and his wife have been killed, has been organized in Serbia with complicity within the Serbian government and the Serbian army. The Austrians are certain of it from the very first day. Among other things, it is true: the investigations will bring it to light; there is no doubt about it. There is such complicity in Serbia.

And so Austria is determined to punish Serbia. And here you really must make the effort to think of them not as Austria and Serbia, forgotten states as they are then—who remembers anymore? No. Here there is a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad, and there is the country that has suffered the terrorist attack, which is determined to punish the rogue state. In Austria there is a wave of mass indignation. Crowds are in the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” In German it even has assonance: *Serbien muss sterben*.
