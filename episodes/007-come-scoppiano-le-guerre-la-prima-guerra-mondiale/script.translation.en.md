# Come scoppiano le guerre: La prima guerra mondiale — faithful English translation

## 1. Sarajevo, terrorism, and the Austrian case against Serbia

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00123 -->

Good evening. So, how do wars break out? A few months ago, when we began thinking about what I might come and talk about here in Sarzana, there was already something of the atmosphere that, after all, is still with us now: there was already news from Ukraine, there was news from Iraq. Pope Francis had not yet said, let us say, that Even today, after the second failure of another world war, perhaps we can speak of a third war being fought ‘piecemeal’, with crimes, massacres, destruction..., whereas he did say it a short while ago. And so, clearly, it is increasingly timely to ask how—there, do I have to repeat the first part? No, it was only a way of introducing the subject anyway; this is fine too. How do wars break out? In fact, I think what I am going to tell you is also fairly reassuring, in the sense that it shows that wars break out in situations—the great world wars break out in situations—quite different from the one we are experiencing now. Today we are experiencing a disastrous international situation, full of suffering, full of uncertainties, but still quite different from the scenarios that were taking shape in Europe, for example in the Europe of 1914, which is where we begin this evening.

How did the First World War break out? Well, deep down we all know: it began with an assassination, the Sarajevo assassination. On 28 June 1914, a group of Serbian terrorists killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of the Austrian Empire. So the first important thing to say about the outbreak of the First World War is that it arose from an act of terrorism. And terrorism, then as now, provoked visceral reactions in public opinion and therefore in governments as well. To us today, in considering an act of terrorism, it may seem that killing an archduke is perhaps, all things considered, a little less serious than, say, bringing down two skyscrapers with 3,000 people inside. Instead, one thing we must understand very clearly if we are to grasp what the people of 1914 were like is that, for them—at least for the politicians of the time—killing an archduke who was heir to the imperial throne was far more serious than bringing down two skyscrapers would have been, if they could ever have imagined such a thing.

Just think: the Austro-Hungarian Empire was a great power. It was still a great power—weakened, in crisis—but it was one of the few great powers in the world. Emperor Franz Joseph ruled the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Emperor Franz Joseph was eighty-four years old, which meant he was going to die very soon. No one knew when, but he was certain to die very soon. Franz Joseph had reigned over the Austrian Empire since time immemorial; he had reigned for sixty-six years. He would die very soon, everyone knew that. In fact, he would then die in December 1916, a year and a half later; we know that. And when Franz Joseph died, Archduke Franz Ferdinand would become the sovereign of one of the world's great powers for the rest of his life. You understand that killing him changed the situation somewhat. It was not like killing a president of the United States, because another one is chosen four years later anyway. Here they were killing someone who would have helped govern the world for the rest of his life. So the assassination was extremely serious: an act of terrorism of enormous gravity, with complicity abroad.

Here we must remember how—I will spend the whole evening telling you, “We must remember this,” and you will forgive me—the way a war breaks out is not so simple. There is a whole series of connections. The first, the first point to bring into focus: Sarajevo. Why Sarajevo? Sarajevo is in Bosnia, and Bosnia was then, just as it has been in our own time, a sensitive place. Bosnia was also then a place with an ethnic mixture, with hostile ethnic groups. Bosnia had been occupied by Austria-Hungary for some years, and some of its inhabitants were happy about it. The Croat population was happy, for example. The Bosnian Muslim population was very happy to belong to the Austrian Empire. Indeed, Bosnian Muslim soldiers would fight faithfully for the Empire in the First World War, including in Italy—those Muslim regiments wearing fezzes.

But in Serbia—in Serbia, in Bosnia—there was a proportion of the Serbian population that was not at all happy to belong to Austria. Because right next door was Serbia, the Kingdom of Serbia: independent Serbia, small but bellicose, a battle-hardened state with military traditions and grand dreams of expansion. The Bosnian Serbs suffered under Austrian imperial rule. And so there was discontent, and there was terrorism. And the Austrians were certain that this terrorism was backed, supported, and organized in Serbia; that this assassination, in which the Archduke and his wife had been killed, had been organized in Serbia with complicity within the Serbian government and the Serbian army. The Austrians were certain of it from the very first day. Among other things, it was true: the investigations would bring it to light; there is no doubt about it. There was such complicity in Serbia.

And so Austria was determined to punish Serbia. And here you really must make the effort to think of them not as Austria and Serbia, forgotten states as they were then—who remembers anymore? No. Here there is a rogue state that organizes terrorism abroad, and there is the country that suffered the terrorist attack, which is determined to punish the rogue state. In Austria there was a wave of mass indignation. Crowds were in the streets shouting, “Serbia must die.” In German it even has assonance: *Serbien muss sterben*.

## 2. Alliances, German power, and the blank cheque

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00124–U-00274 -->

But there was a problem: Serbia had a great friend, a great protector—Russia. The Russia of Tsar Nicholas II. The friendship between Serbia and Russia was something—it was an obvious fact of European geography; it is true even today. Russia was a Slavic, Orthodox power; the Serbs were Slavic and Orthodox. Even during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, Russia backed Serbia.

Let us broaden the discussion for a moment. Serbia had a great friend, Russia. Everyone in that world of 1914 tried to have friends and allies. The weight of alliances is a fundamental factor in understanding how the world slid into world war, because that was not a world of blocs. Well, ours is no longer one either by now—only the American bloc remains—but in any case we all remember when our world was divided into two blocs and nothing happened outside those two blocs. The world of 1914 was not like that. It was a world of great and small powers. Which were the great powers? Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and then Italy is also usually included as a courtesy, the last of the great powers with a little effort. And then there were the small powers, but they too were bellicose, nationalist, and battle-hardened: Serbia, Romania, Belgium. They were not divided into blocs; each looked after itself.

This was the age in which nationalism was at its height. Every country was convinced that it had a duty to look after itself. It is what an Italian politician, Prime Minister Salandra, in those very days would call “we need a spirit free from every preconception, every prejudice, and every feeling except unlimited and exclusive devotion to our homeland—the sacred self-interest of Italy.” But if one had to look after oneself, one also had to try to have allies, because alone one came to a bad end. So each country sought alliances for its own security. Serbia was allied with Russia.

The Austrians thought that, well, the world would understand: Serbia was clearly a rogue state that had organized terrorism; it had to be punished for everyone's security. But they began to wonder whether, if Austria attacked Serbia, the Russians might intervene. Yet Austria too had a powerful friend, more powerful than itself, because Austria was a great empire in decline—all things considered, almost everyone suspected it was in decline—whereas Austria had an immensely powerful friend that was becoming the greatest power in the world: Germany. The Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm—you remember Wilhelm with the great handlebar moustache and the spiked helmet? But beyond the Kaiser, who was a somewhat comical figure, Germany was a very great power. It had the strongest army in the world and was building the strongest industry in the world. In fact, Germany had already become the greatest industrial power; it had already overtaken Britain.

Austria was allied with Germany in an alliance that bound the two countries to help one another if either was attacked. To tell the truth, the alliance also included Italy. It was called the Triple Alliance for a reason. But during those days of crisis in 1914 it was perfectly clear that no one had any faith in Italy. The Austrians did not believe that, if they went to war, Italy would come to their aid. They ruled it out, excluding the whole idea from the outset, and in fact they were right. In the name of sacred egoism, Italy would decide to stay out for the time being.

But there was Germany. Austria had enough good sense to know that, before doing anything against Serbia, it had to ask what the Germans thought. Franz Ferdinand's funeral took place on 3 July. The following day, 4 July, the Emperor, the old Emperor Franz Joseph, wrote to Kaiser Wilhelm. Now, this letter from the Emperor of Austria is very interesting. It is not precise or lucid; it does not say, “This is what we want to do.” It is a letter more or less along these lines. Franz Joseph says to Wilhelm: “The attack directed against my nephew is the direct consequence of the agitation carried on by the Russian and Serbian Pan-Slavists, whose sole aim is to weaken the Triple Alliance and destroy my empire. This, however, will be possible only when Serbia, which currently forms the pivot of Pan-Slav policy, is eliminated as a political power in the Balkans. You too, after the recent terrible events in Bosnia, will be convinced that reconciliation of the conflict that separates us from Serbia can no longer be contemplated, and that the peace-preserving policy of all the European monarchs will remain threatened as long as this hotbed of criminal agitation in Belgrade continues unpunished.” This is a letter that makes me think of the reasons with which the Western powers went into Iraq or Afghanistan after September 11. In other words, something must be done. There is no lucid reasoning about the consequences, no consideration of what today is called an exit strategy. “Don't you agree that something must be done?”

Fortunately for Austria, Kaiser Wilhelm agreed. When news of the Sarajevo assassination reached him, Kaiser Wilhelm noted on the telegram that “It was high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.”. Consequently, on 5 July Kaiser Wilhelm replied to his Austrian counterpart, telling him: “Go ahead, do not worry. If Russia should interfere, Germany stands with you.” But the Kaiser also added: “Really, do not worry. The Russians will not have the courage to interfere. We are certain Russia will stay out.” That was the first gamble. This evening's story is a story of one gamble after another, all of them wrong. It is as though—as we shall see—wars break out not because someone wants them. No one ever wants war, at least not world war, general war; no one ever wants that. Wars break out because someone makes the wrong gamble.

I want to pause for another moment over this assurance Kaiser Wilhelm sent to his Austrian counterpart: “Go ahead, we are with you, do not worry.” It is a reassurance that historians later called the blank cheque. How was this blank cheque given? Germany was already a parliamentary country; it had political parties, a parliament, free elections. The Kaiser consulted no one. Before consulting anyone at all, he himself wrote to his counterpart, the Emperor of Austria, telling him: “Go ahead, don't worry.” Then the Kaiser did consult someone. Whom? Parliament? Not a chance. He consulted the Chancellor—the prime minister, that is. He consulted the commander of the army, General von Moltke, and General von Moltke told him, “If war breaks out, we are ready.” And then—this is interesting—whom else did Kaiser Wilhelm consult? He consulted Alfred Krupp, the great arms industrialist. Krupp was the great—the largest arms factory in the world. The Kaiser felt the need to consult Alfred Krupp. If war should ever break out, were the Krupp works ready? And Alfred Krupp reassured him: the Krupp works were ready.

But they were certain that war would not break out. Indeed, after sending the blank cheque to Vienna, the Kaiser boarded his yacht and went on a cruise in the Baltic. It was July, people wanted to take their holidays, and the Kaiser went on holiday too. This German gamble—that yes, there was a risk of war, but you would see that war would not come—is a crucial aspect of the argument we must discuss this evening, so I must dwell on it a little longer.

The gamble was even greater than it appeared because of the question of alliances, since Russia was not the only party involved. Russia was Serbia's great friend, but Russia had an alliance with France. Politically speaking it was an unnatural alliance, if you think about it: France, the secular, anticlerical, radical republic, and the Tsar's empire. The Orthodox empire, the most absolutist and reactionary state on the continent. It was an unnatural alliance, and yet France and Russia had been allies for twenty years. Why? One need only look at the map—the map of that time. Today Germany seems to us a great power. The Germany of that time was much larger. Poland did not exist; Czechoslovakia did not exist; the Baltic states did not exist. There was only Germany and then, at a certain point, Russia. On the map of Europe in 1914, Germany stood in the middle, sharing borders with France and Russia. And so it is clear that France and Russia had every interest in being allies.

And when the Germans gambled that there would be no war—that there would be no world war; the small war between Austria and Serbia, yes, certainly, that was perfectly fine, it was necessary—but when they gambled that there would be no war, the Germans were gambling that there would be no war against Russia and France, because that was the risk. Why did they take that risk?

## 3. Armed peace, military planning, and the shrinking window

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00275–U-00469 -->

The second part of this talk, of this chat, will be a kind of countdown through the final days, but first we have to sketch a broader picture, get you inside the heads of the people governing the world at that moment. What was the world like, what was Europe like at that moment? The Europe of 1914 was experiencing a period of great prosperity, of great economic expansion. After the decades of economic crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, the economy had started moving again; everyone was growing. There was even a kind of globalization avant la lettre. Well, today it is very easy to find globalizations in the past; I have seen every kind of them. Even the Roman Empire was a globalization. But in any case this one in 1914 had the features of globalization: the economies were interdependent, the stock exchanges were interdependent, the currencies were linked, anchored to gold. The Italian lira was worth exactly as much as the French franc. You could travel throughout Europe without a passport, except in Russia. You needed a passport to enter Russia. But otherwise this Europe was so interdependent that it seemed incredible that a war could break out among these powers. And yet at the same time all these countries, Italy included, were armed to the teeth. They all had military spending at a level comparable to American military spending today. They spent enormous sums on the army, on the navy, and why did they do it? They did it because they were afraid of one another. An important aspect in understanding the mentality of the ruling classes of that era is that they were all steeped in a kind of Darwinian ideology, but a very simplified one, right? The survival of the fittest, the struggle for survival, the survival of the strongest. They were convinced that states, too, were struggling with one another in this way. The strongest would triumph, the weakest would be eaten. And because they were convinced of this, everyone wanted to be strong and they all armed themselves in order to survive. No one imagined that they themselves would be the aggressor in the future; it was always the others who were the aggressors. We are threatened, we must be strong, we must have a great army, and since we are spending an enormous amount of money on a great army, well, we have to expect that perhaps sooner or later we will use it. It cannot be ruled out; in fact it is quite likely, some said. Those years—it is striking, because in those years books came out that talked quite calmly about the next war. It was a foreseeable scenario, part of the horizon. In 1911 a book came out in Germany by a German general, General von Bernhardi, entitled *Germany and the Next War*. Three years remained before the First World War. In this book there were chapters entitled “CHAPTER I THE RIGHT TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER II THE DUTY TO MAKE WAR; CHAPTER V WORLD POWER OR DOWNFALL.” I have given a German example because German generals were particularly inclined to state these arguments explicitly, but in reality similar arguments were made in every country. And of course not everyone reasoned that way. There were also lucid people who said: look, with the interdependent economies we have, a war among the great powers would be a catastrophe. Let me mention another book that came out in those years. These are the books that prime ministers and army commanders read; they kept them on their bedside tables. In 1910 a book came out by an English writer, Norman Angell, entitled *The Great Illusion*. Do you remember the film *The Grand Illusion*? Well, it later copied its title from this book, which was very famous in its day. What was the great illusion? The author said that the great illusion was precisely that of our great powers, which believed that having a great army and preparing to win the next war was a guarantee. It was not. We will all lose the next war, this author said. The next war will destroy even the victors; it is an illusion to believe that strength will save us. Now, books like this circulated, politicians read them, and in the Europe of 1914 various politicians had become fairly convinced that a war among the great powers would indeed be a catastrophe. But the paradox was that these politicians were at the head of countries armed to the teeth. The scenario of the next war was so foreseeable that at a certain point this happened. Let me tell you this detail. In London, at a certain point, the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, was speaking with the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky. At a certain point the British foreign secretary Grey said to the German ambassador—listen to this conversation—“But if a European war arose because Austria moved against Serbia, and Russia—compelled by public opinion and wishing not to suffer another humiliation like that of 1909—marched into Galicia, which would oblige us to give assistance, France's participation would be unavoidable and the further consequences foreseeable.”. That is the description of what I am telling you about tonight, the Sarajevo crisis, the crisis of the summer of 1914, except that this conversation took place two years earlier. In December 1912 Grey said these things to the German ambassador. Those were the scenarios in which a war could break out, and there were only a few places. The Balkans were a typical place, with all the unrest there. Now, starting from the fact that the scenario of the next war was foreseeable, a split nevertheless emerged between the politicians and the military, because the politicians said: look, we must do everything we can to avoid this scenario; it would be a catastrophe if it came to pass. But the military had a different job. The military’s job was to say: and what do we do if it happens anyway? If it happens anyway, we must be ready. So in those years of great economic growth the military pressed: let us use all this money, let us use it to become stronger. And the politicians gave in. The years preceding 1914 were years of an arms race in which every country strengthened its army, not because it wanted to attack anyone but because it was concerned about its security. In August 1913, ten months before the Sarajevo assassination, France passed a law under which military service, conscription, was increased from two to three years. Imagine a world in which conscription lasted three years. What did the Germans say when the French parliament passed the law increasing military service? The Germans said: the French want to make war, they are threatening us. It is a very understandable reaction that political scientists have studied. It is a reaction called the security paradox. I want to be safer, so I arm myself. My neighbors become frightened, they feel less safe than before, so they arm themselves too, and so on and so on. The penultimate thing before we reach—return to—Austria, which was deciding what to do against Serbia. The penultimate thing needed to make the picture of that moment clear. The military did not merely ask for more weapons; they also did something else. Their job was to say: if war really happens, we must be ready. Being ready meant making plans. In 1914 the great powers had not fought one another for a very long time. Almost fifty years had passed since the Crimean War, since the Franco-Prussian War. In those fifty years the general staffs had done nothing but prepare plans: if war breaks out—that particular war, against that particular enemy—what do we do? Plans were ready for every possible war, extremely detailed, extremely precise. Every year they reopened them and refined them, adjusted a detail. They had perfected them for fifty years. Plans were ready for everything. An anecdote about this: in November 1913, eight months before the Sarajevo assassination, the king of Belgium was on a state visit to Berlin. There was a reception, and during the reception Kaiser Wilhelm pointed out a general to the king of Belgium and told him, The Emperor introduced General von Kluck to him, saying with a distinctly deliberate emphasis: “This is the general who will be charged with marching on Paris.”. The plans were so complete that everything had already been decided, even who would command each individual army, and so on. Everything was ready, should war come. Finally, one last thing before returning to Vienna, where they were discussing exactly what to demand of the Serbs and how to teach them a good lesson. One last thing: this arms race of those years had another characteristic that made it very dangerous, one that made it possible to predict how the balance of power would evolve. Armaments were slow things. You laid down a battleship and it would be ready in five years; you increased conscription by one year and two years passed before the effects were felt, and so on and so on. So everyone paid close attention: when will we be at our strongest? And when will they? Russia, for example, was militarily weak at that moment after the Russo-Japanese War, but it was making enormous investments. In a few years it would be much stronger. So in this climate, you understand, the military did their job. Their job was to say: if there is a war, we must be ready. And by the way, if there is a war, when would it suit us for it to happen? Would it suit us for it to happen soon, or would it suit us for it to happen later? All military officers made these calculations. And then every so often something changed. In April 1914 it became known that Russia and Britain were negotiating a naval agreement. I have hardly mentioned Britain so far; it had no alliances. I will come back to Britain in a moment, but it was now beginning talks with Russia. You understand what effect this had in Berlin? In Berlin they said: we already have Russia and France allied with each other against us in the next war, when it comes. If Britain were to ally with them too, our position would become worse. These talks between the Russians and the British began in April. In May 1914 the commander of the German army, von Moltke, stated in private, not in public: from this moment on, every day of delay can only diminish our probability of success. A few more weeks: on 1 June 1914 von Moltke declared, “I consider a war inevitable, and the sooner the better.”. Four weeks remained before the Sarajevo assassination.

## 4. Austria's ultimatum and Britain's initial detachment

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00470–U-00587 -->

Did von Moltke organize the Sarajevo assassination? No, obviously. It was the catalyst that brought out all these things that had been simmering there for years. Let us return to Vienna, where they were preparing the ultimatum to send to Serbia. They would send an extremely harsh ultimatum because the Austrians did not want the Serbs to accept it. The Austrians wanted war and they had a blank cheque from Germany. It would be an extremely severe ultimatum. The Germans pressed, the Germans pressed Vienna, saying: hurry up, present the world with a fait accompli; the sooner you act the better; the Russians will not dare to intervene. But Austria was a country where things were done calmly. It was a country where things were done slowly. There were two governments, the Austrian one and the Hungarian one; they had to reach agreement. And then the Austrian army was a slow, backward army; it took a long time to prepare. What is more, consider something no one today would think of: it was July, harvest time. The soldiers were all at home on leave—Austria was an agricultural country—to harvest the grain. It took time. The Austrians were in no hurry; in Berlin they were champing at the bit: we are always being dragged along behind this backward country. Finally, on 23 July, almost a month later, the Austrian ultimatum was ready. It was delivered to the Serbian government. It was an extremely harsh ultimatum. The Austrians said to Serbia: the Serbian government has done nothing to prevent the proliferation of terrorism; Serbian officers and officials are involved in the Sarajevo assassination. Therefore we Austrians want the Serbian government to publish a declaration in which it dissociates itself from every form of hostility toward Austria. The Serbian government must close all the newspapers and dissolve all the associations that sow hatred against Austria. The Serbian government must dismiss all public officials and officers who are in some way involved with forms of hostility toward Austria. It must have these people arrested—the Austrians presented the list—and it must open investigations into terrorism on Serbian territory in which Austrian investigators will take part. On 24 July the capitals of the whole world awoke to the news of this ultimatum, and the ultimatum was received very badly. In St. Petersburg the Russian foreign minister summoned the Austrian ambassador and told him: but do you want to make war on Serbia? Are the Germans inciting you? “You are setting fire to Europe. You are taking a grave responsibility upon yourself.” That is number one: for the Russians the responsibility lay with Vienna and the Austrians. We will see a whole series of these phrases, “you are the ones assuming responsibility.” This is only the first. In London too, the foreign secretary Grey was aghast. He called the German ambassador Lichnowsky and told him: I have never seen one state send another state a document like this. It is impossible for the Serbs to accept it. Here there is a small period detail, right, to give a sense of the era. Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador, said: “In response to my observation that the Balkan peoples ought not to be judged by the same standard as European civilized peoples … [Wilhelm II's marginal annotation:] Correct; they simply aren't!” Now we are in London, then, in this exchange between Grey and Lichnowsky. What was happening in London? In London the German gamble did indeed appear to be holding for the moment. British public opinion did not give the slightest damn about Serbia. Britain had alliances with no one. It had a friendship with France that had lasted for years, this must be said. British and French military officers were accustomed to consulting each other; they had even prepared plans together, they had become fairly friendly. But there were no obligations, there were no treaties. And Britain was an isolationist country at that moment. Britain was a country that dominated the world—India, Africa, the colonies, trade, the stock exchange. It did not give a damn about minor continental disputes, and especially about the Balkans. British newspapers wrote in those days: why should we let ourselves be dragged into someone else’s war? There was a Manchester newspaper that published an article whose theme was: “We care as little for Belgrade as Belgrade does for Manchester.” The proof that until that moment Britain was truly outside, outside these thoughts, is in the minutes of the British government, of cabinet meetings. From the day of the Sarajevo assassination, 28 June, to the day when the whole world was thrown into an uproar because the Austrians had delivered the ultimatum to Serbia, 24 July, for almost a month the British government never discussed foreign policy. They had problems of their own. They had Ireland, which was not yet independent, where there was terrorism, there was everything, a revolution about to break out. Foreign policy did not exist for the British. There were only some people in Britain who reasoned differently. There were some officials in the Foreign Office; we know from the notes they exchanged in those days. There were some officials in the Foreign Office who were instead afraid of what was happening. And again, the security paradox. We are fairly safe; Germany is becoming very strong. The Germans say they are doing it only because they are afraid of us, but if they become too strong, then—some senior Foreign Office official in London said in those days—we must stand with the French and the Russians, because this struggle is not for the possession of Serbia but between a Germany that aspires to political dictatorship in Europe and the powers that want to remain free. And then there were people in Britain who thought that way, and they were the military officers. By then the military were accustomed to considering themselves friends of the French, to doing things together with them. They knew it would be difficult for Britain to enter the war alongside France because public opinion did not want it, and for years they had been brooding over this matter. Here too it is striking to see how, for years and years, they had thought and discussed and reasoned among themselves: when the next war comes, what do we do? There is a revealing anecdote, from 1910. An English general who was very friendly with the French, Wilson, was talking with a French general, Foch, and Wilson said to him: well, if war breaks out, assuming that we come to help you—I hope so, but who knows, Wilson said—what would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could really help France? We are four years earlier, in 1910. What would be the minimum number of British soldiers who could help France? And Foch replied: “A single British soldier—and we will see to it that he is killed.” Because what matters is that British public opinion should be mobilized for a cause. It is almost midnight; let us begin the countdown. On 25 July the Serbian reply to the ultimatum arrived.

## 5. Serbia's reply, Churchill's fleet, and mobilization logic

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00588–U-00679 -->

The Serbs accept almost all the conditions. In the meantime, however, Serbia appeals to the tsar to make sure that the Russian tsar will support them if they are attacked. The tsar replies to the Serbs: you can count on us. That day the tsar makes a symbolic decision—symbolic things matter in politics too. All the officer cadets in their final year who are in Russia are commissioned as officers on the same day, at the same hour: the hour when the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia expires. It could not be any clearer than that.

July 26: Austria rejects the Serbian reply. Having rejected the reply means it will go to war. That day something happens in London. It is a detail—I have filled this lecture with details, which means I will run late and you will have to be patient; I did not have the courage to cut many things. This detail seems to me, it seems to me, in its own way another curious detail. On the day Austria rejects the Serbian reply, the English navy minister, the First Lord of the Admiralty, makes a decision on his own initiative. The English war fleet is engaged in major maneuvers; it is all gathered together with full crews. The major maneuvers end tomorrow, after which the squadrons will each return to their own port and the sailors will go on leave. The First Lord of the Admiralty, on his own initiative, gives the order: tomorrow the fleet is not to disperse, it is to remain all together and move to the naval base at Scapa Flow in the Orkneys, where it will be safe.

I am telling you this also because the First Lord of the Admiralty in 1914 is Winston Churchill, whom we are accustomed to associating more with the Second World War, and he was still alive when I was a child, and many of you, well, we remember him. Yet few people remember that Churchill was precocious; he was a precocious young man and was already navy minister in 1914. He is the one who decides that the war fleet will not disperse but remain ready and, indeed, take shelter in the naval base. The next day the German stock market collapses. The news from London is enough to raise doubts. Perhaps England will not stay out after all.

There is still one thing I have to explain to you, to explain what constraints are acting upon these soldiers and these politicians. It is the last thing, I hope, that interrupts our countdown. At this point war is certain—at least between Austria and Serbia it is certain; then we will see. To wage war, the first thing these countries must do is order mobilization. What does that mean? They have conscript armies, which means that the country is full of young men and men who did their military service in previous years and who can always be called back if war breaks out—or rather, who are certain to be called back. If war breaks out, the army must immediately be expanded, doubled, tripled, by calling up everyone who is at home. This is mobilization.

It is an extremely complicated thing because, as you understand, it means calling up millions of men, putting them in uniform, arming them, organizing the units, and putting them on trains that will take them to the frontier. All of it according to plans, themselves calculated down to the smallest detail. German mobilization, calculated to the very minute and second, calls for the simultaneous movement of 11,000 trains. The Germans are accustomed to doing these things well; everything is scheduled to the minute. Mobilization, as you understand, is an enormously consequential decision. Among other things it costs a tremendous amount of money, so no one does it unless the situation has really become very serious.

But there are differences from one country to another. There are countries that are slow to mobilize. One at random: Russia. Russia, which is immense, has few railways. Russia takes months to mobilize its army. So if the situation becomes serious, Russia has to mobilize somewhat quickly; it cannot afford to wait. Indeed, the first country that begins talking about mobilization on July 26 is not Austria, which is about to wage war on Serbia—but Serbia is small, there is no need to mobilize the whole army against Serbia. The first country that begins talking about mobilization is Russia, which knows it will take a long time.

On the other hand, there is a country in Europe—now let us see whether you can guess which one—that is certain it can mobilize extremely quickly. Its plans are extremely precise, so much so that they have already decided that if they mobilize, it is because there is a war. There is no interval, no distinction between the two decisions. If they mobilize, they might as well go to war immediately, immediately, immediately. Has everyone understood? It is the Germans.

So on that same July 26 when Austria rejects Serbia's very moderate reply to the ultimatum, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg warns the Russian government and tells it: “Preparatory military measures by Russia would compel us to take countermeasures. Mobilization, however, means war.” But we cannot believe that Russia wants to unleash such a European war. And two: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the Russians; it is the Russians who must decide to take a step back and not mobilize.

## 6. Austria declares war and the cousins exchange telegrams

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00680–U-00759 -->

July 28, a twist: the Kaiser returns from his cruise and they have him read the Serbian reply to the Austrian ultimatum, and the Kaiser says, but excellent, it is everything we wanted, even more than that. The Serbian reply removes every reason for war. The Kaiser's joy when he discovers this is the first sign that the Germans, even though General von Moltke had said two months earlier, now we are ready—well, it is one thing to say it in theory; now that it is really happening, signs begin to appear that even the Germans are not so sure they want to fight this war after all. The Kaiser is happy: the Serbian reply puts everything right, no war. Too bad that on that very day Austria declares war on Serbia. The Germans had done nothing but insist—once again, we have to understand the people of that time. There is such popular mobilization in Austria. People are convinced: let's go punish these terrorists who threaten the world. There is such a—I'll give you an example—Sigmund Freud. Freud is Austrian, lives in Vienna, is fifty-eight years old, already a mature man who has lived his whole life in the Austrian Empire. When Austria declares war on Serbia, Freud says—writes, I no longer remember, but in any case it is established—for the first time in thirty years I feel truly Austrian. For the first time I think that perhaps this empire has a future. And then this really is a quotation: “My entire libido belongs to Austria-Hungary.” It was July 28.

On July 29 Russia begins mobilization preparations, which is exactly the thing the Germans had told them: don't do it, because otherwise it means war. Russia mobilizes because it is afraid, and the Russians would not want to go to war either, as we discover from another episode that begins on this July 29, which is very curious and is itself typical of this strange age, because the Europe of these years is truly strange, suspended between modernity—liberal parliamentary democracy—and the archaism of these dynastic empires where emperors and kings still have power. On July 29 begins the story of the telegrams from Nicky to Willy and from Willy to Nicky. It begins with a telegram from the Russian tsar Nicholas, who on that July 29 sends a telegram to Kaiser Wilhelm, against whom he is about to mobilize the army. Tsar Nicholas sends a telegram to the Kaiser in English. This too is very significant; it gives you an idea of what England has now become. Fifty years earlier, if a tsar had to write to a king of Prussia, he wrote to him in French; now instead they speak and write to one another in English.

To appreciate the situation, bear in mind that Tsar Nicholas, Emperor Wilhelm, and King George of England are first cousins. They are first cousins, they are all grandsons of Queen Victoria—you remember the nineteenth-century Queen Victoria, who died in nineteen hundred and something—in short, they are all her little grandsons, they are first cousins, they address one another informally, they call one another by their nicknames. So on July 29 the Russian tsar sends the Kaiser a telegram: I am glad you have returned from your cruise. The moment is serious and I need your help. “An ignoble war has been declared to a weak country. I beg you in the name of our old friendship to do what you can to stop your allies from going too far.” Signed, Nicky. The Kaiser replies the same day in English, saying: but look at it from the other point of view, Serbia is a terrorist state, Austria has the right, and so on; come on, you Russians have to understand, you take a step back. Signed, Willy. They write to one another for four days, a dozen telegrams from Nicky to Willy and Willy to Nicky, until August 1, when Willy declares war on Nicky. In any event, let us not get ahead of ourselves; we still have precisely four days to deal with.

July 29: the Germans are furious with these stupid Russians who have not understood that by mobilizing they are causing the war. General von Moltke: Russia has sided with a criminal country, a den of terrorists. “Then the mutual destruction of the civilized states of Europe can begin.” A war—this is still General von Moltke—that will annihilate the civilization of almost all Europe for decades. Yet this war must be fought anyway, because no one wants to back down. Here too there emerges, how can I put it, an almost schizophrenic trait in this ruling class, which understands perfectly clearly that this war will be a catastrophe, but at the same time cannot back down and lose face. Let the others take a step back. Not us. At this point, a further split opens in Germany between the military and the politicians.

## 7. German war plans, Belgian neutrality, and Britain's warning

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00760–U-00859 -->

The military say war is inevitable, so let us fight it immediately, let us attack, give us the order to mobilize. The politicians instead say, wait a moment, because from the politicians' point of view it is essential to show that Germany is not to blame for the war: it is entirely the fault of the Russians and the French, not Germany. So why do the German politicians care so much about this? For many reasons, perhaps also to save face, out of pride, out of a sense of honor, but then they care because of German public opinion. Public opinion matters in Germany too; there is a huge socialist party in Germany. What will the socialists say? If people think we have been attacked, then there will be no problems—and in fact that is exactly what will happen. Germany delays mobilization, and the German population will go to war convinced that innocent Germany has been attacked and therefore must be defended.

And then they must prevent England from entering the war. If England sees that Germany is doing everything it can to avoid it, perhaps it will stay out of the war. After all, the English, well, it has become clear, would like to stay out if only they can. But there is another problem that makes it very difficult for the Germans to keep England out of the war. It is the last problem, so to speak, that I pull out of my hat: Belgium. You will say, what does Belgium have to do with it? It does. Belgium is a small neutral country that enjoys a guarantee signed by all the great powers, including Germany and England, guaranteeing that Belgium will always be neutral. And this is something the English care a great deal about, because in their vision of Europe, Belgium, with its ports facing the Channel—Antwerp—is a fundamental link for England. We English might quarrel with France, it does not matter; as long as Belgium exists, we have a link with Europe. So the English do not care at all about Serbia, but they certainly would care about Belgium.

And why on earth should the Germans be concerned about Belgium? Because of the plans. Because the German general staff has a perfect plan for fighting a war against France and Russia and winning it in a few weeks. Everything has been calculated. The Russians are slow, so we send the entire army, the famous 11,000 trains, against France. We invade it and finish the war in a few weeks. But in order to invade France and outflank the French army—forgive me for going into these strategic considerations—the German military have decided that they must pass through Belgium. It is the famous Schlieffen Plan, which some of you may remember, perfected over years and years. The German military say: if you want us to win the war for you, this is the plan, we have to pass through Belgium; it is up to you politicians to work out how to manage the matter.

On July 29, the Kaiser and the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg realize that if the gamble is that England stays out of the war, they must nevertheless work out what to do about this matter of Belgium, because we Germans will invade Belgium; it has already been decided. So on July 29 the Kaiser and the chancellor make a crucial decision, which will later be judged unanimously the stupidest decision the Germans made in those days. They write to London, to the English government, giving guarantees. First: if in this war there is a war against France, the English can rest easy, Germany has no territorial ambitions. We do not want to take pieces of France, and therefore we do not want to upset the European balance. Second: the Germans assure the English government that if by chance during the war they should be forced to invade Belgium, they will nevertheless evacuate it at the end of the war. And if the Belgians behave themselves and do not prove hostile, they will even pay them damages. And finally the Germans write to London that, after all, let us think about it carefully, because we Germans and you English could still come to an agreement; together we are very strong, we can divide up the world. What do you care about being friends with France? What do you care about Belgium? Let us divide the world between us.

In London they see this message arrive and are stunned. A senior Foreign Office official comments: “The only comment that need be made on these astounding proposals is that they reflect discredit on the statesman who makes them.” On top of that, the English say, what, they are telling us that they are going to invade Belgium; they have actually put it in writing. Grey calls the German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, and tells him: look, if war breaks out, we cannot stay out, and at that point we too will enter the war alongside our friends. Grey also says something that everyone is repeating almost obsessively in those days: “if war did break out, it would be the greatest catastrophe that the world had ever seen” But England cannot make this agreement with Germany; betraying its friends would be an indelible disgrace, so think very carefully in Berlin. And three: for the English, responsibility for everything lies in Berlin. Berlin is where they must take a step back.

At this news from London, the German reaction is uncontrollable. The Kaiser writes on the communication: “Aha! The vile deceiver! Vile blackguard! I will never make a naval agreement with such scoundrels! The vile shopkeeper rabble tried to deceive us with dinners and speeches. But he avoids saying the word and threatens us instead! Vile blackguard! England alone bears responsibility for war and peace, no longer we! That must also be made clear publicly.” And four: for the Germans, responsibility lies with the English. That same day, July 29, the German ambassador in Brussels, von Below-Saleske, receives a sealed packet from Berlin with instructions not to open it until further telegraphic communications arrive. Another useless detail—we are nearly there, I will inflict it on you anyway. Von Below-Saleske is a career diplomat who is in the habit of joking that he brings bad luck. He was in China, and the Boxer Rebellion broke out. He was in Turkey, and the Young Turk Revolution broke out. But then he says, now, though, I can relax: I am in Belgium, nothing will ever happen in Brussels. Let us pick up the pace or I will keep you here all night.

## 8. 30 July: mobilization pressure and political collapse

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00860–U-00890 -->

During the night between July 29 and 30, they do not go to sleep in Berlin, because the news from London has thrown them into a panic. It is becoming apparent that England will enter the war. At three in the morning on July 30, Bethmann Hollweg, the German chancellor, calls Vienna and says, have you understood nothing, do you really want to wage war on Serbia? Take it easy, slow down. Germany does not intend to be dragged into an absurd war because of Austria. Imagine their faces in Vienna, where until the previous day the Germans had been telling them to go ahead. So, and five: for the Germans, responsibility now lies in Vienna.

In the meantime, however, we have now reached July 30. Russia, after finishing the preparations—and the preparations alone already take several days—orders general mobilization on July 30. In Berlin the military go to the government and say, let us mobilize immediately too. The government says, let us wait one more day, because perhaps in Vienna they will manage to come up with something. On this day, July 30, in Berlin the crisis between the military and the politicians comes close—I will not say to a coup d'état, but yes, to high treason. That afternoon an afternoon newspaper comes out—in those days there were afternoon newspapers in Germany—announcing the mobilization order. It is not true. The newspaper is seized, and then it is discovered that the general staff leaked the false news to present the government with a fait accompli. General von Moltke telephones his Austrian counterpart, the Austrian commander Conrad. Austria has not mobilized yet, incidentally, because Austria does things so slowly that it has not mobilized yet. And Moltke says to Conrad: mobilize immediately, because if you mobilize then we will have to mobilize too. In short, the German military do everything they can to force the government to mobilize.

The government is still holding firm, but it is in a state of total panic. That day another characteristic aspect of the communications of those very last days surfaces: the feeling everyone has that something is happening which no one wanted and which can no longer be prevented. At the council of ministers, the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg says: “In conclusion, the Minister-President emphasized that all governments—including Russia's—and the great majority of the peoples were peaceably disposed in themselves, but that direction had been lost and the stone had started rolling. As a politician, however, so long as his démarche in Vienna had not yet been rejected, he had not abandoned his hopes and efforts to preserve peace.” There, that is a fine declaration of the surrender of politics. The stone is rolling and we no longer know what to do. That day the New York Stock Exchange collapses. July 31—there is the time difference—in the morning the news arrives from New York, the London Stock Exchange collapses. They close it at ten in the morning to limit the losses.

## 9. 31 July–1 August: ultimatums, war, and an impossible reversal

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00891–U-00959 -->

Germany still does not mobilize, but it sends Russia an ultimatum: you have begun mobilization; stop it. If you stop it by midnight, fine, otherwise it is war. In London on that July 31, Grey, the foreign secretary, is now convinced that war is inevitable and that England must enter it, but he is in the minority in the government. The government continues to say, this has nothing to do with us. Grey does what he can: he sends a telegram to Paris and one to Berlin asking for a guarantee that Belgian neutrality will be respected. The French reply within an hour; the Germans do not reply.

On August 1 Grey speaks to the government, calls Germany an aggressor as dangerous as Napoleon, and lets it be understood that he will resign if England does not enter the war. But the majority of the ministers are opposed. The majority of the ministers threaten to resign if there is a war. Outside the door the French ambassador, Cambon, is waiting, as he has done throughout all those days. Do you understand the job of the French ambassador in London in those days? To make sure that, since war is about to break out, the English enter the war with us—we are such good friends. Grey is forced to go out and tell Cambon that there is no guarantee, the government is granting nothing. Cambon enters the office of a friend there at the English Foreign Office. His friend describes him as white as a sheet, on the verge of bursting into tears. The French ambassador collapses onto a chair and says to his friend, “They're going to abandon us; they're going to let us down.”

Meanwhile, however, everything is plunging onward. On that August 1 Germany declares war on Russia. It is the first declaration of war between two great powers. Why does Germany declare war? Well, because that is the procedure. We sent the ultimatum, we told the Russians to stop by midnight, the Russians did not stop, therefore we have to declare war. The previous evening the ministers had met at Bethmann Hollweg's house to draft the declaration of war on Russia. The navy minister Tirpitz said, “On 1 August I learned in the Federal Council session that we had followed the ultimatum with a declaration of war on Russia. […] We were to assume the odium of the attacking party because of the jurists of the Foreign Office. […] Presumably we did it out of formal legal conscientiousness.” That is the procedure: you sent the ultimatum, after the ultimatum you must declare war. That same day, August 1 at four in the afternoon, Germany declares war on Russia and orders general mobilization. For them it really is the same thing.

But there is still room for a farce, a grotesque misunderstanding. At five in the afternoon, a telegram arrives in Berlin from the ambassador in London, Prince Lichnowsky. Lichnowsky says: “Sir E. Grey just asked me by telephone whether I believed that I could declare that we would not attack France in a war between Germany and Russia in case France should remain neutral. I declared that I thought I could give that assurance.” The telegram arrives in Berlin and causes pandemonium. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg say, fantastic, we no longer have to fight France and Russia, only Russia. If England and France stay out, we are fine; all we have to do is change the mobilization orders, everything against Russia.

They summon General von Moltke, who commands the army and is organizing the great mobilization. Eleven thousand trains have already begun moving. At that precise moment, the telegram containing the secret orders is arriving at all the stations and all the barracks in Germany. Everything has been set in motion. They summon von Moltke to the imperial palace. I have to tell you this about von Moltke: he is not a great general; they will soon remove him from command. He is there above all because he is the nephew of a great man. In the nineteenth century there had been another von Moltke, a very famous Prussian general who won all his wars. So, the younger von Moltke is there mainly because he is the nephew of that great uncle, but he is a man with rather fragile nerves.

He arrives at the imperial palace and they tell him: look, a telegram from London, England and France will probably stay out, fantastic, change the mobilization orders, everything against Russia. Von Moltke nearly has a fit. He starts to cry and declares that it cannot be done. Everything is already moving: 11,000 trains, millions of people, everything calculated to the second. It cannot be done. They insist. Moltke, who, I repeat, is on the verge of collapse—that evening he will return home and have a heart attack—stands firm and says it cannot be done. The Kaiser tells him: “Your uncle would have given me a different answer!” Moltke is a broken man. In the meantime, however, mobilization cannot be changed, and therefore everything continues according to plan. That evening the Germans invade Luxembourg. The first step toward the invasion of Belgium.

## 10. 2–4 August: Belgium brings Britain into the war

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01015 -->

Before invading Belgium, however, the formalities have to be observed: they must give it the ultimatum. On August 2, von Below-Saleske in Brussels receives the telegram: open the sealed packet, deliver the ultimatum to Belgium while pretending it has just arrived—he has had it there for several days. But the ultimatum to Belgium, what it says to the Belgians, is a heap of lies. Here another aspect begins—I am finishing, but it is the last slightly grotesque aspect of this situation. You have seen how complicated and at the same time terrifyingly simple the path leading to war is. Despite all this, the Germans remain convinced that in order to send someone an ultimatum or declare war on them, one needs pretexts, excuses. And so they tell the Belgians: “The German Government has received reliable information according to which French forces intend to march on the Meuse by way of Givet and Namur.”; we Germans, you understand, must enter Belgium too for our own security, but we would not want the Belgians to interpret it as a hostile act. If Belgium remains neutral and behaves itself, the Germans will evacuate it at the end of the war, we promise. The Belgians decide to resist.

That evening in Berlin, at Bethmann Hollweg's house, they prepare the declaration of war on France. Admiral von Tirpitz says, but why do we have to declare war on France too? Let us wait for them to declare war on us. They explain to him that according to the rules, this is how it must be done. The declaration of war on France too is a heap of lies. French aircraft have flown over Germany, they have dropped bombs, they have killed civilians, and therefore France has put itself at war on its own, and so on. Evidently, even when one practices power politics, there is this need to cling to something for public opinion. Perhaps it reminds me of Colin Powell at the UN with the vial of Saddam Hussein's biological weapons, but anyway, never mind.

That evening, August 2, in London, Grey learns of the German ultimatum to Belgium and finally wrests the mobilization order from the English government. Several ministers resign. That evening an American diplomat in Brussels notes in his diary: “Nobody seems to remember that a few days ago Serbia was playing a star rôle in this affair. She seems to have faded away behind the scenes.”

August 3, we really are at the end. Germany declares war on France. Grey wrests from the House of Commons—finally, it is the first parliament to come into play and do something in this crisis, which has been managed entirely by governments and sovereigns—Grey wrests from the House of Commons authorization to send the Germans an ultimatum. If they invade Belgium, it is war. August 4, the Germans invade Belgium. The English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum to see Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, not knowing that the Germans have already begun the invasion of Belgium that morning.

And the whole affair ends—this story of mine too ends—with two famous phrases, one very famous, the other a little less so. The very famous phrase is the one from the German chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, who, when the English ambassador arrives with the ultimatum, makes a scene and says: “just for a word—'neutrality,' a word which in war time had so often been disregarded—just for a scrap of paper Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation” It is a phrase that has passed into history as the emblem, let us say, of this politics that thinks it can sign treaties and then, well, if it wants, pretend they do not exist—there you have treaties.

The other historic phrase is a little less historic, but anyway it is the Kaiser's. Remember, he was the cousin of the king of England and of the tsar, and they all called one another by nicknames and addressed one another informally. And when it is obvious at this point that there is war against Russia, against France, and also against England, and they are all little grandsons of Queen Victoria, remember? The Kaiser declares: who would have thought that Georgie and Nicky were about to betray me? If my grandmother had been alive, she would never have allowed it.
