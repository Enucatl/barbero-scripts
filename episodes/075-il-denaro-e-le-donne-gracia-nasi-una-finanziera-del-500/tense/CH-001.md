## 1. A woman, a family, many names

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00077 -->

Good evening, everyone. So, tonight we’re going to tell a story: the story of a woman, a complicated story with many intertwined threads. It really is complicated; eventually, you’ll get lost. You’ll confuse one person with another—just know that from the start—but it’s inevitable.

It is a story about money, naturally, because in this series we decide to talk about economics, which in concrete terms means money. In the period we are talking about, the Renaissance, it means, very concretely indeed, sacks of gold ducats. It is a story about family and money. And, as always happens in these cases, it is also a story of conflict. When family, money, and the management of money are involved, clashes and differing opinions about what ought to be done are inevitable. It is the story of a great family capable of protecting its members, but also of dividing amid hatred and resentment.

It is also a story of intolerance and persecution, and of the stubborn resistance of a people, the Jews, in the Mediterranean of the Renaissance. Contrary to what we might expect today, given the way tolerance and intolerance are distributed in the present world, this is a people that then has to face the intolerance of the Christian world and, by contrast, the tolerance of the Muslim world.

It is a story that unfolds between Portugal, Spain, Flanders, Italy, and Constantinople. Italy has a central role and is an extraordinarily contradictory place in that period. It can be a place of welcome, but also—and in reality more often—a place of danger and persecution.

Above all, it is the story of a woman. A woman exceptional for her age, as she would have been in any other, because if today it is not very easy for a female employee of San Paolo to become an executive, as we have heard—even though work is being done in that direction—it was no easier in the past. And yet the dynamics of family, inheritance, marriages, and wills could bring a woman to the head of a family, a company, and commercial dealings on an international scale.

This woman is called—well, she has many names. We’ll call her Gracia Nasi. More correctly, it would be Doña Gracia Nasi, but she is also called Beatriz de Luna. For much of her life she goes by that name, though in the end almost everybody simply calls her the Lady.

This is a story of people who pass from one world to another and from one name to another, people with many names. Nasi is a Hebrew name, a Hebrew word which in the Hebrew of the Bible means leader, prince. Today it is the title of the president of the State of Israel: the *nasi*. A family bearing that surname in the diaspora is obviously a prestigious family, a family of leaders, one the community regards as noble and illustrious. The idea is that those called Nasi descend from King David.
