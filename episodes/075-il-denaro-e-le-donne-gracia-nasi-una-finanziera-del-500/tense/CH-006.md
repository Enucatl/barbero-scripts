## 6. The rescue network and the loan to Charles V

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00601–U-00684 -->

The best route is through Venice. From Antwerp a support network helps converted Jews—who in reality have not converted at all—reach Venice and from there Constantinople. The Jewish community in Antwerp, with its bankers and wealth, tries to assist these compatriots and co-religionists seeking refuge far from Christendom.

Gracia begins to emerge not only as a businesswoman and banker, but as the creator of a network that makes this passage easier. It has to be done as secretly as possible. If a rich Jew informed the Spanish authorities that he wished to leave, they would say: we are delighted that you are going, it was high time—go, but you will not take the money. To take it, he has to leave secretly and perform banking operations. This is where Gracia can help. Banking also means transferring capital through bills of exchange, cheques, and movements between accounts, getting money out from under Emperor Charles V’s nose.

The authorities consequently watch them. Soon after Diogo’s death an investigation begins, and the dead man is accused of having secretly remained a Jew: crypto-Judaism. He is dead, but his widow, sister-in-law, daughter, and niece are alive, and nobody knows what might happen if the inquiry continues.

At this level one also knows how to move. Why have the authorities targeted us? Can we do anything? It immediately emerges that something could be done. Emperor Charles V needs an interest-free loan of 100,000 ducats and has decided that these faithful New Christian subjects will certainly provide it. The Nasi, de Luna–Mendes firm manages to do so.

It is difficult to say what 100,000 ducats means. In a present-day equivalence one might say fifty million euros, which does not seem so very much. But then one can do more with 100,000 ducats than one can now with fifty million euros. One can build a fleet, field an army, and wage a war. Think what kind of world this is: bankers possess sums that states cannot assemble without borrowing from those very bankers. A few years ago we might have called it incredible. Today we are no longer surprised that private finance can command wealth sometimes greater than that of certain states.

They hold out for a few more years. But when the emperor next demands another interest-free loan, the family frankly begins thinking of departure. In 1544 Gracia is thirty-four and fully head of the family. She, her sister, her daughter, and her niece leave Antwerp without announcing they will not return. They are going on holiday, to take the warm baths at Aachen. There are thermal springs there, you know; Charlemagne has gone to live there long before for a reason. He is old and in pain, and the warm baths are just what he needs. Many people go to take the waters.

The family leaves Antwerp for Aachen, then passes into France and goes to Lyon, and from Lyon to Venice.
