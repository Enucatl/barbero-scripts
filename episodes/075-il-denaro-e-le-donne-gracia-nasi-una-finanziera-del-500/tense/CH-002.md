## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

To tell you this story, I shall begin at the moment when I encountered her myself, many years ago, while writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the celebrated Battle of Lepanto: 1571, the great victory of the Catholic fleets, Venetian and Spanish, over the sultan’s fleet. While working on that book and constructing the context of the battle and the war, I came across a figure I had never heard of, though he had been extremely famous in his own day.

He is called Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally he has another name as well: Miques. The Venetians pronounce the Portuguese in their own way—and, as I said, this is a story of people with many names. This Joseph Nasi is the nephew of our Gracia, of Doña Gracia as everybody calls her. He is her nephew and also her son-in-law. These are families inclined to keep everything within the family, marriages included, because if one marries outside, money leaves with the dowries, whereas the money has to remain at home.

I encountered Joseph Nasi because in the years before Lepanto he is famous in Constantinople, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, continually discusses him in his reports. The Venetians have an ambassador in Constantinople who writes long letters to Venice informing the government about events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. One can go and read them; I went and read them. One Venetian obsession is finding out what Joseph Nasi is doing. They had first known him as João Miques, and so the ambassador writes: João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.” Do you understand what “at present calls himself” means? He has resumed his Jewish name. Before, he has one of our names; now that he is among the Turks, who are more tolerant, he has taken back his Jewish name. This is not merely a registry matter: there is an entire judgment contained in the statement that the man we know as João Miques now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians know him well because he has previously lived in Venice and has then been expelled over a strange affair we shall recount. He is banished from Venice and risks his head if he returns. But he does not return. He stays in Constantinople, where he intrigues and makes money, and becomes one of the city’s richest and most influential men.

Marcantonio Barbaro is convinced that this man is so influential because he has learned how to seduce the sultan, who has his weaknesses. The sultan then is the son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman has reigned for many years and is feared throughout the world; then he dies and his son Selim reigns. Sultan Selim is completely different from his father. The Venetian ambassadors say that he is a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It is not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent’s son and then have to take his place. Selim gives audiences in the same palace room where his father has, but does not sit in the same place because he says he is unworthy. He sits farther back, in a corner.

One indication that Selim has a troubled personality is that, by common agreement, he is an alcoholic. Naturally he is the Ottoman sultan and caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople is a metropolis in which half the population consisted of Christians and Jews, wine flowed in rivers, and the Turks are not particularly strict about these prohibitions. In any case, the sultan is not. According to the Venetian ambassadors, he begins the day with a small carafe of spirits at breakfast and continues from there.

Barbaro says that, being a drunkard and also a glutton, Sultan Selim “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—always our Joseph Nasi—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” Thanks to this, says the Venetian ambassador, he wins the sultan’s confidence and incites him against us, against Venice, which these Jews hate. He therefore does everything possible to turn the sultan against us, being the head of his entire nation.

In Venetian eyes Joseph Nasi becomes an almost mythological figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews are spread throughout the world, and their leader therefore has information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence—the English word: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan knows the secrets of Christendom, especially those of Venice.

We should pause over this. You have recognized aspects of something that would return in more recent periods: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. Jews supposedly want to dominate the world; they are everywhere, connected to one another, and control the banks. This obsession is widespread in Renaissance Italy, particularly in Venice. It is a pathology, naturally. They imagine the power of these Jewish bankers to be far greater than it really is.

But it is also a pathology with a motive, because there is ferocious competition between Venetians and Jewish merchants. For the Venetians the Ottoman Empire is an indispensable commercial and financial partner. They have enormous investments there—in Constantinople, but also Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet by law the sultan allows the Venetian unbelievers to operate in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who therefore take a substantial share of the profits. Consequently, the Venetians brood upon the wickedness of these Jews and the danger they represent.

The theme recurs in the letters of Venetian ambassadors from Constantinople. They are struck that Jews who, where still present in Italy, are watched closely and have to be careful not to raise their heads too high, whereas in Constantinople these Jews lord it over everyone. Nobody troubles them in public; the sultan protects them. The ambassadors write: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Joseph Nasi conducts his business. Is it a legend that he supplies the sultan with wine? Perhaps, but it is certain that he holds the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a metropolis where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drink wine every day, the contract to supply wine is clearly substantial. Our young Joseph Nasi is a colossal businessman, but behind him stands his aunt. In the years I mentioned, 1568–70, when I encountered him, she is nearing the end of her life. Sixty is old then, and Joseph, the little nephew, is taking over the family affairs. Until shortly before, however, she has controlled everything.
