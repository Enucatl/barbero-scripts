## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia manages to avert that. Years pass and now they are in Venice, but a rumor immediately circulates among young patricians: there are two Jewesses with two daughters who will inherit a fortune. One is Gracia’s and she keeps her under control, but the other is Brianda’s, and Brianda does not seem entirely sound in the head. Well-bred young men begin buzzing around, thinking that if they could catch the girl—

But that is not the only problem. The spirit of the age is against them. Whenever they arrive somewhere, after a while the age catches up. Christian governments are growing ever more intolerant.

They have been in Venice five years when, in 1550, it orders the expulsion of all *marranos*, all converted Spanish Jews. Evidently many have arrived, not only our little family. They are formally Christians, but the Venetian government could do as it pleases and decides it does not want converts whom nobody trusts. They must all leave.

Gracia has her connections. She remains a banker operating at the highest level, learns the measure is coming, and takes precautions. She finds a place in Italy where Jews could still live quietly: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife Renée of France.

Ferrara is extraordinary because Renée is Protestant. The Reformation has initially made considerable headway in France. Although eventually suppressed, for a long time very many French people—the Huguenots—are Protestant. In 1550 this story is only beginning; Huguenots are not yet persecuted, and some French princes and nobles are Protestant.

Duke Ercole has married a French Protestant princess, and at her court the Inquisition does not interfere. Renée protects Protestants and even receives Calvin, who would have been burned if seized elsewhere in Italy. Jews too are protected and arrive in large numbers.

All of you have had some occasion to notice that Ferrara is a capital of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Until Fascism and the Second World War, Ferrara has a large Jewish community, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there.

The family finds not only asylum. Under Renée’s protection Gracia can do something that would bring her to the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: publicly return to Judaism. She no longer hides, resumes Jewish practice and the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and rapidly becomes one of Ferrara’s most important Jewish figures.

At Ferrara they publish the Hebrew Bible translated into Spanish; she finances it. They make two editions, one dedicated to the duke and one to Gracia Nasi, who has supplied the money. Samuel Usque publishes a Portuguese work there, since so many of these Jews come from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel is afflicted and persecuted, but there is consolation. Here at Ferrara we have found it: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects and assists the Jews and helps them take refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—meaning the Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it could last? It could not. Pressure comes upon Ercole from every direction: the emperor, the pope, everybody. Your wife is a scandal; this must end. The duke resists and resists, but then has to explain to her that a Protestant cannot rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits intervene; Saint Ignatius of Loyola intervenes. Finally Renée surrenders, publicly converts to Catholicism, and abjures Protestantism.

Gracia decides it is time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection these things do not happen. Let us put an end to it once and for all. Very well—
