## 4. Marriage, spices, and inheritance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00389–U-00484 -->

For years they live fairly peacefully in Portugal and do business. When Gracia is eighteen, she marries an extremely rich merchant, Francisco Mendes. Her name changes; now she is Beatriz Mendes, but I’ll spare you that. This rich merchant is naturally also a converted Jew, because people marry only within their own community. He is also a cousin, because when I say within their own community, I mean it literally. They are all Christians only in appearance; they have all remained faithful to Judaism, but in secret. They could not show it.

Why? Because the authorities watch, the neighbors spy, and the Inquisition checks. Do these people observe the feast days? Yes. They observe the Lenten fast, certainly—but they do eat pork, don’t they? If a neighbor notices that one week and then another passes and a particular family never buys any pork, suspicions arise and anonymous denunciations begin, because, as you know, Jews do not eat it.

But they are enormously rich, because in Lisbon in those years fabulous fortunes are made in commerce. Why? Because the Portuguese have discovered the shortest route to the Indies and could import the most precious commodity there is: spices. Spices are all the rage in the market and provoke genuine madness among rich people willing to pay anything for them. We find it a little surprising. Bear in mind that these people have no coffee, no tea, no spirits, no cocaine, and so forth. Spices replace all those things; they are a status symbol.

The Portuguese control the spice trade. Remember studying the geographical explorations at school? Vasco da Gama rounds the Cape of Good Hope and reaches Asia by passing below Africa. Why? To fetch spices. That route saves time, and enormous capital accumulated—capital then used for marriages between cousins.

Marriage between cousins is naturally forbidden. But if one applies to Rome for a license, explains the reasons, and accompanies the application with a sufficient number of bribes, one obtains permission to marry a cousin.

Thus Gracia marries at eighteen. Seven years later her husband dies. At twenty-five she is a widow. Her husband leaves a will establishing that his fortune will henceforth be administered jointly by his brother Diogo and his wife Gracia. That is not automatic. When a woman’s husband dies, she has the right to recover her dowry; naturally that belongs to her and forms the basis of her future survival as a widow. But it is by no means automatic for a husband also to make her heir to the family estate and officially charge her with managing it. This already tells us that Gracia evidently possesses particular qualities. Her husband has recognized them.

At twenty-five, therefore, Gracia administers an enormous commercial fortune together with her brother-in-law, Diogo, her husband’s brother, who represents the firm in Antwerp. Antwerp, on the coast of Flanders, is another place where money circulates in that age, one of the great commercial capitals of the Renaissance world. It is the capital of the Atlantic, perhaps Europe’s greatest commercial port at that moment.

Antwerp is in Flanders but belongs to Charles V, king of Spain and emperor of Germany, and is therefore one of Spain’s great ports. Yet unlike Spanish ports remote from the rest of Europe, it lies close to the German world, Central Europe, the north, and the British Isles. Most Spanish and Portuguese trade with Flanders, Germany, England, and the Baltic passes through Antwerp.

English wool arrives there before being taken to Italy for the Florentine textile industry. Wines arrive from France and Spain and are then traded in England and Germany. Timber comes from Scandinavia for shipyards throughout Europe. Gold and silver arrive too, among the principal commodities carried by the Spanish galleons from the American colonies. All that gold and silver reach Seville and Antwerp, where it is spent. The de Luna–Mendes firm therefore has its head office in Lisbon, but a large branch in Antwerp.
