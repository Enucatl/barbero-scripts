# Money and Women: Gracia Nasi, a Sixteenth-Century Financier — faithful English translation

## 1. A woman, a family, many names

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00077 -->

Good evening, everyone. So, tonight we are going to tell a story: the story of a woman, a complicated story in which many threads are intertwined. It really is complicated; at a certain point you will get lost. You will confuse one person with another—know that from the outset—but it is inevitable.

It is a story about money, naturally, because in this series we decided to talk about economics, which in concrete terms means money. In the period we are talking about, the Renaissance, it means, very concretely indeed, sacks of gold ducats. It is a story about family and money. And, as always happens in these cases, it is also a story of conflict. When family, money, and the management of money are involved, clashes and differing opinions about what ought to be done are inevitable. It is the story of a great family capable of protecting its members, but also of dividing amid hatred and resentment.

It is also a story of intolerance and persecution, and of the stubborn resistance of a people, the Jews, in the Mediterranean of the Renaissance. Contrary to what we might expect today, given the way tolerance and intolerance are distributed in the present world, this was a people that then had to face the intolerance of the Christian world and, by contrast, the tolerance of the Muslim world.

It is a story that unfolds between Portugal, Spain, Flanders, Italy, and Constantinople. Italy has a central role and is an extraordinarily contradictory place in that period. It can be a place of welcome, but also—and in reality more often—a place of danger and persecution.

Above all, it is the story of a woman. A woman exceptional for her age, as she would have been in any other, because if today it is not very easy for a female employee of San Paolo to become an executive, as we have heard—even though work is being done in that direction—it was no easier in the past. And yet the dynamics of family, inheritance, marriages, and wills could bring a woman to the head of a family, a company, and commercial dealings on an international scale.

This woman was called—well, in reality she had many names. We shall call her, with the Italian pronunciation, Gracia Nasi. With the more correct pronunciation it would be Doña Gracia Nasi, but she was also called Beatriz de Luna. For much of her life she was called that, perhaps because in the end almost everybody simply called her the Lady.

This is a story of people who pass from one world to another and from one name to another, people with many names. Nasi is a Hebrew name, a Hebrew word which in the Hebrew of the Bible means leader, prince. Today it is the title of the president of the State of Israel: the *nasi*. A family bearing that surname in the diaspora was obviously a prestigious family, a family of leaders, one the community regarded as noble and illustrious. The idea was that those called Nasi descended from King David.

## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

To tell you this story, I shall begin at the moment when I encountered her myself, many years ago, while writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the celebrated Battle of Lepanto: 1571, the great victory of the Catholic fleets, Venetian and Spanish, over the sultan’s fleet. While working on that book and constructing the context of the battle and the war, I came across a figure I had never heard of, though he had been extremely famous in his own day.

He was called Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally he had another name as well: Miques. The Venetians pronounced the Portuguese in their own way—and, as I said, this is a story of people with many names. This Joseph Nasi is the nephew of our Gracia, of Doña Gracia as everybody called her. He is her nephew and also her son-in-law. These were families inclined to keep everything within the family, marriages included, because if one marries outside, money leaves with the dowries, whereas the money has to remain at home.

I encountered Joseph Nasi because in the years before Lepanto he was famous in Constantinople, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, continually discussed him in his reports. The Venetians had an ambassador in Constantinople who wrote long letters to Venice informing the government about events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. One can go and read them; I went and read them. One Venetian obsession was finding out what Joseph Nasi was doing. They had first known him as João Miques, and so the ambassador writes: João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.” Do you understand what “at present calls himself” means? He had resumed his Jewish name. Before, he had one of our names; now that he was among the Turks, who were more tolerant, he had taken back his Jewish name. This is not merely a registry matter: there is an entire judgment contained in the statement that the man we knew as João Miques now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians knew him well because he had previously lived in Venice and had then been expelled over a strange affair we shall recount. He was banished from Venice and risked his head if he returned. But he did not return. He stayed in Constantinople, where he intrigued and made money, and became one of the city’s richest and most influential men.

Marcantonio Barbaro was convinced that this man was so influential because he had learned how to seduce the sultan, who had his weaknesses. The sultan then was the son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman had reigned for many years and was feared throughout the world; then he died and his son Selim reigned. Sultan Selim was completely different from his father. The Venetian ambassadors say that he was a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It is not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent’s son and then have to take his place. Selim gave audiences in the same palace room where his father had, but did not sit in the same place because he said he was unworthy. He sat farther back, in a corner.

One indication that Selim had a troubled personality is that, by common agreement, he was an alcoholic. Naturally he was the Ottoman sultan and caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople was a metropolis in which half the population consisted of Christians and Jews, wine flowed in rivers, and the Turks were not particularly strict about these prohibitions. In any case, the sultan was not. According to the Venetian ambassadors, he began the day with a small carafe of spirits at breakfast and continued from there.

Barbaro says that, being a drunkard and also a glutton, Sultan Selim “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—always our Joseph Nasi—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” Thanks to this, says the Venetian ambassador, he won the sultan’s confidence and incited him against us, against Venice, which these Jews hate. He therefore did everything possible to turn the sultan against us, being the head of his entire nation.

In Venetian eyes Joseph Nasi became an almost mythological figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews were spread throughout the world, and their leader therefore had information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence—the English word: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan knew the secrets of Christendom, especially those of Venice.

We should pause over this. You have recognized aspects of something that would return in more recent periods: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. Jews supposedly wanted to dominate the world; they were everywhere, connected to one another, and controlled the banks. This obsession was widespread in Renaissance Italy, particularly in Venice. It was a pathology, naturally. They imagined the power of these Jewish bankers to be far greater than it really was.

But it was also a pathology with a motive, because there was ferocious competition between Venetians and Jewish merchants. For the Venetians the Ottoman Empire was an indispensable commercial and financial partner. They had enormous investments there—in Constantinople, but also Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet by law the sultan allowed the Venetian unbelievers to operate in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who therefore took a substantial share of the profits. Consequently, the Venetians brooded upon the wickedness of these Jews and the danger they represented.

The theme recurs in the letters of Venetian ambassadors from Constantinople. They were struck that Jews who, where still present in Italy, were watched closely and had to be careful not to raise their heads too high, whereas in Constantinople these Jews lorded it over everyone. Nobody troubled them in public; the sultan protected them. The ambassadors wrote: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Joseph Nasi conducted his business. Is it a legend that he supplied the sultan with wine? Perhaps, but it is certain that he held the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a metropolis where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drank wine every day, the contract to supply wine was clearly substantial. Our young Joseph Nasi was a colossal businessman, but behind him stood his aunt. In the years I mentioned, 1568–70, when I encountered him, she was nearing the end of her life. Sixty was old then, and Joseph, the little nephew, was taking over the family affairs. Until shortly before, however, she had controlled everything.

## 3. From Spain to Portugal

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00240–U-00388 -->

“A Portuguese woman,” says a European envoy. This one was not Venetian but an envoy of the Fuggers. You know the Fugger bank, another financial colossus of the age, a German bank. They too naturally had envoys in Constantinople, and these men wrote: yes, yes, money circulates here, and it all passes through the hands of this Portuguese woman. To look at her, she resembles a European noblewoman; she dresses like a European princess and lives in the most unrestrained luxury, surrounded by servants.

This is our Gracia Nasi at sixty, in 1568. That is how I encountered her. Then I went back to see what had happened before: how she arrived there, how Gracia arrived, how Joseph arrived, and how this family arrived with its enormous wealth. Where did they come from? From far away. Their family had nothing to do with Constantinople. They came from Europe, from the West, from Spain. They were one of the very many Jewish families that lived in medieval Spain.

That was partly because Spain was ruled by the Arabs during the Middle Ages, when Arabs and Muslims were more tolerant than Christians. Jews were therefore extremely numerous in Arab Spain and remained as the Christians gradually reconquered the country. You know that Spanish history has the epic of the *Reconquista*, which for them is in a certain sense the equivalent of our Risorgimento: it is what one learns at school. The nation was founded in that way, over centuries in which Catholic kings reconquered Spain piece by piece, progressively expelling the Arabs.

The Reconquista ended in a year that seems deliberately chosen as a watershed in history: 1492. I need not tell you what else happened that year, or who left Spain with three caravels. In that same year the Catholic Monarchs ended the last Arab dominion, Granada. From then on Spain was entirely Christian; Muslims were expelled or forced to convert. At that point the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella and Ferdinand, decided to do the same to the Jews. We have purified Spain: there must be no more infidels or unbelievers; there are no more Muslims and there will be no more Jews either.

Think what a year 1492 was: on one side the world opens, while on the other this idea emerges forcefully of a Europe that wants to be pure, free of—well. The Jews must convert or be expelled. Very many resigned themselves to conversion; very many others preferred to leave. Those leaving were told plainly: you may take your possessions, but no money. You cannot take the gold away.

Communities that had lived there for centuries now had to depart. They spent their final night in their cemeteries saying farewell to their ancestors, then left forever. Where did they go? To the Islamic world, the Ottoman Empire, which at that time took for granted that Jews and Christians were inferior to Muslims, but also held—because it is written in the Quran—that if Jews and Christians caused no trouble, they could live among us.

And so Jews departed for the Ottoman Empire: Constantinople, Salonica, Sarajevo, Smyrna, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. All these places were under Ottoman rule and filled with Jews. Sultan Bayezid is said to have remarked: I had not understood that the king of Spain was a fool; clearly he had all these useful subjects and has sent them to me. These Jews were businesspeople, entrepreneurs, bankers. Of course, whether they could bring their capital was another question, though there were ways to bring something out.

Many others naturally remained and converted to Christianity. How many? You should know that this is still a delicate question in Spain, because from that moment the dominant ideology there was *limpieza de sangre*, purity of blood. The poorest Spanish peasant in the sixteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century—and perhaps even today—took pride in saying: I am an Old Christian. I am not like these descendants of Jews or Muslims. To be a *cristiano viejo* meant being proud of one’s pure blood.

So you understand the problem: how many Spaniards actually descend from those converts? Recent genetic research maintains that about twenty percent of today’s Spaniards descend from these Jews and Moors. The research naturally caused a scandal in Spain, because nobody wants to be one of them.

For the extremely rich there was an alternative, however, because for the moment Portugal pursued a more flexible policy. If somebody was a great banker full of money, the kingdom of Portugal allowed him to settle in its territory. That is what the Nasi family did. They moved to Lisbon.

But it did not last. This was a kind of struggle against the spirit of the age. These perpetually fleeing Jews sought a place where they would be treated a little better, or at least tolerated. Yet the spirit of the age progressively led one government after another to decide that it too had to follow Spain and drive the Jews out.

They remained in Lisbon for only five years. In 1497 Gracia had not yet been born; her parents were there. That year the king of Portugal also decided that he wanted a pure country and therefore no Jews: convert or leave. Gracia’s family decided to convert to Christianity. They remained in Lisbon and officially became New Christians, *conversos*. People actually called them by another name, *marranos*. The word rapidly became an insult in Spanish and Portuguese, as it did in Italian—it evokes *The Three Musketeers* for us, I think: “vile marrano!” But *marrano* was originally a technical term meaning a converted Jew, one of the New Christians whom the Old Christians regarded with suspicion and looked down upon.

Naturally they also had to change surname. Enough of the Jewish surname Nasi; they needed a different, Portuguese surname. They chose de Luna. And so in Lisbon, probably around 1510, Gracia was born. Now you understand why she had two names. She was baptized Beatriz, Beatriz de Luna, but the secret name used within the family was Gracia, a Hebrew name—or rather the Spanish translation of the Hebrew name Hannah. In secret, therefore, she was Gracia Nasi, while in the registry she was Beatriz de Luna.

## 4. Marriage, spices, and inheritance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00389–U-00484 -->

For years they lived fairly peacefully in Portugal and did business. When Gracia was eighteen she married an extremely rich merchant, Francisco Mendes. Her name changed; now she was Beatriz Mendes, but I shall spare you that. This rich merchant was naturally also a converted Jew, because one married only among one’s own people. He was also a cousin, because when I say among one’s own people, I mean it literally. In reality they were all Christians only in appearance; they had all remained faithful to Judaism, but in secret. They could not show it.

Why? Because the authorities watched, the neighbors spied, and the Inquisition checked. Do these people observe the feast days? Yes. They observe the Lenten fast, certainly—but they do eat pork, don’t they? If a neighbor notices that one week and then another passes and a particular family never buys any pork, suspicions arise and anonymous denunciations begin, because, as you know, Jews do not eat it.

But they were enormously rich, because in Lisbon in those years fabulous fortunes were made in commerce. Why? Because the Portuguese had discovered the shortest route to the Indies and could import the most precious commodity there was: spices. Spices were all the rage in the market and provoked genuine madness among rich people willing to pay anything for them. We find it a little surprising. Bear in mind that these people had no coffee, no tea, no spirits, no cocaine, and so forth. Spices replaced all those things; they were a status symbol.

The Portuguese controlled the spice trade. Remember studying the geographical explorations at school? Vasco da Gama rounded the Cape of Good Hope and reached Asia by passing below Africa. Why? To fetch spices. That route saved time, and enormous capital accumulated—capital then used for marriages between cousins.

Marriage between cousins was naturally forbidden. But if one applied to Rome for a license, explained the reasons, and accompanied the application with a sufficient number of bribes, one obtained permission to marry a cousin.

Thus Gracia married at eighteen. Seven years later her husband died. At twenty-five she was a widow. Her husband left a will establishing that his fortune would henceforth be administered jointly by his brother Diogo and his wife Gracia. That was not automatic. When a woman’s husband died, she had the right to recover her dowry; naturally that belonged to her and formed the basis of her future survival as a widow. But it was by no means automatic for a husband also to make her heir to the family estate and officially charge her with managing it. This already tells us that Gracia evidently possessed particular qualities. Her husband had recognized them.

At twenty-five, therefore, Gracia administered an enormous commercial fortune together with her brother-in-law, Diogo, her husband’s brother, who represented the firm in Antwerp. Antwerp, on the coast of Flanders, was another place where money circulated in that age, one of the great commercial capitals of the Renaissance world. It was the capital of the Atlantic, perhaps Europe’s greatest commercial port at that moment.

Antwerp was in Flanders but belonged to Charles V, king of Spain and emperor of Germany, and was therefore one of Spain’s great ports. Yet unlike Spanish ports remote from the rest of Europe, it lay close to the German world, Central Europe, the north, and the British Isles. Most Spanish and Portuguese trade with Flanders, Germany, England, and the Baltic passed through Antwerp.

English wool arrived there before being taken to Italy for the Florentine textile industry. Wines arrived from France and Spain and were then traded in England and Germany. Timber came from Scandinavia for shipyards throughout Europe. Gold and silver arrived too, among the principal commodities carried by the Spanish galleons from the American colonies. All that gold and silver reached Seville and Antwerp, where it was spent. The de Luna–Mendes firm therefore had its head office in Lisbon, but a large branch in Antwerp.

## 5. The Inquisition and the move to Antwerp

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00485–U-00600 -->

We said that Gracia was twenty-five; it was 1535. Until then they had lived quietly in Lisbon, provided they pretended to have converted to Christianity. The following year the machinery of persecution began moving again. On 23 May 1536 the pope established the Portuguese Inquisition on the model of the Spanish Inquisition.

What is the Inquisition? In itself it is a tribunal found throughout the Catholic world, run by specialists charged with hunting heretics and false converts. In Spain, however, it was something special. Elsewhere it depended directly on Rome and the pope and had relatively calm and moderate procedures, so to speak: trials were sent to Rome and discussed. Spain had requested a state of emergency. Being full of converted Jews and Moriscos whom the authorities distrusted, it obtained a special Inquisition that answered not to Rome but to the king and therefore operated locally much more swiftly and ruthlessly. The same institution was created in Portugal.

This gave the Inquisition enormous strength. In other Catholic countries it answered to Rome and had to reckon with local authorities. Those authorities might be thoroughly Catholic, but they did not like a tribunal operating in their territory while obeying Rome rather than the local state. They generally created all sorts of difficulties for it. Venice, for example, hated the Jews but disliked the Inquisition too. We Venetian patricians do not like an inquisitor who questions, arrests, and condemns people without answering to the Most Serene Republic, so the Republic played every possible trick on the Inquisition. The Spanish Inquisition, by contrast, depended directly on the king and did not have these problems. The same was true in Portugal.

It was 1536. Within a couple of years Gracia left Lisbon for Antwerp. This too was in the Spanish Empire, but Lisbon, with its newly created Inquisition, had become dangerous for anyone who still did not eat pork and secretly practiced Judaism at home. Gracia left and took everything with her. The firm concentrated in Antwerp. She also brought her younger sister, Brianda. I warned you that at a certain point the characters would multiply.

In Antwerp there was Diogo—remember him?—the brother of Gracia’s dead husband, who managed the company. Now that everybody was reunited, what could be better than a fine marriage? Diogo Mendes married Brianda. Francisco had married Gracia, and his brother Diogo married Gracia’s sister.

Under Sephardic Jewish rules, when a man died his brother ought to marry his widow, especially if there were no children. But Francisco and Gracia already had a daughter, also called Reyna. It was therefore not essential for Diogo to marry his brother’s widow; he could marry the widow’s sister instead, and everything still remained in the family.

There are extraordinary symmetries in this family’s history. Gracia had a daughter by Francisco called Anna—which itself would mean Gracia—but also officially called Reyna. Brianda, who married her brother-in-law Diogo, likewise had a daughter whom she called Gracia within the family, while in the registry she was naturally Beatriz, like her aunt. I assure you that historians too go mad over this, so do not worry too much. But notice the symmetry: two sisters married two brothers, and each couple had a daughter.

Then Francisco died. I do not know when Diogo noticed how striking the asymmetry was, but Diogo died as well. Now there were two widowed sisters, the elder Gracia and the younger Brianda, each with a little daughter. In his will Diogo made his sister-in-law Gracia administrator of the entire family fortune. All of it. Technically, half belonged to Gracia and half to Brianda. The ownership was not in dispute, but Diogo placed administration of the company and its capital entirely in Gracia’s hands.

This is a second piece of evidence that Gracia knew how to conduct business. We can only imagine how Brianda felt. She cannot have been pleased, as we shall soon see. For the moment there were more alarming matters. They were in Antwerp, certainly, but still within the Spanish Empire. And through that great port Jews continually passed from one corner or another of the Christian world, having decided to leave it and reach the Muslim world, Constantinople.

## 6. The rescue network and the loan to Charles V

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00601–U-00684 -->

The best route was through Venice. From Antwerp a support network helped converted Jews—who in reality had not converted at all—reach Venice and from there Constantinople. The Jewish community in Antwerp, with its bankers and wealth, tried to assist these compatriots and co-religionists seeking refuge far from Christendom.

Gracia began to emerge not only as a businesswoman and banker, but as the creator of a network that made this passage easier. It had to be done as secretly as possible. If a rich Jew informed the Spanish authorities that he wished to leave, they would say: we are delighted that you are going, it was high time—go, but you will not take the money. To take it, he had to leave secretly and perform banking operations. This was where Gracia could help. Banking also meant transferring capital through bills of exchange, cheques, and movements between accounts, getting money out from under Emperor Charles V’s nose.

The authorities consequently watched them. Soon after Diogo’s death an investigation began, and the dead man was accused of having secretly remained a Jew: crypto-Judaism. He was dead, but his widow, sister-in-law, daughter, and niece were alive, and nobody knew what might happen if the inquiry continued.

At this level one also knew how to move. Why have the authorities targeted us? Can we do anything? It immediately emerged that something could be done. Emperor Charles V needed an interest-free loan of 100,000 ducats and had decided that these faithful New Christian subjects would certainly provide it. The Nasi, de Luna–Mendes firm managed to do so.

It is difficult to say what 100,000 ducats means. In a present-day equivalence one might say fifty million euros, which does not seem so very much. But then one could do more with 100,000 ducats than one can now with fifty million euros. One could build a fleet, field an army, and wage a war. Think what kind of world this was: bankers possessed sums that states could not assemble without borrowing from those very bankers. A few years ago we might have called it incredible. Today we are no longer surprised that private finance can command wealth sometimes greater than that of certain states.

They held out for a few more years. But when the emperor next demanded another interest-free loan, the family frankly began thinking of departure. In 1544 Gracia was thirty-four and fully head of the family. She, her sister, her daughter, and her niece left Antwerp without announcing they would not return. They were going on holiday, to take the warm baths at Aachen. There are thermal springs there, you know; Charlemagne had gone to live there long before for a reason. He was old and in pain, and the warm baths were just what he needed. Many people went to take the waters.

The family left Antwerp for Aachen, then passed into France and went to Lyon, and from Lyon to Venice.

## 7. Venice and the dispute over the fortune

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00685–U-00760 -->

In Venice they rented a palace on the Grand Canal, just to avoid attracting attention. Venice was naturally full of Jews, but they were shut inside the ghetto, a fairly recent invention. During the Middle Ages Jews had lived wherever they wished, even in Christian cities, but by the Renaissance they had to be watched and separated. Venice had had its ghetto since 1516, and Jews were required to live there.

But our women were not Jews, were they? They were called de Luna, Beatriz, and so forth. They were converted Christians and could live wherever they pleased: in a palace on the Grand Canal.

They had left part of the capital in Antwerp, having failed to remove everything: real estate, palaces, offices, and considerable capital, though they hoped to recover some. They also left the little nephew there. Here João Miques, later Joseph Nasi, enters the story. Through maneuvering, paying bribes, and obtaining recommendations, he recovered part of the capital and transferred it to Venetian banks. The following year, 1546, he too reached Venice.

They were still in a violently antisemitic Christian world, but the Inquisition was less frightening in Venice than in Spain or Portugal, and Turkey was only a step away. One merely had to board a galley and after a few days was under the protection of the Grand Signor, the sultan. One could breathe in Venice—or so Gracia must have thought: perhaps now we can breathe for a moment.

It was then that Brianda challenged Gracia’s management of everything. She had endured it for a long time; now, enough. Venice had courts called the *Giudici del Forestier*, charged with hearing foreigners’ complaints and lawsuits. Before them Brianda objected: half the capital is mine, yet my sister manages all of it. She will not even let me see the accounts, using the will of my late husband, God rest his soul, as a pretext.

Brianda sued and the judges found for her. Gracia had to deposit half the family capital in the Venetian public treasury. For the time being, we shall take it into custody. The judgment said that Brianda’s daughter Beatriz, then only eight, could withdraw that half when she turned eighteen. In essence, the authorities obtained half this immense fortune and would manage it for ten years. When the child was grown, they would see.

Gracia had probably expected something of the kind. There were two women with two daughters. Sooner or later some man would lay hands on the money by marrying one of the girls. Whoever married them would inevitably control it. Gracia had realized this in Antwerp. Her own daughter Beatriz was not yet ten when approaches arrived from nothing less than a prince of the royal house, Don Francisco of Aragon, who let it be known that perhaps a marriage could be discussed.

## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia managed to avert that. Years passed and now they were in Venice, but a rumor immediately circulated among young patricians: there are two Jewesses with two daughters who will inherit a fortune. One is Gracia’s and she keeps her under control, but the other is Brianda’s, and Brianda does not seem entirely sound in the head. Well-bred young men began buzzing around, thinking that if one could catch the girl—

But that was not the only problem. The spirit of the age was against them. Whenever they arrived somewhere, after a while the age caught up. Christian governments were growing ever more intolerant.

They had been in Venice five years when, in 1550, it ordered the expulsion of all *marranos*, all converted Spanish Jews. Evidently many had arrived, not only our little family. They were formally Christians, but the Venetian government could do as it pleased and decided it did not want converts whom nobody trusted. They must all leave.

Gracia had her connections. She remained a banker operating at the highest level, learned the measure was coming, and took precautions. She found a place in Italy where Jews could still live quietly: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife Renée of France.

Ferrara was extraordinary because Renée was Protestant. The Reformation had initially made considerable headway in France. Although eventually suppressed, for a long time very many French people—the Huguenots—were Protestant. In 1550 this story was only beginning; Huguenots were not yet persecuted, and some French princes and nobles were Protestant.

Duke Ercole had married a French Protestant princess, and at her court the Inquisition did not interfere. Renée protected Protestants and even received Calvin, who would have been burned if seized elsewhere in Italy. Jews too were protected and arrived in large numbers.

All of you have had some occasion to notice that Ferrara is a capital of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Until Fascism and the Second World War, Ferrara had a large Jewish community, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there.

The family found not only asylum. Under Renée’s protection Gracia could do something that would have brought her to the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: publicly return to Judaism. She no longer hid, resumed Jewish practice and the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and rapidly became one of Ferrara’s most important Jewish figures.

At Ferrara they published the Hebrew Bible translated into Spanish; she financed it. They made two editions, one dedicated to the duke and one to Gracia Nasi, who had supplied the money. Samuel Usque published a Portuguese work there, since so many of these Jews came from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel is afflicted and persecuted, but there is consolation. Here at Ferrara we have found it: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects and assists the Jews and helps them take refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—meaning the Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it could last? It could not. Pressure came upon Ercole from every direction: the emperor, the pope, everybody. Your wife is a scandal; this must end. The duke resisted and resisted, but then had to explain to her that a Protestant could not rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits intervened; Saint Ignatius of Loyola intervened. Finally Renée surrendered, publicly converted to Catholicism, and abjured Protestantism.

Gracia decided it was time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection these things did not happen. Let us put an end to it once and for all. Very well—

## 9. Departure for Constantinople

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00877–U-00969 -->

They must go to Constantinople. First, however, Brianda had to be persuaded, and she did not agree. Second, what about all the money deposited in Venice while they waited for the heiress to grow up? They could hardly leave it there. They were not going to Constantinople without taking the money.

At this point we see who Gracia Nasi was and the level on which she operated. She contacted the sultan and his government, informed them that the entire family, company, and capital wished to move to Constantinople, and asked whether the Ottoman Empire could contact the Venetians and persuade them to reach an agreement. The sultan’s government began negotiating with the Most Serene Republic over the emigration of the Nasi family and its capital.

They eventually agreed. The Venetian government had frozen half the capital, an immense sum. They settled on something smaller. Gracia had to deposit 100,000 ducats in Venice—they liked round numbers, as you see: fifty million euros—in the name of her niece Beatriz, since otherwise Brianda would not consent. But they allowed her to take away the bulk of the capital. Gracia and her daughter went to Constantinople. Brianda and Beatriz returned to Venice to wait until Beatriz was fifteen and could withdraw the money.

Young Venetian patricians increasingly buzzed around her, wanting to marry her. But the little nephew Joseph Nasi reappeared; he was the man called upon to untangle alarming situations. He secretly reached Venice in January 1553. Beatriz was thirteen, with two years remaining before the capital became hers. Her cousin Joseph abducted her, took her to Ravenna, and married her there. Unfortunately we have no precise information as to whether Gracia knew; one might suppose she did.

It was a fine attempt, but matters were not so simple. Brianda sued: my nephew has carried off my daughter, abducted her, and married her against her will. The Venetian government annulled the marriage, had the girl returned to her mother, and banished Joseph from Venice on pain of death. If you show yourself again, know that it will end badly.

Then we do not know exactly what happened. Fortunately Brianda must have opened her eyes, or they finally convinced her through persistence. She and her daughter eventually joined the family in Constantinople. There Joseph was circumcised, officially returned to Judaism, and became Joseph Nasi. Since his marriage to Brianda’s daughter had been annulled and another unmarried cousin remained, he married Gracia’s daughter. Everything was settled perfectly—apart from the 100,000 ducats still in Venice in Beatriz’s name.

Beatriz and Brianda, moreover, continued traveling between Constantinople, Italy, and Ferrara; they were not settled. In 1555 Beatriz turned fifteen, and her mother died soon afterward. Beatriz finally inherited the last portion of capital still in Venice in her name. She was then in Ferrara, and the money was brought from Venice.

I have not yet told you that Joseph also had a brother, Bernardo, now called Samuel because he too had returned to Judaism. Exactly one year after Brianda’s death—one must observe a year of mourning—Samuel came to Ferrara, where his cousin Beatriz was with her 100,000 ducats, and married her.

The sultan’s government then intervened with the duke of Ferrara to obtain permission for everybody to go to Constantinople with the money. The duke negotiated for a while and finally yielded. Officially, at last, the entire family and all the money moved to Constantinople—

## 10. The Lady of the Ottoman Empire

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00970–U-00995 -->

—where, with all that money, they became the leading financial firm of the Ottoman Empire. For although the Ottoman Empire was ahead of the Christians in tolerance, it lagged behind in commercial and financial techniques. There was nothing there comparable to the banking networks and credit mechanisms of Christian Europe.

That was why Sultan Bayezid had said many years before: how foolish the king of Spain is to send me these subjects of his; they are the only people arriving here who can establish banking among us as well.

And so Doña Gracia, supervising her nephews’ activities, continued to manage the bank, accumulate money, and protect Jews seeking escape from the Christian world into the Ottoman Empire. She built synagogues. For a long time one in Constantinople was called the Lady’s Synagogue, where Portuguese Jews gathered. She financed synagogues, Jewish hospitals, and rabbinical schools throughout the Ottoman Empire, and tried to protect Jews who remained in the Christian world too, though that was becoming ever more difficult.

## 11. The burnings at Ancona and the boycott

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00996–U-01046 -->

At a certain point something terrible happened. Incredible though it may seem, there was a fairly prosperous Jewish community at Ancona, even in the Papal States. Although the pope promoted antisemitic policy more than anyone, these Jewish merchants and financiers were so valuable that for a long time the popes allowed openly unconverted Jews to live there. Alongside these Jews, watched and confined in the ghetto, there were also many converts. The pope himself prevented the Inquisition from troubling them.

But times change, the age advances, and it is always the same age. In 1555 the pope died and Cardinal Carafa, the Inquisition’s principal supporter, became Pope Paul IV. He supported it completely and ordered the arrest of all the Ancona conversos: former Jews from Spain and Portugal who pretended to be Christian, though the Inquisition knew perfectly well that this was untrue. It arrested them all.

Doña Gracia then intervened with the sultan. Understand what kind of world this was. Powers that officially hated one another and officially had no contacts—the pope and the sultan—in reality met, spoke, and exerted pressure on one another. Urged by Gracia, the sultan contacted the pope on behalf of the prisoners and asked for their release and the restoration of their property.

They negotiated at length. The pope eventually agreed to restore the money and lift the sequestration of the prisoners’ property, but refused to release them. An example had to be made of people who pretended to become Christians but remained Jewish. In 1556 twenty-four Jews were burned at the stake in Ancona, among them the local agent of Gracia Nasi’s firm.

Gracia then contacted the entire international Jewish commercial community and proposed boycotting Ancona and doing no further business with the pope. This proved difficult to implement, because business is business in the end. Yet the very fact that she could conceive of it gives an idea of who she was.

## 12. Tiberias, death, and the legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01047–U-01115 -->

She was a person who, a few years later, in 1558—now we are nearing the end, and Gracia is growing old, but we must still tell this story—had an idea. The sultan was Süleyman the Magnificent, not yet his son Selim, the alcoholic with whom we began. In 1558 Gracia discussed her idea with Süleyman and obtained his consent.

She leased a piece of the Holy Land: the region of Tiberias in Galilee, containing the ruins of the ancient city and many surrounding villages. Jews had not lived there since time immemorial, but Gracia’s idea was to bring them back to the Holy Land. With the sultan’s consent she leased Tiberias, built a settlement, and began transferring refugees arriving from the Christian world. In her own way, in short, she had also invented Zionism: the idea of returning the Jews to the Holy Land.

The settlement functioned for a time, then did not last long. The Jews fleeing Europe were almost all merchants and financiers. What were they to do there in the Palestinian desert? The undertaking did not prosper, though the project was still discussed a few years later.

By then it was attributed to the nephew Joseph, who, as we have seen, was a kind of bogeyman to Westerners and Christians. Whenever he was involved, they feared the worst. A French ambassador in Constantinople wrote that the Jew Miques, Joseph Nasi, had again obtained the sultan’s permission to build a city on Lake Tiberias where only Jews could live and that, as far as could be judged, he planned to make himself king of the Jews. Once again, you see what these people represented in the political imagination of the Christian world.

Every life eventually ends. In 1569 Doña Gracia fell ill and died, leaving the baton to her nephew. For years he continued to manage the company and passed through difficult times. The defeat at Lepanto has gone down in Christian history as the moment when Christendom triumphed over the Muslim world, the Ottoman Empire. In reality it was certainly a grand and disastrous battle, but one that literally left matters as they were, because the Ottomans ultimately won that war and obtained what they wanted: Cyprus.

Joseph Nasi naturally obtained the monopoly on wine exports from Cyprus. Thus, as if the capital accumulated by his aunt had not been enough, the family continued to accumulate more.

It would be nice if I had a brilliant conclusion, but in truth I do not. That was the story. Gracia died; that world ended and is far away. So many things have been overturned, including arguments about tolerance and the capacity to be tolerant. But in any case, I hope it seemed a good story to you. Thank you.
