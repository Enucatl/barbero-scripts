# Money and Women: Gracia Nasi, a Sixteenth-Century Financier — faithful English translation

## 1. A woman, a family, many names

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00077 -->

Good evening, everyone. Tonight we're telling the story of a woman. It's a complicated story, with all kinds of threads running through it. And I do mean complicated: eventually, you're going to get lost. You're going to mix up one person with another. Just know that going in. There's no way around it.

Naturally, it's a story about money. This series is about economics, and in practical terms, that means money. In the Renaissance, the period we're talking about, it means actual sacks of gold ducats. So this is a story about family and money. And whenever you put family and money together—especially the question of who controls the money—you get conflict. People clash. They have very different ideas about what to do. This is the story of a great family that can protect its own, but can also tear itself apart in hatred and resentment.

It's also a story of intolerance and persecution, and of the Jewish people's stubborn resistance in the Renaissance Mediterranean. The pattern may be the opposite of what we'd expect today, given where we now see tolerance and intolerance in the world. At the time, Jews face intolerance in the Christian world and, by contrast, tolerance in the Muslim world.

The story moves through Portugal, Spain, Flanders, Italy, and Constantinople. Italy plays a central role, and at the time it's a place of extraordinary contradictions. It can offer refuge, but it can also—and more often does—bring danger and persecution.

Above all, though, this is the story of a woman—an exceptional woman in her own time, or any other. As we've heard, even today it's not exactly easy for a woman working at San Paolo to rise into management, though people are working on it. It certainly isn't any easier in the past. And yet the workings of family, inheritance, marriage, and wills can put a woman in charge of a family, a company, and an international business empire.

This woman's name is—well, she actually has several names. We'll use the Italian pronunciation and call her Gracia Nasi. The more accurate pronunciation would be Doña Gracia Nasi. But she's also known as Beatriz de Luna, and that's the name she uses for much of her life. In the end, though, almost everyone simply calls her the Lady.

This is a story about people who move from one world to another and one name to another—people with many names. Nasi is a Hebrew name. In biblical Hebrew, the word means leader or prince. Today it's the title of the president of the State of Israel: the *nasi*. A family that carries this name in the diaspora is clearly a prestigious one, a family of leaders that the community sees as noble and distinguished. The Nasis are believed to be descendants of King David.

## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

I'll begin this story where I first came across Gracia myself, many years ago, while I was writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the famous battle: 1571, the great victory of the Venetian and Spanish Catholic fleets over the sultan's fleet. As I worked on the book and pieced together the background to the battle and the war, I ran into someone I'd never heard of—even though he was enormously famous in his own time.

His name is Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally, he has another name too: Miques, as the Venetians pronounce the Portuguese name in their own way. Like I said, this is a story about people with many names. Joseph Nasi is our Gracia's nephew—Doña Gracia, as everyone calls her. He's both her nephew and her son-in-law. These families tend to keep everything in the family, including marriage. Marry an outsider, and the dowry takes money out of the family. The money has to stay home.

I come across Joseph Nasi because he's famous in Constantinople in the years leading up to Lepanto, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, talks about him constantly in his reports. Venice has an ambassador in Constantinople who sends long letters home, keeping the government up to date on events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. Anyone can go read them, and I did. One of the Venetians' obsessions is finding out what Joseph Nasi is up to. They first know him as João Miques, so the ambassador writes of João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.”

Do you hear the judgment packed into “at present calls himself”? He has taken back his Jewish name. At first, he goes by one of our names. Now he's living among the more tolerant Turks, and he has reclaimed his Jewish one. This isn't just a matter of official records. That phrase passes judgment on a man the Venetians know as João Miques and who now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians know him well because he lives in Venice for a time, until he's expelled over a strange affair we'll get to later. He's banished, and if he comes back, he risks losing his head. But he doesn't come back. He stays in Constantinople, where he schemes, makes money, and becomes one of the richest and most influential men in the city.

Marcantonio Barbaro is convinced that Nasi has so much influence because he knows how to win over the sultan—and the sultan has his weaknesses. The sultan at this point is Selim, son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman reigns for years and inspires fear around the world. Then he dies, and Selim takes the throne. Selim is nothing like his father. The Venetian ambassadors describe him as a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It's not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent's son and then have to replace him. Selim holds audiences in the same room where his father did, but he won't sit in the same place because he says he isn't worthy. He sits farther back, in a corner.

One sign of Selim's troubled personality is that, by all accounts, he's an alcoholic. Now, he's the Ottoman sultan and the caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople is a huge city where half the population is Christian or Jewish, wine flows freely, and the Turks aren't especially strict about the ban. The sultan certainly isn't. The Venetian ambassadors report that he starts his day with a small carafe of liquor at breakfast and keeps going from there.

Barbaro says that Sultan Selim, being both a drunk and a glutton, “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—our Joseph Nasi again—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” This, the Venetian ambassador says, is how Nasi wins the sultan's trust and turns him against us—against Venice, which these Jews hate. As the leader of his entire people, Nasi does everything he can to set the sultan against us.

In Venetian eyes, Joseph Nasi becomes an almost mythical figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews live all over the world, so their leader must have information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence in the English sense: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan learns the secrets of Christendom, especially the secrets of Venice.

Let's stop here for a moment. You recognize parts of an idea that comes back in more recent times: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. The Jews supposedly want to rule the world. They're everywhere, they're all connected, and they control the banks. This obsession is widespread in Renaissance Italy, especially in Venice. It's a pathology, of course. The Venetians imagine these Jewish bankers have far more power than they really do.

But this pathology does have a motive behind it: fierce competition between Venetian and Jewish merchants. The Ottoman Empire is an essential trading and financial partner for Venice. The Venetians have huge investments there—not only in Constantinople, but also in Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet the sultan allows the Venetian unbelievers to do business in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who take a sizable cut of the profits. So the Venetians stew over how wicked these Jews are and what a threat they pose.

This theme keeps coming up in letters from the Venetian ambassadors in Constantinople. They're amazed by the contrast. In the parts of Italy where Jews are still allowed to live, they're watched closely and have to be careful not to get above themselves. In Constantinople, as the Venetians see it, Jews lord it over everyone. No one bothers them openly; the sultan protects them. The ambassadors write: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Meanwhile, Joseph Nasi goes about his business. Is the story that he supplies the sultan with wine just a legend? Maybe. But we know he holds the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a city where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drink wine every day, that's obviously a very big contract. Young Joseph Nasi is a business giant, but behind him stands his aunt. By the years I'm talking about, 1568 to 1570, she's nearing the end of her life. Sixty is old at the time, and little Joseph, the nephew, is taking over the family business. Until very recently, though, she controls everything.

## 3. From Spain to Portugal

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00240–U-00388 -->

“A Portuguese woman,” says a European envoy. He's not Venetian; he works for the Fuggers. You know the Fugger bank, another financial powerhouse of the age, based in Germany. Naturally, the Fuggers have agents in Constantinople too, and those agents write home: yes, there's plenty of money moving around here, and all of it passes through the hands of this Portuguese woman. She looks like a European noblewoman, dresses like a European princess, and lives in extravagant luxury, surrounded by servants.

This is our Gracia Nasi at sixty, in 1568. That's how I first encounter her. Then I go back to find out what happens earlier. How does she get there? How does Joseph get there? How does this family arrive with such enormous wealth? Where do they come from? A long way off. The family has no roots in Constantinople. They come from Western Europe, from Spain. They're one of the many Jewish families living in Spain during the Middle Ages.

One reason is that much of Spain is ruled by Arabs during the Middle Ages, when Arabs and Muslims are more tolerant than Christians. Large numbers of Jews live in Muslim Spain, and they stay as Christians gradually reconquer the country. Spanish history has the great epic of the *Reconquista*. In a sense, it's their equivalent of our Risorgimento, the story everyone learns in school. This is how the nation is founded: over centuries, Catholic kings take Spain back piece by piece and drive the Arabs out.

The Reconquista ends in a year that seems almost chosen to mark a turning point in history: 1492. I don't need to tell you what else happens that year, or who sails from Spain with three caravels. That same year, the Catholic Monarchs conquer Granada, the last Muslim kingdom. From that point on, all of Spain is Christian. Muslims are expelled or forced to convert. Then Isabella and Ferdinand, the Catholic Monarchs, decide to do the same thing to the Jews. We've purified Spain. There can be no more infidels, no more unbelievers. There are no Muslims left, and soon there will be no Jews either.

Think about what kind of year 1492 is. On one side, the world opens up. On the other, we see this powerful vision of a Europe that wants to be pure, free of—well. The Jews have to convert or face expulsion. Many give in and convert; many others choose to leave. And the message to those leaving is clear: you can take your belongings, but no money. The gold stays here.

Communities that have lived there for centuries now have to go. They spend their last night in the cemeteries, saying goodbye to their ancestors, and then leave forever. Where do they go? To the Islamic world, to the Ottoman Empire. At the time, the empire takes it for granted that Jews and Christians are inferior to Muslims. But the Quran also teaches that as long as Jews and Christians cause no trouble, they can live among us.

So Jews leave for the Ottoman Empire: Constantinople, Salonica, Sarajevo, Smyrna, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. All these places are under Ottoman rule, and they fill with Jewish refugees. Sultan Bayezid is said to remark: I hadn't realized the king of Spain was a fool. He had all these useful subjects, and now he's sending them to me. These Jews are merchants, entrepreneurs, and bankers. Whether they can bring their capital with them is another question, of course. Still, there are ways to get some of it out.

Many others stay and convert to Christianity. How many? You should know this is still a sensitive question in Spain. From this point on, the country's dominant ideology is *limpieza de sangre*, purity of blood. Even the poorest Spanish peasant in the sixteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century—and perhaps even today—takes pride in saying: I'm an Old Christian. I'm not like those descendants of Jews or Muslims. To be a *cristiano viejo* is to take pride in your pure blood.

So you see the problem: how many Spaniards really descend from those converts? A study of paternal lineages estimates that about 20 percent are of Sephardic Jewish origin and 10 percent of North African origin. [N-004] Naturally, the research causes an uproar in Spain. Nobody wants to be one of them.

The very rich do have another option, though. For the moment, Portugal takes a more flexible approach. If you're a major banker with plenty of money, the kingdom of Portugal will let you settle there. That's what the Nasi family does. They move to Lisbon.

But it doesn't last. They're fighting the spirit of the age. These Jews, always on the run, keep looking for somewhere they'll be treated a little better, or at least tolerated. But one government after another eventually decides it has to follow Spain's example and drive them out.

They stay in Lisbon for only five years. In 1497, before Gracia is born, her parents are living there. That year, the king of Portugal decides he wants a pure country too, which means no Jews: convert or leave. Gracia's family converts to Christianity. They stay in Lisbon and officially become New Christians, or *conversos*. But people call them something else: *marranos*. The word quickly becomes an insult in Spanish and Portuguese, and in Italian too. For us, I think, it brings to mind *The Three Musketeers*: “vile marrano!” Originally, though, *marrano* is a technical term for a converted Jew, one of the New Christians the Old Christians distrust and look down on.

Naturally, they have to change their last name too. They can't go on using the Jewish name Nasi; they need a Portuguese one. They choose de Luna. And so Gracia is born in Lisbon, probably around 1510. Now you can see why she has two names. She's baptized Beatriz—Beatriz de Luna—but inside the family they secretly call her Gracia, a Hebrew name, or rather the Spanish translation of the Hebrew name Hannah. In private, then, she's Gracia Nasi. In the official records, she's Beatriz de Luna.

## 4. Marriage, spices, and inheritance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00389–U-00484 -->

For years, they live fairly quietly in Portugal and do business. When Gracia turns eighteen, she marries an extremely rich merchant named Francisco Mendes. Her name changes again—now she's Beatriz Mendes—but I'll spare you that. Naturally, Francisco is also a converted Jew, because people marry within the community. He's also her cousin, because when I say within the community, I mean very much within the community. In truth, they're all Christians for show. They've stayed faithful to Judaism, but they have to practice in secret.

Why? Because the authorities are watching, the neighbors are spying, and the Inquisition is checking. Do these people observe Christian feast days? Yes. They fast during Lent, of course. But they eat pork too, don't they? If a neighbor notices that one week goes by, then another, and a certain family never buys any pork, people start to wonder. Then the anonymous accusations begin, because Jews, as you know, don't eat pork.

But they're enormously rich, because fabulous fortunes are being made in Lisbon's trade at the time. Why? The Portuguese have found the shortest route to the Indies, and now they can import the most valuable goods of all: spices. Spices are the craze of the day. Rich people go wild for them and will pay any price. That may seem a little strange to us, but remember: these people have no coffee, no tea, no hard liquor, no cocaine, and so on. Spices fill the place of all those things. They're a status symbol.

The Portuguese control the spice trade. Remember learning about the great voyages of exploration in school? Vasco da Gama rounds the Cape of Good Hope and reaches Asia by sailing around the bottom of Africa. Why? To get spices. The route saves time, enormous fortunes pile up—and then that money goes toward marriages between cousins.

Marriage between cousins is forbidden, of course. But if you apply to Rome for permission, explain your reasons, and send enough bribes along with the application, you get permission to marry your cousin.

So Gracia marries at eighteen. Seven years later, her husband dies. She's a widow at twenty-five. His will says that from now on, his brother Diogo and his wife Gracia will manage his fortune together. That isn't automatic. When a woman's husband dies, she has the right to take back her dowry. It's hers, after all, and it's what she'll live on as a widow. But a husband doesn't automatically make his wife an heir to the family fortune, much less formally put her in charge of managing it. This already tells us that Gracia has unusual abilities. Her husband sees them.

So at twenty-five, Gracia is managing an enormous business fortune alongside her brother-in-law Diogo, her husband's brother, who represents the firm in Antwerp. Antwerp, on the coast of Flanders, is another place awash in money. It's one of the great trading capitals of the Renaissance world—the capital of the Atlantic, perhaps the busiest commercial port in Europe at the time.

Antwerp is in Flanders, but it belongs to Charles V, king of Spain and emperor of Germany, so it's one of Spain's great ports. Unlike the ports in Spain itself, far from the rest of Europe, Antwerp is close to the German-speaking world, Central Europe, northern Europe, and the British Isles. Most Spanish and Portuguese trade with Flanders, Germany, England, and the Baltic passes through Antwerp.

English wool arrives there on its way to Italy and the Florentine textile industry. Wine comes in from France and Spain, then goes on to England and Germany. Timber comes from Scandinavia for shipyards across Europe. Gold and silver arrive too, among the main cargo carried by Spanish galleons from the American colonies. All that gold and silver flows into Seville and Antwerp, where it gets spent. So the de Luna–Mendes firm has its headquarters in Lisbon and a major branch in Antwerp.

## 5. The Inquisition and the move to Antwerp

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00485–U-00600 -->

Gracia is twenty-five, so the year is 1535. Up to now, the family has lived quietly in Lisbon, as long as they pretend to be Christian converts. The next year, the machinery of persecution starts moving again. On May 23, 1536, the pope establishes the Portuguese Inquisition, modeled on the Spanish one.

What is the Inquisition? Basically, it's a court found throughout the Catholic world, staffed by specialists whose job is to hunt down heretics and false converts. But in Spain, it's something different. Elsewhere, the Inquisition answers directly to Rome and the pope and follows relatively calm, moderate procedures, so to speak. Cases are sent to Rome and debated. Spain has asked for emergency powers. The country is full of converted Jews and Moriscos whom the authorities don't trust, so it gets a special Inquisition. This one answers to the king, not Rome, and can act much faster and more ruthlessly on the ground. Portugal now gets the same institution.

That gives the Inquisition enormous power. In other Catholic countries, it answers to Rome and has to deal with local authorities. Those authorities may be thoroughly Catholic, but they don't like having a court operate in their territory while taking orders from Rome instead of their own government. So they generally throw up every obstacle they can. Venice, for example, hates the Jews, but it doesn't care for the Inquisition either. We Venetian patricians don't want some inquisitor questioning, arresting, and sentencing people without answering to the Most Serene Republic. So the Republic uses every trick in the book to frustrate the Inquisition. The Spanish Inquisition, by contrast, answers directly to the king and doesn't have that problem. The same goes for Portugal.

It's 1536. Within a couple of years, Gracia leaves Lisbon for Antwerp. Antwerp is also part of the Spanish Empire, but with the new Inquisition, Lisbon has become a dangerous place for anyone who still won't eat pork and secretly practices Judaism at home. Gracia leaves and takes everything with her. The firm's operations move to Antwerp. She brings along her younger sister, Brianda. I warned you the cast would start multiplying.

Diogo is already in Antwerp—remember him? He's the brother of Gracia's late husband, and he runs the company there. Now that everyone's together again, what could be better than a nice wedding? Diogo Mendes marries Brianda. Francisco marries Gracia, and Francisco's brother Diogo marries Gracia's sister.

Under Sephardic Jewish law, when a man dies, his brother is supposed to marry his widow, especially if the couple has no children. But Francisco and Gracia already have a daughter, also known as Reyna. So Diogo doesn't have to marry his brother's widow. He can marry the widow's sister instead, and everything still stays in the family.

The symmetries in this family's history are remarkable. Gracia and Francisco have a daughter they call Anna—a name that itself means Gracia—but her official name is Reyna. Brianda, who marries her brother-in-law Diogo, has a daughter too. At home they call the girl Gracia, while in the official records she's naturally Beatriz, like her aunt. I promise, historians go crazy over this too, so don't worry about it too much. Just notice the symmetry: two sisters marry two brothers, and each couple has a daughter.

Then Francisco dies. I don't know when Diogo notices the glaring lack of symmetry, but he dies too. Now we have two widowed sisters, the older Gracia and the younger Brianda, each with a little girl. In his will, Diogo puts his sister-in-law Gracia in charge of the entire family fortune. All of it. Technically, half belongs to Gracia and half to Brianda. No one disputes the ownership. But Diogo puts Gracia alone in charge of the company and its capital.

This is our second sign that Gracia knows how to run a business. We can only imagine how Brianda feels. She can't be too happy, as we'll soon see. For now, though, there are more urgent things to worry about. Yes, they're in Antwerp, but they're still inside the Spanish Empire. And Jews are constantly passing through this great port from every corner of the Christian world, determined to leave it behind and reach the Muslim world—to reach Constantinople.

## 6. The rescue network and the loan to Charles V

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00601–U-00684 -->

The best route goes through Venice. A support network in Antwerp helps converted Jews—who haven't really converted at all—get to Venice and then on to Constantinople. Antwerp's Jewish community, with its bankers and its wealth, tries to help their fellow Jews find refuge far from the Christian world.

Gracia now emerges not only as a businesswoman and banker, but as the organizer of a network that makes the journey easier. Everything has to happen as secretly as possible. If a wealthy Jew tells the Spanish authorities he wants to leave, they'll say: we're delighted to see you go—about time. Go ahead, but the money stays here. If he wants to take his money, he has to slip out in secret and move it through the banking system. That's where Gracia can help. Banking means you can transfer capital through bills of exchange, checks, and transfers between accounts. You can get money out right under Emperor Charles V's nose.

So the authorities keep an eye on the family. Soon after Diogo dies, an investigation begins. The dead man is accused of secretly remaining Jewish, what they call crypto-Judaism. Diogo is dead, but his widow, sister-in-law, daughter, and niece are all alive. If the investigation goes forward, no one knows what might happen to them.

People at this level also know how to work the system. Why are the authorities coming after us? Is there anything we can do? It soon turns out there is. Emperor Charles V needs an interest-free loan of 100,000 ducats, and he has decided that these loyal New Christian subjects will surely be happy to provide it. The Nasi, de Luna–Mendes firm manages to come up with the money.

It's hard to convey what 100,000 ducats means. In today's money, you might call it fifty million euros, which doesn't sound like all that much. But 100,000 ducats can buy far more then than fifty million euros can today. It's enough to build a fleet, raise an army, and fight a war. Think about the world we're in: bankers control sums that governments can't raise unless they borrow from those same bankers. A few years ago, we might have found that incredible. Today, we're no longer surprised when private finance controls more wealth than some countries do.

They hang on for a few more years. But the next time the emperor demands another interest-free loan, the family starts seriously thinking about leaving. In 1544, Gracia is thirty-four and firmly in charge of the family. She leaves Antwerp with her sister, daughter, and niece, without mentioning that they won't be back. They're just going on vacation, they say, to enjoy the hot springs at Aachen. Aachen is a spa town, you know. There's a reason Charlemagne moves there long before. He's old, he's in pain, and the hot baths are exactly what he needs. Plenty of people go there to take the waters.

The family travels from Antwerp to Aachen, then crosses into France and heads for Lyon. From Lyon, they go on to Venice.

## 7. Venice and the dispute over the fortune

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00685–U-00760 -->

In Venice, they rent a palace on the Grand Canal—just to keep a low profile. Venice, of course, is full of Jews, but they’re confined to the ghetto, a fairly recent invention. In the Middle Ages, Jews live wherever they want, even in Christian cities. By the Renaissance, that’s no longer the case. Now they’re supposed to be watched and kept apart. Venice has had a ghetto since 1516, and Jews are required to live there.

But our women aren’t Jews, are they? Their names are de Luna, Beatriz, and so on. They’re converts to Christianity, so they can live wherever they like—including a palace on the Grand Canal.

They’ve had to leave part of their capital behind in Antwerp. They couldn’t get everything out: real estate, palaces, offices, and a great deal of money. But they hope to recover at least some of it. They also leave the young nephew behind. This is where João Miques, the future Joseph Nasi, enters the story. He wheels and deals, pays bribes, calls in recommendations, and manages to recover part of the capital and move it into Venetian banks. The next year, 1546, he joins the family in Venice.

They’re still in a violently antisemitic Christian world. But the Inquisition isn’t as frightening in Venice as it is in Spain or Portugal, and Turkey is just a short trip away. All you have to do is board a galley, and within a few days you’re under the protection of the Grand Signor, the sultan. In Venice, they can breathe—or at least that must be what Gracia thinks. Maybe now we can breathe for a while.

That’s when Brianda decides to challenge Gracia’s control over everything. She’s put up with it long enough. Now she’s had enough. Venice has courts called the *Giudici del Forestier*, which hear complaints and lawsuits brought by foreigners. Brianda takes her case to them: Half the capital is mine, but my sister controls all of it. She won’t even show me the accounts. She uses the will of my late husband, God rest his soul, as an excuse.

Brianda sues, and the judges side with her. Gracia has to deposit half the family’s capital in the Venetian treasury. We’ll hold on to it for now. The ruling says that Brianda’s daughter Beatriz, who is only eight, can withdraw that half when she turns eighteen. In other words, the Venetian authorities get their hands on half this enormous fortune and control it for the next ten years. Once the girl grows up, they’ll see what happens.

Gracia probably sees something like this coming. Here are two women, each with a daughter. Sooner or later, some man is going to get his hands on the money by marrying one of the girls. Whoever marries either one will control her fortune. Gracia has already figured this out in Antwerp. Her own daughter Beatriz isn’t even ten when none other than a royal prince, Don Francisco of Aragon, sends word that he might be open to discussing a marriage.

## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia manages to head that off. The years pass, and now the family is in Venice. But word quickly spreads among the young patricians: two Jewish women have two daughters who are going to inherit a fortune. Gracia keeps a close eye on her daughter. Brianda, though, doesn’t seem entirely sound of mind. So young men from good families start circling, thinking, If only I could land that girl—

But that’s not their only problem. The whole age is against them. Wherever they go, sooner or later it catches up with them. Christian governments are becoming more and more intolerant.

They’ve been in Venice for five years when, in 1550, the city orders all *marranos* expelled—all Spanish Jews who have converted to Christianity. Clearly, plenty of them have arrived, not just our little family. Officially, they’re Christians. But the Venetian government can do whatever it likes, and it decides it doesn’t want converts no one trusts. They all have to leave.

Gracia has connections. She’s still a banker at the very highest level, so she hears what’s coming and takes precautions. She finds a place in Italy where Jews can still live in peace: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife, Renée of France.

Ferrara is unusual because Renée is Protestant. At first, the Reformation makes real headway in France. It’s eventually crushed, but for a long time a great many French people—the Huguenots—are Protestant. In 1550, that story is just beginning. The Huguenots aren’t being persecuted yet, and some French princes and nobles are Protestant themselves.

Duke Ercole has married a French Protestant princess, and the Inquisition stays out of her court. Renée protects Protestants. She even welcomes Calvin, who would be burned at the stake if he were caught anywhere else in Italy. She protects Jews too, and they arrive in large numbers.

You’ve probably come across the idea that Ferrara is one of the great centers of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Ferrara has a large Jewish community right up to Fascism and the Second World War, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there today.

The family finds more than asylum there. Under Renée’s protection, Gracia can do something that would get her burned at the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: return to Judaism in public. She stops hiding. She practices Judaism again, goes back to the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and quickly becomes one of the leading figures in Ferrara’s Jewish community.

In Ferrara, they publish a Spanish translation of the Hebrew Bible, financed by Gracia. There are two editions: one dedicated to the duke, and one to Gracia Nasi, who put up the money. Samuel Usque also publishes a work in Portuguese there, since so many of these Jews come from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel suffers and faces persecution, but there is consolation. We’ve found it here in Ferrara: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects the Jews, helps them, and helps them find refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—the nation of Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it can last? Of course not. Ercole comes under pressure from every direction—the emperor, the pope, everyone. Your wife is a scandal. This has to stop. The duke holds out for as long as he can, but eventually he has to tell her that a Protestant can’t rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits step in. Saint Ignatius of Loyola himself steps in. Finally, Renée gives way. She publicly converts to Catholicism and renounces Protestantism.

Gracia decides it’s time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection, none of this happens. Let’s settle the matter once and for all. All right—

## 9. Departure for Constantinople

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00877–U-00969 -->

They have to go to Constantinople. But first they need to convince Brianda, and she’s against it. Then there’s all the money sitting in Venice while they wait for the heiress to grow up. What happens to that? They can’t just leave it behind. They’re not moving to Constantinople without the money.

This is where we see who Gracia Nasi really is—and the level she operates at. She contacts the sultan and his government. She tells them that the whole family wants to move to Constantinople, along with the business and its capital, and asks the Ottoman government to approach the Venetians and help work out a deal. So the sultan’s government begins negotiating with the Most Serene Republic over the Nasi family’s departure and the transfer of its money.

Eventually, they reach an agreement. The Venetian government has frozen half the capital, an enormous sum, but now they settle on a smaller amount. Gracia has to leave 100,000 ducats in Venice in the name of her niece Beatriz. They like round numbers, as you can see: fifty million euros. Without that, Brianda won’t agree. But Gracia is allowed to take most of the capital with her. She and her daughter leave for Constantinople, while Brianda and Beatriz return to Venice and wait for Beatriz to turn fifteen, when she can withdraw the money.

More and more young Venetian patricians circle around Beatriz, hoping to marry her. But then the young nephew, Joseph Nasi, reappears. He’s the man they call when a dangerous situation needs sorting out. He slips into Venice in January 1553. Beatriz is thirteen, with two years to go before the money becomes hers. Her cousin Joseph abducts her, takes her to Ravenna, and marries her there. Unfortunately, we don’t know for sure whether Gracia knows about the plan. You might imagine she does.

It’s a bold attempt, but things aren’t that simple. Brianda sues: My nephew carried off my daughter, abducted her, and married her against her will. The Venetian government annuls the marriage, returns the girl to her mother, and banishes Joseph from Venice on pain of death. Show your face here again, and it won’t end well.

After that, we don’t know exactly what happens. Brianda must finally see sense—or perhaps they simply wear her down. In any case, she and her daughter eventually join the family in Constantinople. There, Joseph is circumcised, formally returns to Judaism, and becomes Joseph Nasi. His marriage to Brianda’s daughter has been annulled, and he still has another unmarried cousin, so he marries Gracia’s daughter. Everything works out perfectly—except for the 100,000 ducats still sitting in Venice under Beatriz’s name.

Meanwhile, Beatriz and Brianda keep traveling between Constantinople, Italy, and Ferrara. They haven’t settled anywhere. In 1555, Beatriz turns fifteen, and her mother dies soon afterward. Beatriz finally inherits the last share of the capital still held for her in Venice. She’s in Ferrara by then, and the money is brought to her from Venice.

I haven’t told you yet that Joseph also has a brother, Bernardo. He now goes by Samuel because he too has returned to Judaism. Exactly one year after Brianda’s death—you have to observe a year of mourning—Samuel comes to Ferrara, where his cousin Beatriz is waiting with her 100,000 ducats, and marries her.

The sultan’s government then approaches the duke of Ferrara and asks him to let everyone leave for Constantinople with the money. The duke negotiates for a while, then finally gives in. At long last, the whole family and all its money officially move to Constantinople—

## 10. The Lady of the Ottoman Empire

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00970–U-00995 -->

—where all that money makes them the leading financial firm in the Ottoman Empire. The empire may be ahead of the Christian world in tolerance, but it’s behind in commercial and financial methods. It has nothing comparable to the banking networks and credit systems of Christian Europe.

That’s why Sultan Bayezid had said many years before: how foolish the king of Spain is to send me these subjects of his; they are the only people arriving here who can establish banking among us as well.

And so Doña Gracia keeps an eye on her nephews’ work while continuing to run the bank, build the family fortune, and protect Jews trying to escape the Christian world for the Ottoman Empire. She builds synagogues. For many years, one in Constantinople is known as the Lady’s Synagogue, and it becomes a gathering place for Portuguese Jews. She funds synagogues, Jewish hospitals, and rabbinical schools across the Ottoman Empire. She also tries to protect Jews who remain in the Christian world, though that’s getting harder and harder.

## 11. The burnings at Ancona and the boycott

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00996–U-01046 -->

Then something terrible happens. Hard as it may be to believe, Ancona has a fairly prosperous Jewish community, right there in the Papal States. The pope pushes antisemitic policies more aggressively than anyone. But these Jewish merchants and financiers are so valuable that, for many years, the popes allow Jews who have never converted to live openly in Ancona. Alongside them are many converts. The Jews are watched and confined to the ghetto, but the pope himself keeps the Inquisition from harassing them.

But times change. The age moves on, always in the same direction. In 1555, the pope dies, and Cardinal Carafa, the Inquisition’s leading champion, becomes Pope Paul IV. He backs it all the way and orders every converso in Ancona arrested. These are former Jews from Spain and Portugal who have presented themselves as Christians, though the Inquisition is convinced they’re lying. It arrests all of them.

Doña Gracia appeals to the sultan. And think about the kind of world this is. The pope and the sultan officially hate each other. Officially, they don’t even have contact. In practice, their representatives meet and talk, and the two sides put pressure on each other. At Gracia’s urging, the sultan contacts the pope on behalf of the prisoners. He asks that they be released and their property returned.

The negotiations drag on. Eventually, the pope agrees to return the money and lift the seizure of the prisoners’ property, but he refuses to free them. People who pretend to become Christians while remaining Jewish have to be made an example of. In 1556, twenty-four Jews are burned at the stake in Ancona. Among them is the local agent for Gracia Nasi’s firm.

Gracia responds by reaching out to the entire international Jewish business community. She proposes a boycott of Ancona: no more business with the pope. The boycott proves hard to carry out because, in the end, business is business. But the fact that she can even imagine organizing something like this tells you who she is.

## 12. Tiberias, death, and the legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01047–U-01115 -->

A few years later, in 1558, this remarkable woman has an idea. We’re nearing the end now, and Gracia is growing old, but there’s still one more story to tell. The sultan is Süleyman the Magnificent—not yet his son Selim, the alcoholic we begin with. Gracia takes her idea to Süleyman and gets his approval.

She leases part of the Holy Land: the area around Tiberias in Galilee, which includes the ruins of the ancient city and many surrounding villages. No Jews have lived there for as long as anyone can remember, but Gracia wants to bring them back to the Holy Land. With the sultan’s approval, she leases Tiberias, builds a settlement, and starts bringing in refugees from the Christian world. In her own way, you might say she invents Zionism too: the idea of returning the Jews to the Holy Land.

The settlement works for a while, but it doesn’t last. The Jews fleeing Europe are almost all merchants and financiers. What are they supposed to do out there in the Palestinian desert? The venture doesn’t prosper, although people are still talking about the project a few years later.

By then, the project is associated with her nephew Joseph. As we’ve seen, Westerners and Christians treat him as a kind of bogeyman. Whenever his name comes up, they assume the worst. A French ambassador in Constantinople writes that the Jew Miques—Joseph Nasi—has once again received the sultan’s permission to build a city on Lake Tiberias where only Jews can live. As far as the ambassador can tell, Joseph plans to make himself king of the Jews. Once again, you can see what these people represent in the Christian world’s political imagination.

Every life comes to an end. In 1569, Doña Gracia falls ill and dies, passing the baton to her nephew. He keeps running the company for years and lives through some difficult times. In Christian history, the defeat at Lepanto goes down as the moment when Christendom triumphs over the Muslim world, over the Ottoman Empire. It’s certainly a huge and disastrous battle. But in practical terms, it changes nothing. The Ottomans ultimately win the war and get what they want: Cyprus.

Joseph Nasi, naturally, gets the monopoly on wine exports from Cyprus. As if the fortune his aunt built weren’t already enough, the family keeps making more.

It would be nice if I had some brilliant conclusion, but honestly, I don’t. That’s the story. Gracia dies. That world comes to an end, and it feels very far away. So much has changed, including the way we think about tolerance and who is capable of it. But in any case, I hope you found it a good story. Thank you.
