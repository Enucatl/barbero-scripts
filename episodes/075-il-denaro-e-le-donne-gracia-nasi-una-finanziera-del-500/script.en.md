# Money and Women: Gracia Nasi, a Sixteenth-Century Financier — faithful English translation

## 1. A woman, a family, many names

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00077 -->

Good evening, everyone. So, tonight we’re going to tell a story: the story of a woman, a complicated story with many intertwined threads. It really is complicated; eventually, you’ll get lost. You’ll confuse one person with another—just know that from the start—but it’s inevitable.

It is a story about money, naturally, because in this series we decide to talk about economics, which in concrete terms means money. In the period we are talking about, the Renaissance, it means, very concretely indeed, sacks of gold ducats. It is a story about family and money. And, as always happens in these cases, it is also a story of conflict. When family, money, and the management of money are involved, clashes and differing opinions about what should be done are inevitable. It is the story of a great family capable of protecting its members, but also of being torn apart by hatred and resentment.

It is also a story of intolerance and persecution, and of the stubborn resistance of a people, the Jews, in the Mediterranean of the Renaissance. Contrary to what we might expect today, given the way tolerance and intolerance are distributed in the present world, this is a people that then has to face the intolerance of the Christian world and, by contrast, the tolerance of the Muslim world.

It is a story that unfolds between Portugal, Spain, Flanders, Italy, and Constantinople. Italy has a central role and is an extraordinarily contradictory place in that period. It can be a place of welcome, but also—and in reality more often—a place of danger and persecution.

Above all, it is the story of a woman. A woman exceptional for her age, as she would have been in any other, because if today it is not very easy for a female employee of San Paolo to become an executive, as we have heard—even though work is being done in that direction—it was no easier in the past. And yet the dynamics of family, inheritance, marriages, and wills could bring a woman to the head of a family, a company, and commercial dealings on an international scale.

This woman is called—well, she has many names. We’ll call her Gracia Nasi. More correctly, it would be Doña Gracia Nasi, but she is also called Beatriz de Luna. For much of her life she goes by that name, though in the end almost everybody simply calls her the Lady.

This is a story of people who pass from one world to another and from one name to another, people with many names. Nasi is a Hebrew name, a Hebrew word which in the Hebrew of the Bible means leader, prince. Today it is the title of the president of the State of Israel: the *nasi*. A family bearing that surname in the diaspora is obviously a prestigious family, a family of leaders, one the community regards as noble and illustrious. The idea is that those called Nasi descend from King David.

## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

To tell you this story, I’ll begin with the moment I first encountered her, many years ago, while writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the celebrated Battle of Lepanto: 1571, the great victory of the Catholic fleets, Venetian and Spanish, over the sultan’s fleet. While working on that book and building the context for the battle and the war, I came across a figure I had never heard of, though he had been extremely famous in his own day.

He is called Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally he has another name as well: Miques. The Venetians pronounce the Portuguese in their own way—and, as I said, this is a story of people with many names. This Joseph Nasi is the nephew of our Gracia, of Doña Gracia as everybody calls her. He is her nephew and also her son-in-law. These are families inclined to keep everything within the family, marriages included, because if one marries outside, money leaves with the dowries, whereas the money has to remain at home.

I encountered Joseph Nasi because in the years before Lepanto he is famous in Constantinople, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, continually discusses him in his reports. The Venetians have an ambassador in Constantinople who writes long letters to Venice informing the government about events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. One can go and read them; I went and read them. One Venetian obsession is finding out what Joseph Nasi is doing. They first know him as João Miques, and so the ambassador writes: João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.” Do you understand what “at present calls himself” means? He has resumed his Jewish name. Before, he has one of our names; now that he is among the Turks, who are more tolerant, he has taken back his Jewish name. This is not merely a registry matter: there is an entire judgment contained in the statement that the man we know as João Miques now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians know him well because he has previously lived in Venice and has then been expelled over a strange affair we’ll get to. He is banished from Venice and risks his head if he returns. But he does not return. He stays in Constantinople, where he intrigues and makes money, and becomes one of the city’s richest and most influential men.

Marcantonio Barbaro is convinced that this man is so influential because he has learned how to seduce the sultan, who has his weaknesses. The sultan then is the son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman has reigned for many years and is feared throughout the world; then he dies and his son Selim reigns. Sultan Selim is completely different from his father. The Venetian ambassadors say that he is a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It is not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent’s son and then have to take his place. Selim gives audiences in the same palace room where his father has, but does not sit in the same place because he says he is unworthy. He sits farther back, in a corner.

One indication that Selim has a troubled personality is that, by common agreement, he is an alcoholic. Naturally he is the Ottoman sultan and caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople is a metropolis in which half the population consists of Christians and Jews, wine flows in rivers, and the Turks are not particularly strict about these prohibitions. In any case, the sultan is not. According to the Venetian ambassadors, he begins the day with a small carafe of spirits at breakfast and continues from there.

Barbaro says that, being a drunkard and also a glutton, Sultan Selim “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—always our Joseph Nasi—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” Thanks to this, says the Venetian ambassador, he wins the sultan’s confidence and incites him against us, against Venice, which these Jews hate. He therefore does everything possible to turn the sultan against us, being the head of his entire nation.

In Venetian eyes Joseph Nasi becomes an almost mythological figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews are spread throughout the world, and their leader therefore has information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence—the English word: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan knows the secrets of Christendom, especially those of Venice.

We should pause over this. You have recognized aspects of something that would return in more recent periods: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. Jews supposedly want to dominate the world; they are everywhere, connected to one another, and control the banks. This obsession is widespread in Renaissance Italy, particularly in Venice. It is a pathology, naturally. They imagine the power of these Jewish bankers to be far greater than it really is.

But it is also a pathology with a motive, because there is ferocious competition between Venetians and Jewish merchants. For the Venetians the Ottoman Empire is an indispensable commercial and financial partner. They have enormous investments there—in Constantinople, but also Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet by law the sultan allows the Venetian unbelievers to operate in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who therefore take a substantial share of the profits. Consequently, the Venetians brood upon the wickedness of these Jews and the danger they represent.

The theme recurs in the letters of Venetian ambassadors from Constantinople. They are struck that Jews who, where still present in Italy, are watched closely and have to be careful not to raise their heads too high, whereas in Constantinople these Jews lord it over everyone. Nobody troubles them in public; the sultan protects them. The ambassadors write: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Joseph Nasi conducts his business. Is it a legend that he supplies the sultan with wine? Perhaps, but it is certain that he holds the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a metropolis where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drink wine every day, the contract to supply wine is clearly substantial. Our young Joseph Nasi is a colossal businessman, but behind him stands his aunt. In the years I mentioned, 1568–70, when I encountered him, she is nearing the end of her life. Sixty is old then, and her young nephew Joseph is taking over the family affairs. Until shortly before, however, she has controlled everything.

## 3. From Spain to Portugal

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00240–U-00388 -->

“A Portuguese woman,” says a European envoy. This one is not Venetian but an envoy of the Fuggers. You know the Fugger bank, another financial colossus of the age, a German bank. They too naturally have envoys in Constantinople, and these men write: yes, yes, money circulates here, and it all passes through the hands of this Portuguese woman. To look at her, she resembles a European noblewoman; she dresses like a European princess and lives in the most unrestrained luxury, surrounded by servants.

This is our Gracia Nasi at sixty, in 1568. That is how I encountered her. Then I went back to see what had happened before: how she arrived there, how Gracia arrived, how Joseph arrived, and how this family arrived with its enormous wealth. Where do they come from? From far away. Their family has nothing to do with Constantinople. They come from Europe, from the West, from Spain. They are one of the very many Jewish families that live in medieval Spain.

That is partly because Spain is ruled by the Arabs during the Middle Ages, when Arabs and Muslims are more tolerant than Christians. Jews are therefore extremely numerous in Arab Spain and remain as the Christians gradually reconquer the country. You know that Spanish history has the epic of the *Reconquista*, which for them is in a certain sense the equivalent of our Risorgimento: it is what one learns at school. The nation is founded in that way, over centuries in which Catholic kings reconquer Spain piece by piece, progressively expelling the Arabs.

The Reconquista ends in a year that seems deliberately chosen as a watershed in history: 1492. I need not tell you what else happens that year, or who leaves Spain with three caravels. In that same year the Catholic Monarchs end the last Arab dominion, Granada. From then on Spain is entirely Christian; Muslims are expelled or forced to convert. At that point the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella and Ferdinand, decide to do the same to the Jews. We have purified Spain: there must be no more infidels or unbelievers; there are no more Muslims and there will be no more Jews either.

Think what a year 1492 is: on one side the world opens, while on the other this idea emerges forcefully of a Europe that wants to be pure, free of—well. The Jews must convert or be expelled. Very many resign themselves to conversion; very many others prefer to leave. Those leaving are told plainly: you may take your possessions, but no money. You cannot take the gold away.

Communities that have lived there for centuries now have to depart. They spend their final night in their cemeteries saying farewell to their ancestors, then leave forever. Where do they go? To the Islamic world, the Ottoman Empire, which at that time takes for granted that Jews and Christians are inferior to Muslims, but also holds—because it is written in the Quran—that if Jews and Christians cause no trouble, they can live among us.

And so Jews depart for the Ottoman Empire: Constantinople, Salonica, Sarajevo, Smyrna, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. All these places are under Ottoman rule and filled with Jews. Sultan Bayezid is said to have remarked: I have not understood that the king of Spain is a fool; clearly he has all these useful subjects and has sent them to me. These Jews are businesspeople, entrepreneurs, bankers. Of course, whether they could bring their capital is another question, though there are ways to bring something out.

Many others naturally remain and convert to Christianity. How many? You should know that this is still a delicate question in Spain, because from that moment the dominant ideology there is *limpieza de sangre*, purity of blood. The poorest Spanish peasant in the sixteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century—and perhaps even today—takes pride in saying: I am an Old Christian. I am not like these descendants of Jews or Muslims. To be a *cristiano viejo* means being proud of one’s pure blood.

So you understand the problem: how many Spaniards actually descend from those converts? A study of paternal lineages estimated that about 20 percent are of Sephardic Jewish origin and 10 percent of North African origin. [N-004] The research naturally causes a scandal in Spain, because nobody wants to be one of them.

For the extremely rich there is an alternative, however, because for the moment Portugal pursues a more flexible policy. If somebody is a great banker full of money, the kingdom of Portugal allows him to settle in its territory. That is what the Nasi family does. They move to Lisbon.

But it does not last. This is a kind of struggle against the spirit of the age. These perpetually fleeing Jews seek a place where they will be treated a little better, or at least tolerated. Yet the spirit of the age progressively leads one government after another to decide that it too has to follow Spain and drive the Jews out.

They remain in Lisbon for only five years. In 1497 Gracia has not yet been born; her parents are there. That year the king of Portugal also decides that he wants a pure country and therefore no Jews: convert or leave. Gracia’s family decides to convert to Christianity. They remain in Lisbon and officially become New Christians, *conversos*. People actually call them by another name, *marranos*. The word rapidly becomes an insult in Spanish and Portuguese, as it does in Italian—it evokes *The Three Musketeers* for us, I think: “vile marrano!” But *marrano* is originally a technical term meaning a converted Jew, one of the New Christians whom the Old Christians regard with suspicion and look down upon.

Naturally they also have to change surname. Enough of the Jewish surname Nasi; they need a different, Portuguese surname. They choose de Luna. And so in Lisbon, probably around 1510, Gracia is born. Now you understand why she has two names. She is baptized Beatriz, Beatriz de Luna, but the secret name used within the family is Gracia, a Hebrew name—or rather the Spanish translation of the Hebrew name Hannah. In secret, therefore, she is Gracia Nasi, while in the registry she is Beatriz de Luna.

## 4. Marriage, spices, and inheritance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00389–U-00484 -->

For years they live fairly peacefully in Portugal and do business. When Gracia is eighteen, she marries an extremely rich merchant, Francisco Mendes. Her name changes; now she is Beatriz Mendes, but I’ll spare you that. This rich merchant is naturally also a converted Jew, because people marry only within their own community. He is also a cousin, because when I say within their own community, I mean it literally. They are all Christians only in appearance; they have all remained faithful to Judaism, but in secret. They could not show it.

Why? Because the authorities watch, the neighbors spy, and the Inquisition checks. Do these people observe the feast days? Yes. They observe the Lenten fast, certainly—but they do eat pork, don’t they? If a neighbor notices that one week and then another passes and a particular family never buys any pork, suspicions arise and anonymous denunciations begin, because, as you know, Jews do not eat it.

But they are enormously rich, because in Lisbon in those years fabulous fortunes are made in commerce. Why? Because the Portuguese have discovered the shortest route to the Indies and could import the most precious commodity there is: spices. Spices are all the rage in the market and provoke genuine madness among rich people willing to pay anything for them. We find it a little surprising. Bear in mind that these people have no coffee, no tea, no spirits, no cocaine, and so forth. Spices replace all those things; they are a status symbol.

The Portuguese control the spice trade. Remember studying the geographical explorations at school? Vasco da Gama rounds the Cape of Good Hope and reaches Asia by passing below Africa. Why? To fetch spices. That route saves time, and enormous capital accumulated—capital then used for marriages between cousins.

Marriage between cousins is naturally forbidden. But if one applies to Rome for a license, explains the reasons, and accompanies the application with a sufficient number of bribes, one obtains permission to marry a cousin.

Thus Gracia marries at eighteen. Seven years later her husband dies. At twenty-five she is a widow. Her husband leaves a will establishing that his fortune will henceforth be administered jointly by his brother Diogo and his wife Gracia. That is not automatic. When a woman’s husband dies, she has the right to recover her dowry; naturally that belongs to her and forms the basis of her future survival as a widow. But it is by no means automatic for a husband also to make her heir to the family estate and officially charge her with managing it. This already tells us that Gracia evidently possesses particular qualities. Her husband has recognized them.

At twenty-five, therefore, Gracia administers an enormous commercial fortune together with her brother-in-law, Diogo, her husband’s brother, who represents the firm in Antwerp. Antwerp, on the coast of Flanders, is another place where money circulates in that age, one of the great commercial capitals of the Renaissance world. It is the capital of the Atlantic, perhaps Europe’s greatest commercial port at that moment.

Antwerp is in Flanders but belongs to Charles V, king of Spain and emperor of Germany, and is therefore one of Spain’s great ports. Yet unlike Spanish ports remote from the rest of Europe, it lies close to the German world, Central Europe, the north, and the British Isles. Most Spanish and Portuguese trade with Flanders, Germany, England, and the Baltic passes through Antwerp.

English wool arrives there before being taken to Italy for the Florentine textile industry. Wines arrive from France and Spain and are then traded in England and Germany. Timber comes from Scandinavia for shipyards throughout Europe. Gold and silver arrive too, among the principal commodities carried by the Spanish galleons from the American colonies. All that gold and silver reach Seville and Antwerp, where it is spent. The de Luna–Mendes firm therefore has its head office in Lisbon, but a large branch in Antwerp.

## 5. The Inquisition and the move to Antwerp

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00485–U-00600 -->

We say that Gracia is twenty-five; it is 1535. Until then they have lived quietly in Lisbon, provided they pretend to have converted to Christianity. The following year the machinery of persecution begins moving again. On 23 May 1536 the pope establishes the Portuguese Inquisition on the model of the Spanish Inquisition.

What is the Inquisition? In itself it is a tribunal found throughout the Catholic world, run by specialists charged with hunting heretics and false converts. In Spain, however, it is something special. Elsewhere it depends directly on Rome and the pope and has relatively calm and moderate procedures, so to speak: trials are sent to Rome and discussed. Spain has requested a state of emergency. Being full of converted Jews and Moriscos whom the authorities distrust, it obtains a special Inquisition that answers not to Rome but to the king and therefore operates locally much more swiftly and ruthlessly. The same institution is created in Portugal.

This gives the Inquisition enormous strength. In other Catholic countries it answers to Rome and has to reckon with local authorities. Those authorities might be thoroughly Catholic, but they do not like a tribunal operating in their territory while obeying Rome rather than the local state. They generally create all sorts of difficulties for it. Venice, for example, hates the Jews but dislikes the Inquisition too. We Venetian patricians do not like an inquisitor who questions, arrests, and condemns people without answering to the Most Serene Republic, so the Republic plays every possible trick on the Inquisition. The Spanish Inquisition, by contrast, depends directly on the king and does not have these problems. The same is true in Portugal.

It is 1536. Within a couple of years, Gracia leaves Lisbon for Antwerp. This too is in the Spanish Empire, but Lisbon, with its newly created Inquisition, has become dangerous for anyone who still does not eat pork and secretly practices Judaism at home. Gracia leaves and takes everything with her. The firm concentrates in Antwerp. She also brings her younger sister, Brianda. I warned you the cast of characters would eventually multiply.

In Antwerp there is Diogo—remember him?—the brother of Gracia’s dead husband, who manages the company. Now that everybody is reunited, what could be better than a fine marriage? Diogo Mendes marries Brianda. Francisco has married Gracia, and his brother Diogo marries Gracia’s sister.

Under Sephardic Jewish rules, when a man dies his brother should marry his widow, especially if there are no children. But Francisco and Gracia already have a daughter, also called Reyna. It is therefore not essential for Diogo to marry his brother’s widow; he could marry the widow’s sister instead, and everything still remains in the family.

There are extraordinary symmetries in this family’s history. Gracia has a daughter by Francisco called Anna—which itself would mean Gracia—but also officially called Reyna. Brianda, who marries her brother-in-law Diogo, likewise has a daughter whom she calls Gracia within the family, while in the registry she is naturally Beatriz, like her aunt. I assure you that historians too go mad over this, so do not worry too much. But notice the symmetry: two sisters marry two brothers, and each couple has a daughter.

Then Francisco dies. I do not know when Diogo notices how striking the asymmetry is, but Diogo dies as well. Now there are two widowed sisters, the elder Gracia and the younger Brianda, each with a little daughter. In his will Diogo makes his sister-in-law Gracia administrator of the entire family fortune. All of it. Technically, half belongs to Gracia and half to Brianda. The ownership is not in dispute, but Diogo places administration of the company and its capital entirely in Gracia’s hands.

This is a second piece of evidence that Gracia knows how to conduct business. We can only imagine how Brianda feels. She cannot have been pleased, as we’ll soon see. For the moment, there are more alarming matters. They are in Antwerp, certainly, but still within the Spanish Empire. And through that great port, Jews continually pass from one corner or another of the Christian world, having decided to leave it and reach the Muslim world, Constantinople.

## 6. The rescue network and the loan to Charles V

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00601–U-00684 -->

The best route is through Venice. From Antwerp a support network helps converted Jews—who in reality have not converted at all—reach Venice and from there Constantinople. The Jewish community in Antwerp, with its bankers and wealth, tries to assist these compatriots and co-religionists seeking refuge far from Christendom.

Gracia begins to emerge not only as a businesswoman and banker, but as the creator of a network that makes this passage easier. It has to be done as secretly as possible. If a rich Jew informed the Spanish authorities that he wished to leave, they would say: we are delighted that you are going, it was high time—go, but you will not take the money. To take it, he has to leave secretly and perform banking operations. This is where Gracia could help. Banking also means transferring capital through bills of exchange, cheques, and movements between accounts, getting money out from under Emperor Charles V’s nose.

The authorities consequently watch them. Soon after Diogo’s death an investigation begins, and the dead man is accused of having secretly remained a Jew: crypto-Judaism. He is dead, but his widow, sister-in-law, daughter, and niece are alive, and nobody knows what might happen if the inquiry continues.

At this level one also knows how to move. Why have the authorities target us? Can we do anything? It immediately emerges that something could be done. Emperor Charles V needs an interest-free loan of 100,000 ducats and has decided that these faithful New Christian subjects will certainly provide it. The Nasi, de Luna–Mendes firm manages to do so.

It is difficult to say what 100,000 ducats means. In a present-day equivalence one might say fifty million euros, which does not seem so very much. But then one could do more with 100,000 ducats than one can now with fifty million euros. One could build a fleet, field an army, and wage a war. Think what kind of world this is: bankers possess sums that states could not assemble without borrowing from those very bankers. A few years ago we might have called it incredible. Today we are no longer surprised that private finance can command wealth sometimes greater than that of certain states.

They hold out for a few more years. But when the emperor next demands another interest-free loan, the family frankly begins thinking of departure. In 1544 Gracia is thirty-four and fully head of the family. She, her sister, her daughter, and her niece leave Antwerp without announcing they will not return. They are going on holiday, to take the warm baths at Aachen. There are thermal springs there, you know; Charlemagne has gone to live there long before for a reason. He is old and in pain, and the warm baths are just what he needs. Many people go to take the waters.

The family leaves Antwerp for Aachen, then passes into France and goes to Lyon, and from Lyon to Venice.

## 7. Venice and the dispute over the fortune

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00685–U-00760 -->

In Venice they rent a palace on the Grand Canal, just to avoid attracting attention. Venice is naturally full of Jews, but they are shut inside the ghetto, a fairly recent invention. During the Middle Ages Jews have lived wherever they wish, even in Christian cities, but by the Renaissance they have to be watched and separated. Venice has had its ghetto since 1516, and Jews are required to live there.

But our women are not Jews, are they? They are called de Luna, Beatriz, and so forth. They are converted Christians and could live wherever they please: in a palace on the Grand Canal.

They have left part of the capital in Antwerp, having failed to remove everything: real estate, palaces, offices, and considerable capital, though they hope to recover some. They also leave the young nephew there. Here João Miques, later Joseph Nasi, enters the story. Through maneuvering, paying bribes, and obtaining recommendations, he recovers part of the capital and transfers it to Venetian banks. The following year, 1546, he too reaches Venice.

They are still in a violently antisemitic Christian world, but the Inquisition is less frightening in Venice than in Spain or Portugal, and Turkey is only a step away. One merely has to board a galley and after a few days is under the protection of the Grand Signor, the sultan. One could breathe in Venice—or so Gracia must have thought: perhaps now we can breathe for a moment.

It is then that Brianda challenges Gracia’s management of everything. She has endured it for a long time; now, enough. Venice has courts called the *Giudici del Forestier*, charged with hearing foreigners’ complaints and lawsuits. Before them Brianda objects: half the capital is mine, yet my sister manages all of it. She will not even let me see the accounts, using the will of my late husband, God rest his soul, as a pretext.

Brianda sues and the judges find for her. Gracia has to deposit half the family capital in the Venetian public treasury. For now, we’ll take it into custody. The judgment says that Brianda’s daughter Beatriz, then only eight, can withdraw that half when she turns eighteen. In essence, the authorities obtain half this immense fortune and will manage it for ten years. When the child is grown, they will see.

Gracia has probably expected something of the kind. There are two women with two daughters. Sooner or later some man will lay hands on the money by marrying one of the girls. Whoever marries them will inevitably control it. Gracia has realized this in Antwerp. Her own daughter Beatriz is not yet ten when approaches arrive from nothing less than a prince of the royal house, Don Francisco of Aragon, who lets it be known that perhaps a marriage can be discussed.

## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia manages to avert that. Years pass and now they are in Venice, but a rumor immediately circulates among young patricians: there are two Jewesses with two daughters who will inherit a fortune. One is Gracia’s and she keeps her under control, but the other is Brianda’s, and Brianda does not seem entirely sound in the head. Well-bred young men begin buzzing around, thinking that if they could catch the girl—

But that is not the only problem. The spirit of the age is against them. Whenever they arrive somewhere, after a while the age catches up. Christian governments are growing ever more intolerant.

They have been in Venice five years when, in 1550, it orders the expulsion of all *marranos*, all converted Spanish Jews. Evidently many have arrived, not only our little family. They are formally Christians, but the Venetian government could do as it pleases and decides it does not want converts whom nobody trusts. They must all leave.

Gracia has her connections. She remains a banker operating at the highest level, learns the measure is coming, and takes precautions. She finds a place in Italy where Jews could still live quietly: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife Renée of France.

Ferrara is extraordinary because Renée is Protestant. The Reformation has initially made considerable headway in France. Although eventually suppressed, for a long time very many French people—the Huguenots—are Protestant. In 1550 this story is only beginning; Huguenots are not yet persecuted, and some French princes and nobles are Protestant.

Duke Ercole has married a French Protestant princess, and at her court the Inquisition does not interfere. Renée protects Protestants and even receives Calvin, who would have been burned if seized elsewhere in Italy. Jews too are protected and arrive in large numbers.

All of you have had some occasion to notice that Ferrara is a capital of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Until Fascism and the Second World War, Ferrara has a large Jewish community, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there.

The family finds not only asylum. Under Renée’s protection Gracia can do something that would bring her to the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: publicly return to Judaism. She no longer hides, resumes Jewish practice and the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and rapidly becomes one of Ferrara’s most important Jewish figures.

At Ferrara they publish the Hebrew Bible translated into Spanish; she finances it. They make two editions, one dedicated to the duke and one to Gracia Nasi, who has supplied the money. Samuel Usque publishes a Portuguese work there, since so many of these Jews come from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel is afflicted and persecuted, but there is consolation. Here at Ferrara we have found it: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects and assists the Jews and helps them take refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—meaning the Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it could last? It could not. Pressure comes upon Ercole from every direction: the emperor, the pope, everybody. Your wife is a scandal; this must end. The duke resists and resists, but then has to explain to her that a Protestant cannot rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits intervene; Saint Ignatius of Loyola intervenes. Finally Renée surrenders, publicly converts to Catholicism, and abjures Protestantism.

Gracia decides it is time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection these things do not happen. Let us put an end to it once and for all. Very well—

## 9. Departure for Constantinople

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00877–U-00969 -->

They must go to Constantinople. First, however, Brianda has to be persuaded, and she does not agree. Second, what about all the money deposited in Venice while they wait for the heiress to grow up? They could hardly leave it there. They are not going to Constantinople without taking the money.

At this point we see who Gracia Nasi is and the level on which she operates. She contacts the sultan and his government, informs them that the entire family, company, and capital wish to move to Constantinople, and asks whether the Ottoman Empire could contact the Venetians and persuade them to reach an agreement. The sultan’s government begins negotiating with the Most Serene Republic over the emigration of the Nasi family and its capital.

They eventually agree. The Venetian government has frozen half the capital, an immense sum. They settle on something smaller. Gracia has to deposit 100,000 ducats in Venice—they like round numbers, as you see: fifty million euros—in the name of her niece Beatriz, since otherwise Brianda will not consent. But they allow her to take away the bulk of the capital. Gracia and her daughter go to Constantinople. Brianda and Beatriz return to Venice to wait until Beatriz is fifteen and can withdraw the money.

Young Venetian patricians increasingly buzz around her, wanting to marry her. But the young nephew Joseph Nasi reappears; he is the man called upon to untangle alarming situations. He secretly reaches Venice in January 1553. Beatriz is thirteen, with two years remaining before the capital becomes hers. Her cousin Joseph abducts her, takes her to Ravenna, and marries her there. Unfortunately, we have no precise information about whether Gracia knows; one might suppose she does.

It is a fine attempt, but matters are not so simple. Brianda sues: my nephew has carried off my daughter, abducted her, and married her against her will. The Venetian government annuls the marriage, has the girl returned to her mother, and banishes Joseph from Venice on pain of death. If you show yourself again, know that it will end badly.

Then we do not know exactly what happens. Fortunately Brianda must have opened her eyes, or they finally convince her through persistence. She and her daughter eventually join the family in Constantinople. There Joseph is circumcised, officially returns to Judaism, and becomes Joseph Nasi. Since his marriage to Brianda’s daughter has been annulled and another unmarried cousin remains, he marries Gracia’s daughter. Everything is settled perfectly—apart from the 100,000 ducats still in Venice in Beatriz’s name.

Beatriz and Brianda, moreover, continue traveling between Constantinople, Italy, and Ferrara; they are not settled. In 1555 Beatriz turns fifteen, and her mother dies soon afterward. Beatriz finally inherits the last portion of capital still in Venice in her name. She is then in Ferrara, and the money is brought from Venice.

I have not yet told you that Joseph also has a brother, Bernardo, now called Samuel because he too has returned to Judaism. Exactly one year after Brianda’s death—one must observe a year of mourning—Samuel comes to Ferrara, where his cousin Beatriz is with her 100,000 ducats, and marries her.

The sultan’s government then intervenes with the duke of Ferrara to obtain permission for everybody to go to Constantinople with the money. The duke negotiates for a while and finally yields. Officially, at last, the entire family and all the money move to Constantinople—

## 10. The Lady of the Ottoman Empire

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00970–U-00995 -->

—where, with all that money, they become the leading financial firm of the Ottoman Empire. For although the Ottoman Empire is ahead of the Christians in tolerance, it lags behind in commercial and financial techniques. There is nothing there comparable to the banking networks and credit mechanisms of Christian Europe.

That is why Sultan Bayezid had said many years before: how foolish the king of Spain is to send me these subjects of his; they are the only people arriving here who can establish banking among us as well.

And so Doña Gracia, supervising her nephews’ activities, continues to manage the bank, accumulate money, and protect Jews seeking escape from the Christian world into the Ottoman Empire. She builds synagogues. For a long time one in Constantinople is called the Lady’s Synagogue, where Portuguese Jews gather. She finances synagogues, Jewish hospitals, and rabbinical schools throughout the Ottoman Empire, and tries to protect Jews who remain in the Christian world too, though that is becoming ever more difficult.

## 11. The burnings at Ancona and the boycott

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00996–U-01046 -->

Then something terrible happens. Incredible though it may seem, there is a fairly prosperous Jewish community at Ancona, even in the Papal States. Although the pope promotes antisemitic policy more than anyone, these Jewish merchants and financiers are so valuable that for a long time the popes allowed openly unconverted Jews to live there. Alongside these Jews, watched and confined in the ghetto, there are also many converts. The pope himself prevents the Inquisition from troubling them.

But times change, the age advances, and it is always the same age. In 1555 the pope dies and Cardinal Carafa, the Inquisition’s principal supporter, becomes Pope Paul IV. He supports it completely and orders the arrest of all the Ancona conversos: former Jews from Spain and Portugal who pretend to be Christian, though the Inquisition knows perfectly well that this is untrue. It arrests them all.

Doña Gracia then intervenes with the sultan. Understand what kind of world this is. Powers that officially hate one another and officially have no contacts—the pope and the sultan—in reality meet, speak, and exert pressure on one another. Urged by Gracia, the sultan contacts the pope on behalf of the prisoners and asks for their release and the restoration of their property.

They negotiate at length. The pope eventually agrees to restore the money and lift the sequestration of the prisoners’ property, but refuses to release them. An example has to be made of people who pretend to become Christians but remain Jewish. In 1556 twenty-four Jews are burned at the stake in Ancona, among them the local agent of Gracia Nasi’s firm.

Gracia then contacts the entire international Jewish commercial community and proposed boycotting Ancona and doing no further business with the pope. This proves difficult to implement, because business is business in the end. Yet the very fact that she could conceive of it gives an idea of who she is.

## 12. Tiberias, death, and the legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01047–U-01115 -->

She is a person who, a few years later, in 1558—now we are nearing the end, and Gracia is growing old, but we must still tell this story—has an idea. The sultan is Süleyman the Magnificent, not yet his son Selim, the alcoholic with whom we begin. In 1558 Gracia discusses her idea with Süleyman and obtains his consent.

She leases a piece of the Holy Land: the region of Tiberias in Galilee, containing the ruins of the ancient city and many surrounding villages. Jews have not lived there since time immemorial, but Gracia’s idea is to bring them back to the Holy Land. With the sultan’s consent she leases Tiberias, builds a settlement, and begins transferring refugees arriving from the Christian world. In her own way, in short, she has also invented Zionism: the idea of returning the Jews to the Holy Land.

The settlement functions for a time, then does not last long. The Jews fleeing Europe are almost all merchants and financiers. What are they to do there in the Palestinian desert? The undertaking does not prosper, though the project is still discussed a few years later.

By then it is attributed to the nephew Joseph, who, as we have seen, is a kind of bogeyman to Westerners and Christians. Whenever he is involved, they fear the worst. A French ambassador in Constantinople writes that the Jew Miques, Joseph Nasi, has again obtained the sultan’s permission to build a city on Lake Tiberias where only Jews could live and that, as far as could be judged, he plans to make himself king of the Jews. Once again, you see what these people represent in the political imagination of the Christian world.

Every life eventually ends. In 1569 Doña Gracia falls ill and dies, leaving the baton to her nephew. For years he continues to manage the company and passes through difficult times. The defeat at Lepanto has gone down in Christian history as the moment when Christendom triumphs over the Muslim world, the Ottoman Empire. In reality it is certainly a grand and disastrous battle, but one that literally leaves matters as they are, because the Ottomans ultimately win that war and obtain what they want: Cyprus.

Joseph Nasi naturally obtains the monopoly on wine exports from Cyprus. Thus, as if the capital accumulated by his aunt has not been enough, the family continues to accumulate more.

It would be nice if I had a brilliant conclusion, but in truth I do not. That is the story. Gracia dies; that world ends and is far away. So many things have been overturned, including arguments about tolerance and the capacity to be tolerant. But in any case, I hope it seems a good story to you. Thank you.
