## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

I'll begin this story where I first came across Gracia myself, many years ago, while I was writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the famous battle: 1571, the great victory of the Venetian and Spanish Catholic fleets over the sultan's fleet. As I worked on the book and pieced together the background to the battle and the war, I ran into someone I'd never heard of—even though he was enormously famous in his own time.

His name is Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally, he has another name too: Miques, as the Venetians pronounce the Portuguese name in their own way. Like I said, this is a story about people with many names. Joseph Nasi is our Gracia's nephew—Doña Gracia, as everyone calls her. He's both her nephew and her son-in-law. These families tend to keep everything in the family, including marriage. Marry an outsider, and the dowry takes money out of the family. The money has to stay home.

I come across Joseph Nasi because he's famous in Constantinople in the years leading up to Lepanto, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, talks about him constantly in his reports. Venice has an ambassador in Constantinople who sends long letters home, keeping the government up to date on events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. Anyone can go read them, and I did. One of the Venetians' obsessions is finding out what Joseph Nasi is up to. They first know him as João Miques, so the ambassador writes of João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.”

Do you hear the judgment packed into “at present calls himself”? He has taken back his Jewish name. At first, he goes by one of our names. Now he's living among the more tolerant Turks, and he has reclaimed his Jewish one. This isn't just a matter of official records. That phrase passes judgment on a man the Venetians know as João Miques and who now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians know him well because he lives in Venice for a time, until he's expelled over a strange affair we'll get to later. He's banished, and if he comes back, he risks losing his head. But he doesn't come back. He stays in Constantinople, where he schemes, makes money, and becomes one of the richest and most influential men in the city.

Marcantonio Barbaro is convinced that Nasi has so much influence because he knows how to win over the sultan—and the sultan has his weaknesses. The sultan at this point is Selim, son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman reigns for years and inspires fear around the world. Then he dies, and Selim takes the throne. Selim is nothing like his father. The Venetian ambassadors describe him as a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It's not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent's son and then have to replace him. Selim holds audiences in the same room where his father did, but he won't sit in the same place because he says he isn't worthy. He sits farther back, in a corner.

One sign of Selim's troubled personality is that, by all accounts, he's an alcoholic. Now, he's the Ottoman sultan and the caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople is a huge city where half the population is Christian or Jewish, wine flows freely, and the Turks aren't especially strict about the ban. The sultan certainly isn't. The Venetian ambassadors report that he starts his day with a small carafe of liquor at breakfast and keeps going from there.

Barbaro says that Sultan Selim, being both a drunk and a glutton, “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—our Joseph Nasi again—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” This, the Venetian ambassador says, is how Nasi wins the sultan's trust and turns him against us—against Venice, which these Jews hate. As the leader of his entire people, Nasi does everything he can to set the sultan against us.

In Venetian eyes, Joseph Nasi becomes an almost mythical figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews live all over the world, so their leader must have information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence in the English sense: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan learns the secrets of Christendom, especially the secrets of Venice.

Let's stop here for a moment. You recognize parts of an idea that comes back in more recent times: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. The Jews supposedly want to rule the world. They're everywhere, they're all connected, and they control the banks. This obsession is widespread in Renaissance Italy, especially in Venice. It's a pathology, of course. The Venetians imagine these Jewish bankers have far more power than they really do.

But this pathology does have a motive behind it: fierce competition between Venetian and Jewish merchants. The Ottoman Empire is an essential trading and financial partner for Venice. The Venetians have huge investments there—not only in Constantinople, but also in Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet the sultan allows the Venetian unbelievers to do business in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who take a sizable cut of the profits. So the Venetians stew over how wicked these Jews are and what a threat they pose.

This theme keeps coming up in letters from the Venetian ambassadors in Constantinople. They're amazed by the contrast. In the parts of Italy where Jews are still allowed to live, they're watched closely and have to be careful not to get above themselves. In Constantinople, as the Venetians see it, Jews lord it over everyone. No one bothers them openly; the sultan protects them. The ambassadors write: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Meanwhile, Joseph Nasi goes about his business. Is the story that he supplies the sultan with wine just a legend? Maybe. But we know he holds the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a city where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drink wine every day, that's obviously a very big contract. Young Joseph Nasi is a business giant, but behind him stands his aunt. By the years I'm talking about, 1568 to 1570, she's nearing the end of her life. Sixty is old at the time, and little Joseph, the nephew, is taking over the family business. Until very recently, though, she controls everything.
