## 2. Joseph Nasi and the Venetian view

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00078–U-00239 -->

To tell you this story, I’ll begin with the moment I first encountered her, many years ago, while writing a book about the Battle of Lepanto. You know the celebrated Battle of Lepanto: 1571, the great victory of the Catholic fleets, Venetian and Spanish, over the sultan’s fleet. While working on that book and building the context for the battle and the war, I came across a figure I had never heard of, though he had been extremely famous in his own day.

He was called Giuseppe, or Joseph, Nasi. Naturally he had another name as well: Miques. The Venetians pronounced the Portuguese in their own way—and, as I said, this is a story of people with many names. This Joseph Nasi is the nephew of our Gracia, of Doña Gracia as everybody called her. He is her nephew and also her son-in-law. These were families inclined to keep everything within the family, marriages included, because if one marries outside, money leaves with the dowries, whereas the money has to remain at home.

I encountered Joseph Nasi because in the years before Lepanto he was famous in Constantinople, and the Venetian ambassador there, Marcantonio Barbaro, continually discussed him in his reports. The Venetians had an ambassador in Constantinople who wrote long letters to Venice informing the government about events in the Ottoman capital. Those letters are now in the Marciana Library in Venice. One can go and read them; I went and read them. One Venetian obsession was finding out what Joseph Nasi was doing. They had first known him as João Miques, and so the ambassador writes: João Miques, “who at present calls himself Don Joseph Nasi.” Do you understand what “at present calls himself” means? He had resumed his Jewish name. Before, he had one of our names; now that he was among the Turks, who were more tolerant, he had taken back his Jewish name. This is not merely a registry matter: there is an entire judgment contained in the statement that the man we knew as João Miques now calls himself Joseph Nasi.

The Venetians knew him well because he had previously lived in Venice and had then been expelled over a strange affair we’ll get to. He was banished from Venice and risked his head if he returned. But he did not return. He stayed in Constantinople, where he intrigued and made money, and became one of the city’s richest and most influential men.

Marcantonio Barbaro was convinced that this man was so influential because he had learned how to seduce the sultan, who had his weaknesses. The sultan then was the son of the great Süleyman the Magnificent. Süleyman had reigned for many years and was feared throughout the world; then he died and his son Selim reigned. Sultan Selim was completely different from his father. The Venetian ambassadors say that he was a tormented man with an inferiority complex. It is not easy to be Süleyman the Magnificent’s son and then have to take his place. Selim gave audiences in the same palace room where his father had, but did not sit in the same place because he said he was unworthy. He sat farther back, in a corner.

One indication that Selim had a troubled personality is that, by common agreement, he was an alcoholic. Naturally he was the Ottoman sultan and caliph, prince of the believers, and Islam forbids alcohol. But Constantinople was a metropolis in which half the population consisted of Christians and Jews, wine flowed in rivers, and the Turks were not particularly strict about these prohibitions. In any case, the sultan was not. According to the Venetian ambassadors, he began the day with a small carafe of spirits at breakfast and continued from there.

Barbaro says that, being a drunkard and also a glutton, Sultan Selim “greatly loves Miques the Jew”—always our Joseph Nasi—“inventor of exquisite foods and the most delicate drinks.” Thanks to this, says the Venetian ambassador, he won the sultan’s confidence and incited him against us, against Venice, which these Jews hate. He therefore did everything possible to turn the sultan against us, being the head of his entire nation.

In Venetian eyes Joseph Nasi became an almost mythological figure: the leader of all the Jews. Jews were spread throughout the world, and their leader therefore had information and contacts everywhere, “having intelligence in every place.” Intelligence—the English word: information, contacts. Through the Jews, then, the sultan knew the secrets of Christendom, especially those of Venice.

We should pause over this. You have recognized aspects of something that would return in more recent periods: the obsession with a Jewish conspiracy. Jews supposedly wanted to dominate the world; they were everywhere, connected to one another, and controlled the banks. This obsession was widespread in Renaissance Italy, particularly in Venice. It was a pathology, naturally. They imagined the power of these Jewish bankers to be far greater than it really was.

But it was also a pathology with a motive, because there was ferocious competition between Venetians and Jewish merchants. For the Venetians the Ottoman Empire was an indispensable commercial and financial partner. They had enormous investments there—in Constantinople, but also Syria, Egypt, and Alexandria. Yet by law the sultan allowed the Venetian unbelievers to operate in his empire only through Jewish intermediaries, who therefore took a substantial share of the profits. Consequently, the Venetians brooded upon the wickedness of these Jews and the danger they represented.

The theme recurs in the letters of Venetian ambassadors from Constantinople. They were struck that Jews who, where still present in Italy, were watched closely and had to be careful not to raise their heads too high, whereas in Constantinople these Jews lorded it over everyone. Nobody troubled them in public; the sultan protected them. The ambassadors wrote: “the insolence of these Jews is unfortunately great [...] the insolence of this rabble grows every day.”

Joseph Nasi conducted his business. Is it a legend that he supplied the sultan with wine? Perhaps, but it is certain that he held the contract for wine imported into Constantinople. In a metropolis where hundreds of thousands of Christians and Jews drank wine every day, the contract to supply wine was clearly substantial. Our young Joseph Nasi was a colossal businessman, but behind him stood his aunt. In the years I mentioned, 1568–70, when I encountered him, she was nearing the end of her life. Sixty was old then, and her young nephew Joseph was taking over the family affairs. Until shortly before, however, she had controlled everything.
