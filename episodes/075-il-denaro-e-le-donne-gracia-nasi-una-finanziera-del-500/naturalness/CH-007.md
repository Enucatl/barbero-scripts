## 7. Venice and the dispute over the fortune

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00685–U-00760 -->

In Venice they rented a palace on the Grand Canal, just to avoid attracting attention. Venice was naturally full of Jews, but they were shut inside the ghetto, a fairly recent invention. During the Middle Ages Jews had lived wherever they wished, even in Christian cities, but by the Renaissance they had to be watched and separated. Venice had had its ghetto since 1516, and Jews were required to live there.

But our women were not Jews, were they? They were called de Luna, Beatriz, and so forth. They were converted Christians and could live wherever they pleased: in a palace on the Grand Canal.

They had left part of the capital in Antwerp, having failed to remove everything: real estate, palaces, offices, and considerable capital, though they hoped to recover some. They also left the young nephew there. Here João Miques, later Joseph Nasi, enters the story. Through maneuvering, paying bribes, and obtaining recommendations, he recovered part of the capital and transferred it to Venetian banks. The following year, 1546, he too reached Venice.

They were still in a violently antisemitic Christian world, but the Inquisition was less frightening in Venice than in Spain or Portugal, and Turkey was only a step away. One merely had to board a galley and after a few days was under the protection of the Grand Signor, the sultan. One could breathe in Venice—or so Gracia must have thought: perhaps now we can breathe for a moment.

It was then that Brianda challenged Gracia’s management of everything. She had endured it for a long time; now, enough. Venice had courts called the *Giudici del Forestier*, charged with hearing foreigners’ complaints and lawsuits. Before them Brianda objected: half the capital is mine, yet my sister manages all of it. She will not even let me see the accounts, using the will of my late husband, God rest his soul, as a pretext.

Brianda sued and the judges found for her. Gracia had to deposit half the family capital in the Venetian public treasury. For now, we’ll take it into custody. The judgment said that Brianda’s daughter Beatriz, then only eight, could withdraw that half when she turned eighteen. In essence, the authorities obtained half this immense fortune and would manage it for ten years. When the child was grown, they would see.

Gracia had probably expected something of the kind. There were two women with two daughters. Sooner or later some man would lay hands on the money by marrying one of the girls. Whoever married them would inevitably control it. Gracia had realized this in Antwerp. Her own daughter Beatriz was not yet ten when approaches arrived from nothing less than a prince of the royal house, Don Francisco of Aragon, who let it be known that perhaps a marriage could be discussed.
