## 7. Venice and the dispute over the fortune

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00685–U-00760 -->

In Venice, they rent a palace on the Grand Canal—just to keep a low profile. Venice, of course, is full of Jews, but they’re confined to the ghetto, a fairly recent invention. In the Middle Ages, Jews live wherever they want, even in Christian cities. By the Renaissance, that’s no longer the case. Now they’re supposed to be watched and kept apart. Venice has had a ghetto since 1516, and Jews are required to live there.

But our women aren’t Jews, are they? Their names are de Luna, Beatriz, and so on. They’re converts to Christianity, so they can live wherever they like—including a palace on the Grand Canal.

They’ve had to leave part of their capital behind in Antwerp. They couldn’t get everything out: real estate, palaces, offices, and a great deal of money. But they hope to recover at least some of it. They also leave the young nephew behind. This is where João Miques, the future Joseph Nasi, enters the story. He wheels and deals, pays bribes, calls in recommendations, and manages to recover part of the capital and move it into Venetian banks. The next year, 1546, he joins the family in Venice.

They’re still in a violently antisemitic Christian world. But the Inquisition isn’t as frightening in Venice as it is in Spain or Portugal, and Turkey is just a short trip away. All you have to do is board a galley, and within a few days you’re under the protection of the Grand Signor, the sultan. In Venice, they can breathe—or at least that must be what Gracia thinks. Maybe now we can breathe for a while.

That’s when Brianda decides to challenge Gracia’s control over everything. She’s put up with it long enough. Now she’s had enough. Venice has courts called the *Giudici del Forestier*, which hear complaints and lawsuits brought by foreigners. Brianda takes her case to them: Half the capital is mine, but my sister controls all of it. She won’t even show me the accounts. She uses the will of my late husband, God rest his soul, as an excuse.

Brianda sues, and the judges side with her. Gracia has to deposit half the family’s capital in the Venetian treasury. We’ll hold on to it for now. The ruling says that Brianda’s daughter Beatriz, who is only eight, can withdraw that half when she turns eighteen. In other words, the Venetian authorities get their hands on half this enormous fortune and control it for the next ten years. Once the girl grows up, they’ll see what happens.

Gracia probably sees something like this coming. Here are two women, each with a daughter. Sooner or later, some man is going to get his hands on the money by marrying one of the girls. Whoever marries either one will control her fortune. Gracia has already figured this out in Antwerp. Her own daughter Beatriz isn’t even ten when none other than a royal prince, Don Francisco of Aragon, sends word that he might be open to discussing a marriage.
