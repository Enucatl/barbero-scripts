## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia manages to head that off. The years pass, and now the family is in Venice. But word quickly spreads among the young patricians: two Jewish women have two daughters who are going to inherit a fortune. Gracia keeps a close eye on her daughter. Brianda, though, doesn’t seem entirely sound of mind. So young men from good families start circling, thinking, If only I could land that girl—

But that’s not their only problem. The whole age is against them. Wherever they go, sooner or later it catches up with them. Christian governments are becoming more and more intolerant.

They’ve been in Venice for five years when, in 1550, the city orders all *marranos* expelled—all Spanish Jews who have converted to Christianity. Clearly, plenty of them have arrived, not just our little family. Officially, they’re Christians. But the Venetian government can do whatever it likes, and it decides it doesn’t want converts no one trusts. They all have to leave.

Gracia has connections. She’s still a banker at the very highest level, so she hears what’s coming and takes precautions. She finds a place in Italy where Jews can still live in peace: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife, Renée of France.

Ferrara is unusual because Renée is Protestant. At first, the Reformation makes real headway in France. It’s eventually crushed, but for a long time a great many French people—the Huguenots—are Protestant. In 1550, that story is just beginning. The Huguenots aren’t being persecuted yet, and some French princes and nobles are Protestant themselves.

Duke Ercole has married a French Protestant princess, and the Inquisition stays out of her court. Renée protects Protestants. She even welcomes Calvin, who would be burned at the stake if he were caught anywhere else in Italy. She protects Jews too, and they arrive in large numbers.

You’ve probably come across the idea that Ferrara is one of the great centers of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Ferrara has a large Jewish community right up to Fascism and the Second World War, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there today.

The family finds more than asylum there. Under Renée’s protection, Gracia can do something that would get her burned at the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: return to Judaism in public. She stops hiding. She practices Judaism again, goes back to the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and quickly becomes one of the leading figures in Ferrara’s Jewish community.

In Ferrara, they publish a Spanish translation of the Hebrew Bible, financed by Gracia. There are two editions: one dedicated to the duke, and one to Gracia Nasi, who put up the money. Samuel Usque also publishes a work in Portuguese there, since so many of these Jews come from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel suffers and faces persecution, but there is consolation. We’ve found it here in Ferrara: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects the Jews, helps them, and helps them find refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—the nation of Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it can last? Of course not. Ercole comes under pressure from every direction—the emperor, the pope, everyone. Your wife is a scandal. This has to stop. The duke holds out for as long as he can, but eventually he has to tell her that a Protestant can’t rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits step in. Saint Ignatius of Loyola himself steps in. Finally, Renée gives way. She publicly converts to Catholicism and renounces Protestantism.

Gracia decides it’s time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection, none of this happens. Let’s settle the matter once and for all. All right—
