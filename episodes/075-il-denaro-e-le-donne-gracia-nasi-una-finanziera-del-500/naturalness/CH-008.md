## 8. Expulsion and refuge in Ferrara

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00761–U-00876 -->

Gracia managed to avert that. Years passed and now they were in Venice, but a rumor immediately circulated among young patricians: there are two Jewesses with two daughters who will inherit a fortune. One is Gracia’s and she keeps her under control, but the other is Brianda’s, and Brianda does not seem entirely sound in the head. Well-bred young men began buzzing around, thinking that if they could catch the girl—

But that was not the only problem. The spirit of the age was against them. Whenever they arrived somewhere, after a while the age caught up. Christian governments were growing ever more intolerant.

They had been in Venice five years when, in 1550, it ordered the expulsion of all *marranos*, all converted Spanish Jews. Evidently many had arrived, not only our little family. They were formally Christians, but the Venetian government could do as it pleased and decided it did not want converts whom nobody trusted. They must all leave.

Gracia had her connections. She remained a banker operating at the highest level, learned the measure was coming, and took precautions. She found a place in Italy where Jews could still live quietly: Ferrara, ruled by Ercole d’Este and his wife Renée of France.

Ferrara was extraordinary because Renée was Protestant. The Reformation had initially made considerable headway in France. Although eventually suppressed, for a long time very many French people—the Huguenots—were Protestant. In 1550 this story was only beginning; Huguenots were not yet persecuted, and some French princes and nobles were Protestant.

Duke Ercole had married a French Protestant princess, and at her court the Inquisition did not interfere. Renée protected Protestants and even received Calvin, who would have been burned if seized elsewhere in Italy. Jews too were protected and arrived in large numbers.

All of you have had some occasion to notice that Ferrara is a capital of Italian Judaism. Think of Giorgio Bassani’s *The Garden of the Finzi-Continis*. Until Fascism and the Second World War, Ferrara had a large Jewish community, and the National Museum of Italian Judaism is still there.

The family found not only asylum. Under Renée’s protection Gracia could do something that would have brought her to the stake anywhere else in Italy or Spain: publicly return to Judaism. She no longer hid, resumed Jewish practice and the name Gracia Nasi instead of Beatriz de Luna, and rapidly became one of Ferrara’s most important Jewish figures.

At Ferrara they published the Hebrew Bible translated into Spanish; she financed it. They made two editions, one dedicated to the duke and one to Gracia Nasi, who had supplied the money. Samuel Usque published a Portuguese work there, since so many of these Jews came from Portugal: *Consolation for the Tribulations of Israel*. Israel is afflicted and persecuted, but there is consolation. Here at Ferrara we have found it: the Lady, Doña Gracia, protects and assists the Jews and helps them take refuge in the Ottoman Empire. She is, Usque says, “the heart of our Portuguese nation”—meaning the Portuguese conversos.

Do you think it could last? It could not. Pressure came upon Ercole from every direction: the emperor, the pope, everybody. Your wife is a scandal; this must end. The duke resisted and resisted, but then had to explain to her that a Protestant could not rule an Italian principality. The Jesuits intervened; Saint Ignatius of Loyola intervened. Finally Renée surrendered, publicly converted to Catholicism, and abjured Protestantism.

Gracia decided it was time to go to Constantinople. Under the Grand Signor’s protection these things did not happen. Let us put an end to it once and for all. Very well—
