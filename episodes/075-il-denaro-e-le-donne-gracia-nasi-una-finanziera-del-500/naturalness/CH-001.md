## 1. A woman, a family, many names

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00077 -->

Good evening, everyone. Tonight we're telling the story of a woman. It's a complicated story, with all kinds of threads running through it. And I do mean complicated: eventually, you're going to get lost. You're going to mix up one person with another. Just know that going in. There's no way around it.

Naturally, it's a story about money. This series is about economics, and in practical terms, that means money. In the Renaissance, the period we're talking about, it means actual sacks of gold ducats. So this is a story about family and money. And whenever you put family and money together—especially the question of who controls the money—you get conflict. People clash. They have very different ideas about what to do. This is the story of a great family that can protect its own, but can also tear itself apart in hatred and resentment.

It's also a story of intolerance and persecution, and of the Jewish people's stubborn resistance in the Renaissance Mediterranean. The pattern may be the opposite of what we'd expect today, given where we now see tolerance and intolerance in the world. At the time, Jews face intolerance in the Christian world and, by contrast, tolerance in the Muslim world.

The story moves through Portugal, Spain, Flanders, Italy, and Constantinople. Italy plays a central role, and at the time it's a place of extraordinary contradictions. It can offer refuge, but it can also—and more often does—bring danger and persecution.

Above all, though, this is the story of a woman—an exceptional woman in her own time, or any other. As we've heard, even today it's not exactly easy for a woman working at San Paolo to rise into management, though people are working on it. It certainly isn't any easier in the past. And yet the workings of family, inheritance, marriage, and wills can put a woman in charge of a family, a company, and an international business empire.

This woman's name is—well, she actually has several names. We'll use the Italian pronunciation and call her Gracia Nasi. The more accurate pronunciation would be Doña Gracia Nasi. But she's also known as Beatriz de Luna, and that's the name she uses for much of her life. In the end, though, almost everyone simply calls her the Lady.

This is a story about people who move from one world to another and one name to another—people with many names. Nasi is a Hebrew name. In biblical Hebrew, the word means leader or prince. Today it's the title of the president of the State of Israel: the *nasi*. A family that carries this name in the diaspora is clearly a prestigious one, a family of leaders that the community sees as noble and distinguished. The Nasis are believed to be descendants of King David.
