## 5. The Inquisition and the move to Antwerp

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00485–U-00600 -->

We said that Gracia was twenty-five; it was 1535. Until then they had lived quietly in Lisbon, provided they pretended to have converted to Christianity. The following year the machinery of persecution began moving again. On 23 May 1536 the pope established the Portuguese Inquisition on the model of the Spanish Inquisition.

What is the Inquisition? In itself it is a tribunal found throughout the Catholic world, run by specialists charged with hunting heretics and false converts. In Spain, however, it was something special. Elsewhere it depended directly on Rome and the pope and had relatively calm and moderate procedures, so to speak: trials were sent to Rome and discussed. Spain had requested a state of emergency. Being full of converted Jews and Moriscos whom the authorities distrusted, it obtained a special Inquisition that answered not to Rome but to the king and therefore operated locally much more swiftly and ruthlessly. The same institution was created in Portugal.

This gave the Inquisition enormous strength. In other Catholic countries it answered to Rome and had to reckon with local authorities. Those authorities might be thoroughly Catholic, but they did not like a tribunal operating in their territory while obeying Rome rather than the local state. They generally created all sorts of difficulties for it. Venice, for example, hated the Jews but disliked the Inquisition too. We Venetian patricians do not like an inquisitor who questions, arrests, and condemns people without answering to the Most Serene Republic, so the Republic played every possible trick on the Inquisition. The Spanish Inquisition, by contrast, depended directly on the king and did not have these problems. The same was true in Portugal.

It was 1536. Within a couple of years, Gracia left Lisbon for Antwerp. This too was in the Spanish Empire, but Lisbon, with its newly created Inquisition, had become dangerous for anyone who still did not eat pork and secretly practiced Judaism at home. Gracia left and took everything with her. The firm concentrated in Antwerp. She also brought her younger sister, Brianda. I warned you the cast of characters would eventually multiply.

In Antwerp there was Diogo—remember him?—the brother of Gracia’s dead husband, who managed the company. Now that everybody was reunited, what could be better than a fine marriage? Diogo Mendes married Brianda. Francisco had married Gracia, and his brother Diogo married Gracia’s sister.

Under Sephardic Jewish rules, when a man died his brother ought to marry his widow, especially if there were no children. But Francisco and Gracia already had a daughter, also called Reyna. It was therefore not essential for Diogo to marry his brother’s widow; he could marry the widow’s sister instead, and everything still remained in the family.

There are extraordinary symmetries in this family’s history. Gracia had a daughter by Francisco called Anna—which itself would mean Gracia—but also officially called Reyna. Brianda, who married her brother-in-law Diogo, likewise had a daughter whom she called Gracia within the family, while in the registry she was naturally Beatriz, like her aunt. I assure you that historians too go mad over this, so do not worry too much. But notice the symmetry: two sisters married two brothers, and each couple had a daughter.

Then Francisco died. I do not know when Diogo noticed how striking the asymmetry was, but Diogo died as well. Now there were two widowed sisters, the elder Gracia and the younger Brianda, each with a little daughter. In his will Diogo made his sister-in-law Gracia administrator of the entire family fortune. All of it. Technically, half belonged to Gracia and half to Brianda. The ownership was not in dispute, but Diogo placed administration of the company and its capital entirely in Gracia’s hands.

This is a second piece of evidence that Gracia knew how to conduct business. We can only imagine how Brianda felt. She cannot have been pleased, as we’ll soon see. For the moment, there were more alarming matters. They were in Antwerp, certainly, but still within the Spanish Empire. And through that great port, Jews continually passed from one corner or another of the Christian world, having decided to leave it and reach the Muslim world, Constantinople.
