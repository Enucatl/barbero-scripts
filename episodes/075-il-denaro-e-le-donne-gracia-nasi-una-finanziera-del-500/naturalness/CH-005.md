## 5. The Inquisition and the move to Antwerp

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00485–U-00600 -->

Gracia is twenty-five, so the year is 1535. Up to now, the family has lived quietly in Lisbon, as long as they pretend to be Christian converts. The next year, the machinery of persecution starts moving again. On May 23, 1536, the pope establishes the Portuguese Inquisition, modeled on the Spanish one.

What is the Inquisition? Basically, it's a court found throughout the Catholic world, staffed by specialists whose job is to hunt down heretics and false converts. But in Spain, it's something different. Elsewhere, the Inquisition answers directly to Rome and the pope and follows relatively calm, moderate procedures, so to speak. Cases are sent to Rome and debated. Spain has asked for emergency powers. The country is full of converted Jews and Moriscos whom the authorities don't trust, so it gets a special Inquisition. This one answers to the king, not Rome, and can act much faster and more ruthlessly on the ground. Portugal now gets the same institution.

That gives the Inquisition enormous power. In other Catholic countries, it answers to Rome and has to deal with local authorities. Those authorities may be thoroughly Catholic, but they don't like having a court operate in their territory while taking orders from Rome instead of their own government. So they generally throw up every obstacle they can. Venice, for example, hates the Jews, but it doesn't care for the Inquisition either. We Venetian patricians don't want some inquisitor questioning, arresting, and sentencing people without answering to the Most Serene Republic. So the Republic uses every trick in the book to frustrate the Inquisition. The Spanish Inquisition, by contrast, answers directly to the king and doesn't have that problem. The same goes for Portugal.

It's 1536. Within a couple of years, Gracia leaves Lisbon for Antwerp. Antwerp is also part of the Spanish Empire, but with the new Inquisition, Lisbon has become a dangerous place for anyone who still won't eat pork and secretly practices Judaism at home. Gracia leaves and takes everything with her. The firm's operations move to Antwerp. She brings along her younger sister, Brianda. I warned you the cast would start multiplying.

Diogo is already in Antwerp—remember him? He's the brother of Gracia's late husband, and he runs the company there. Now that everyone's together again, what could be better than a nice wedding? Diogo Mendes marries Brianda. Francisco marries Gracia, and Francisco's brother Diogo marries Gracia's sister.

Under Sephardic Jewish law, when a man dies, his brother is supposed to marry his widow, especially if the couple has no children. But Francisco and Gracia already have a daughter, also known as Reyna. So Diogo doesn't have to marry his brother's widow. He can marry the widow's sister instead, and everything still stays in the family.

The symmetries in this family's history are remarkable. Gracia and Francisco have a daughter they call Anna—a name that itself means Gracia—but her official name is Reyna. Brianda, who marries her brother-in-law Diogo, has a daughter too. At home they call the girl Gracia, while in the official records she's naturally Beatriz, like her aunt. I promise, historians go crazy over this too, so don't worry about it too much. Just notice the symmetry: two sisters marry two brothers, and each couple has a daughter.

Then Francisco dies. I don't know when Diogo notices the glaring lack of symmetry, but he dies too. Now we have two widowed sisters, the older Gracia and the younger Brianda, each with a little girl. In his will, Diogo puts his sister-in-law Gracia in charge of the entire family fortune. All of it. Technically, half belongs to Gracia and half to Brianda. No one disputes the ownership. But Diogo puts Gracia alone in charge of the company and its capital.

This is our second sign that Gracia knows how to run a business. We can only imagine how Brianda feels. She can't be too happy, as we'll soon see. For now, though, there are more urgent things to worry about. Yes, they're in Antwerp, but they're still inside the Spanish Empire. And Jews are constantly passing through this great port from every corner of the Christian world, determined to leave it behind and reach the Muslim world—to reach Constantinople.
