## 3. From Spain to Portugal

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00240–U-00388 -->

“A Portuguese woman,” says a European envoy. This one was not Venetian but an envoy of the Fuggers. You know the Fugger bank, another financial colossus of the age, a German bank. They too naturally had envoys in Constantinople, and these men wrote: yes, yes, money circulates here, and it all passes through the hands of this Portuguese woman. To look at her, she resembles a European noblewoman; she dresses like a European princess and lives in the most unrestrained luxury, surrounded by servants.

This is our Gracia Nasi at sixty, in 1568. That is how I encountered her. Then I went back to see what had happened before: how she arrived there, how Gracia arrived, how Joseph arrived, and how this family arrived with its enormous wealth. Where did they come from? From far away. Their family had nothing to do with Constantinople. They came from Europe, from the West, from Spain. They were one of the very many Jewish families that lived in medieval Spain.

That was partly because Spain was ruled by the Arabs during the Middle Ages, when Arabs and Muslims were more tolerant than Christians. Jews were therefore extremely numerous in Arab Spain and remained as the Christians gradually reconquered the country. You know that Spanish history has the epic of the *Reconquista*, which for them is in a certain sense the equivalent of our Risorgimento: it is what one learns at school. The nation was founded in that way, over centuries in which Catholic kings reconquered Spain piece by piece, progressively expelling the Arabs.

The Reconquista ended in a year that seems deliberately chosen as a watershed in history: 1492. I need not tell you what else happened that year, or who left Spain with three caravels. In that same year the Catholic Monarchs ended the last Arab dominion, Granada. From then on Spain was entirely Christian; Muslims were expelled or forced to convert. At that point the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella and Ferdinand, decided to do the same to the Jews. We have purified Spain: there must be no more infidels or unbelievers; there are no more Muslims and there will be no more Jews either.

Think what a year 1492 was: on one side the world opens, while on the other this idea emerges forcefully of a Europe that wants to be pure, free of—well. The Jews must convert or be expelled. Very many resigned themselves to conversion; very many others preferred to leave. Those leaving were told plainly: you may take your possessions, but no money. You cannot take the gold away.

Communities that had lived there for centuries now had to depart. They spent their final night in their cemeteries saying farewell to their ancestors, then left forever. Where did they go? To the Islamic world, the Ottoman Empire, which at that time took for granted that Jews and Christians were inferior to Muslims, but also held—because it is written in the Quran—that if Jews and Christians caused no trouble, they could live among us.

And so Jews departed for the Ottoman Empire: Constantinople, Salonica, Sarajevo, Smyrna, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. All these places were under Ottoman rule and filled with Jews. Sultan Bayezid is said to have remarked: I had not understood that the king of Spain was a fool; clearly he had all these useful subjects and has sent them to me. These Jews were businesspeople, entrepreneurs, bankers. Of course, whether they could bring their capital was another question, though there were ways to bring something out.

Many others naturally remained and converted to Christianity. How many? You should know that this is still a delicate question in Spain, because from that moment the dominant ideology there was *limpieza de sangre*, purity of blood. The poorest Spanish peasant in the sixteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century—and perhaps even today—took pride in saying: I am an Old Christian. I am not like these descendants of Jews or Muslims. To be a *cristiano viejo* meant being proud of one’s pure blood.

So you understand the problem: how many Spaniards actually descend from those converts? A study of paternal lineages estimated that about 20 percent were of Sephardic Jewish origin and 10 percent of North African origin. [N-004] The research naturally caused a scandal in Spain, because nobody wants to be one of them.

For the extremely rich there was an alternative, however, because for the moment Portugal pursued a more flexible policy. If somebody was a great banker full of money, the kingdom of Portugal allowed him to settle in its territory. That is what the Nasi family did. They moved to Lisbon.

But it did not last. This was a kind of struggle against the spirit of the age. These perpetually fleeing Jews sought a place where they would be treated a little better, or at least tolerated. Yet the spirit of the age progressively led one government after another to decide that it too had to follow Spain and drive the Jews out.

They remained in Lisbon for only five years. In 1497 Gracia had not yet been born; her parents were there. That year the king of Portugal also decided that he wanted a pure country and therefore no Jews: convert or leave. Gracia’s family decided to convert to Christianity. They remained in Lisbon and officially became New Christians, *conversos*. People actually called them by another name, *marranos*. The word rapidly became an insult in Spanish and Portuguese, as it did in Italian—it evokes *The Three Musketeers* for us, I think: “vile marrano!” But *marrano* was originally a technical term meaning a converted Jew, one of the New Christians whom the Old Christians regarded with suspicion and looked down upon.

Naturally they also had to change surname. Enough of the Jewish surname Nasi; they needed a different, Portuguese surname. They chose de Luna. And so in Lisbon, probably around 1510, Gracia was born. Now you understand why she had two names. She was baptized Beatriz, Beatriz de Luna, but the secret name used within the family was Gracia, a Hebrew name—or rather the Spanish translation of the Hebrew name Hannah. In secret, therefore, she was Gracia Nasi, while in the registry she was Beatriz de Luna.
