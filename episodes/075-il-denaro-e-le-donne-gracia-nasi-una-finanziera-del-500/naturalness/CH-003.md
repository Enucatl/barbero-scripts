## 3. From Spain to Portugal

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00240–U-00388 -->

“A Portuguese woman,” says a European envoy. He's not Venetian; he works for the Fuggers. You know the Fugger bank, another financial powerhouse of the age, based in Germany. Naturally, the Fuggers have agents in Constantinople too, and those agents write home: yes, there's plenty of money moving around here, and all of it passes through the hands of this Portuguese woman. She looks like a European noblewoman, dresses like a European princess, and lives in extravagant luxury, surrounded by servants.

This is our Gracia Nasi at sixty, in 1568. That's how I first encounter her. Then I go back to find out what happens earlier. How does she get there? How does Joseph get there? How does this family arrive with such enormous wealth? Where do they come from? A long way off. The family has no roots in Constantinople. They come from Western Europe, from Spain. They're one of the many Jewish families living in Spain during the Middle Ages.

One reason is that much of Spain is ruled by Arabs during the Middle Ages, when Arabs and Muslims are more tolerant than Christians. Large numbers of Jews live in Muslim Spain, and they stay as Christians gradually reconquer the country. Spanish history has the great epic of the *Reconquista*. In a sense, it's their equivalent of our Risorgimento, the story everyone learns in school. This is how the nation is founded: over centuries, Catholic kings take Spain back piece by piece and drive the Arabs out.

The Reconquista ends in a year that seems almost chosen to mark a turning point in history: 1492. I don't need to tell you what else happens that year, or who sails from Spain with three caravels. That same year, the Catholic Monarchs conquer Granada, the last Muslim kingdom. From that point on, all of Spain is Christian. Muslims are expelled or forced to convert. Then Isabella and Ferdinand, the Catholic Monarchs, decide to do the same thing to the Jews. We've purified Spain. There can be no more infidels, no more unbelievers. There are no Muslims left, and soon there will be no Jews either.

Think about what kind of year 1492 is. On one side, the world opens up. On the other, we see this powerful vision of a Europe that wants to be pure, free of—well. The Jews have to convert or face expulsion. Many give in and convert; many others choose to leave. And the message to those leaving is clear: you can take your belongings, but no money. The gold stays here.

Communities that have lived there for centuries now have to go. They spend their last night in the cemeteries, saying goodbye to their ancestors, and then leave forever. Where do they go? To the Islamic world, to the Ottoman Empire. At the time, the empire takes it for granted that Jews and Christians are inferior to Muslims. But the Quran also teaches that as long as Jews and Christians cause no trouble, they can live among us.

So Jews leave for the Ottoman Empire: Constantinople, Salonica, Sarajevo, Smyrna, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. All these places are under Ottoman rule, and they fill with Jewish refugees. Sultan Bayezid is said to remark: I hadn't realized the king of Spain was a fool. He had all these useful subjects, and now he's sending them to me. These Jews are merchants, entrepreneurs, and bankers. Whether they can bring their capital with them is another question, of course. Still, there are ways to get some of it out.

Many others stay and convert to Christianity. How many? You should know this is still a sensitive question in Spain. From this point on, the country's dominant ideology is *limpieza de sangre*, purity of blood. Even the poorest Spanish peasant in the sixteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century—and perhaps even today—takes pride in saying: I'm an Old Christian. I'm not like those descendants of Jews or Muslims. To be a *cristiano viejo* is to take pride in your pure blood.

So you see the problem: how many Spaniards really descend from those converts? A study of paternal lineages estimates that about 20 percent are of Sephardic Jewish origin and 10 percent of North African origin. [N-004] Naturally, the research causes an uproar in Spain. Nobody wants to be one of them.

The very rich do have another option, though. For the moment, Portugal takes a more flexible approach. If you're a major banker with plenty of money, the kingdom of Portugal will let you settle there. That's what the Nasi family does. They move to Lisbon.

But it doesn't last. They're fighting the spirit of the age. These Jews, always on the run, keep looking for somewhere they'll be treated a little better, or at least tolerated. But one government after another eventually decides it has to follow Spain's example and drive them out.

They stay in Lisbon for only five years. In 1497, before Gracia is born, her parents are living there. That year, the king of Portugal decides he wants a pure country too, which means no Jews: convert or leave. Gracia's family converts to Christianity. They stay in Lisbon and officially become New Christians, or *conversos*. But people call them something else: *marranos*. The word quickly becomes an insult in Spanish and Portuguese, and in Italian too. For us, I think, it brings to mind *The Three Musketeers*: “vile marrano!” Originally, though, *marrano* is a technical term for a converted Jew, one of the New Christians the Old Christians distrust and look down on.

Naturally, they have to change their last name too. They can't go on using the Jewish name Nasi; they need a Portuguese one. They choose de Luna. And so Gracia is born in Lisbon, probably around 1510. Now you can see why she has two names. She's baptized Beatriz—Beatriz de Luna—but inside the family they secretly call her Gracia, a Hebrew name, or rather the Spanish translation of the Hebrew name Hannah. In private, then, she's Gracia Nasi. In the official records, she's Beatriz de Luna.
