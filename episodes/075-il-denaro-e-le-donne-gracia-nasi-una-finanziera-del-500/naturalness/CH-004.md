## 4. Marriage, spices, and inheritance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00389–U-00484 -->

For years, they live fairly quietly in Portugal and do business. When Gracia turns eighteen, she marries an extremely rich merchant named Francisco Mendes. Her name changes again—now she's Beatriz Mendes—but I'll spare you that. Naturally, Francisco is also a converted Jew, because people marry within the community. He's also her cousin, because when I say within the community, I mean very much within the community. In truth, they're all Christians for show. They've stayed faithful to Judaism, but they have to practice in secret.

Why? Because the authorities are watching, the neighbors are spying, and the Inquisition is checking. Do these people observe Christian feast days? Yes. They fast during Lent, of course. But they eat pork too, don't they? If a neighbor notices that one week goes by, then another, and a certain family never buys any pork, people start to wonder. Then the anonymous accusations begin, because Jews, as you know, don't eat pork.

But they're enormously rich, because fabulous fortunes are being made in Lisbon's trade at the time. Why? The Portuguese have found the shortest route to the Indies, and now they can import the most valuable goods of all: spices. Spices are the craze of the day. Rich people go wild for them and will pay any price. That may seem a little strange to us, but remember: these people have no coffee, no tea, no hard liquor, no cocaine, and so on. Spices fill the place of all those things. They're a status symbol.

The Portuguese control the spice trade. Remember learning about the great voyages of exploration in school? Vasco da Gama rounds the Cape of Good Hope and reaches Asia by sailing around the bottom of Africa. Why? To get spices. The route saves time, enormous fortunes pile up—and then that money goes toward marriages between cousins.

Marriage between cousins is forbidden, of course. But if you apply to Rome for permission, explain your reasons, and send enough bribes along with the application, you get permission to marry your cousin.

So Gracia marries at eighteen. Seven years later, her husband dies. She's a widow at twenty-five. His will says that from now on, his brother Diogo and his wife Gracia will manage his fortune together. That isn't automatic. When a woman's husband dies, she has the right to take back her dowry. It's hers, after all, and it's what she'll live on as a widow. But a husband doesn't automatically make his wife an heir to the family fortune, much less formally put her in charge of managing it. This already tells us that Gracia has unusual abilities. Her husband sees them.

So at twenty-five, Gracia is managing an enormous business fortune alongside her brother-in-law Diogo, her husband's brother, who represents the firm in Antwerp. Antwerp, on the coast of Flanders, is another place awash in money. It's one of the great trading capitals of the Renaissance world—the capital of the Atlantic, perhaps the busiest commercial port in Europe at the time.

Antwerp is in Flanders, but it belongs to Charles V, king of Spain and emperor of Germany, so it's one of Spain's great ports. Unlike the ports in Spain itself, far from the rest of Europe, Antwerp is close to the German-speaking world, Central Europe, northern Europe, and the British Isles. Most Spanish and Portuguese trade with Flanders, Germany, England, and the Baltic passes through Antwerp.

English wool arrives there on its way to Italy and the Florentine textile industry. Wine comes in from France and Spain, then goes on to England and Germany. Timber comes from Scandinavia for shipyards across Europe. Gold and silver arrive too, among the main cargo carried by Spanish galleons from the American colonies. All that gold and silver flows into Seville and Antwerp, where it gets spent. So the de Luna–Mendes firm has its headquarters in Lisbon and a major branch in Antwerp.
