## 11. The burnings at Ancona and the boycott

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00996–U-01046 -->

Then something terrible happened. Incredible though it may seem, there was a fairly prosperous Jewish community at Ancona, even in the Papal States. Although the pope promoted antisemitic policy more than anyone, these Jewish merchants and financiers were so valuable that for a long time the popes allowed openly unconverted Jews to live there. Alongside these Jews, watched and confined in the ghetto, there were also many converts. The pope himself prevented the Inquisition from troubling them.

But times change, the age advances, and it is always the same age. In 1555 the pope died and Cardinal Carafa, the Inquisition’s principal supporter, became Pope Paul IV. He supported it completely and ordered the arrest of all the Ancona conversos: former Jews from Spain and Portugal who pretended to be Christian, though the Inquisition knew perfectly well that this was untrue. It arrested them all.

Doña Gracia then intervened with the sultan. Understand what kind of world this was. Powers that officially hated one another and officially had no contacts—the pope and the sultan—in reality met, spoke, and exerted pressure on one another. Urged by Gracia, the sultan contacted the pope on behalf of the prisoners and asked for their release and the restoration of their property.

They negotiated at length. The pope eventually agreed to restore the money and lift the sequestration of the prisoners’ property, but refused to release them. An example had to be made of people who pretended to become Christians but remained Jewish. In 1556 twenty-four Jews were burned at the stake in Ancona, among them the local agent of Gracia Nasi’s firm.

Gracia then contacted the entire international Jewish commercial community and proposed boycotting Ancona and doing no further business with the pope. This proved difficult to implement, because business is business in the end. Yet the very fact that she could conceive of it gives an idea of who she was.
