## 9. Departure for Constantinople

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00877–U-00969 -->

They have to go to Constantinople. But first they need to convince Brianda, and she’s against it. Then there’s all the money sitting in Venice while they wait for the heiress to grow up. What happens to that? They can’t just leave it behind. They’re not moving to Constantinople without the money.

This is where we see who Gracia Nasi really is—and the level she operates at. She contacts the sultan and his government. She tells them that the whole family wants to move to Constantinople, along with the business and its capital, and asks the Ottoman government to approach the Venetians and help work out a deal. So the sultan’s government begins negotiating with the Most Serene Republic over the Nasi family’s departure and the transfer of its money.

Eventually, they reach an agreement. The Venetian government has frozen half the capital, an enormous sum, but now they settle on a smaller amount. Gracia has to leave 100,000 ducats in Venice in the name of her niece Beatriz. They like round numbers, as you can see: fifty million euros. Without that, Brianda won’t agree. But Gracia is allowed to take most of the capital with her. She and her daughter leave for Constantinople, while Brianda and Beatriz return to Venice and wait for Beatriz to turn fifteen, when she can withdraw the money.

More and more young Venetian patricians circle around Beatriz, hoping to marry her. But then the young nephew, Joseph Nasi, reappears. He’s the man they call when a dangerous situation needs sorting out. He slips into Venice in January 1553. Beatriz is thirteen, with two years to go before the money becomes hers. Her cousin Joseph abducts her, takes her to Ravenna, and marries her there. Unfortunately, we don’t know for sure whether Gracia knows about the plan. You might imagine she does.

It’s a bold attempt, but things aren’t that simple. Brianda sues: My nephew carried off my daughter, abducted her, and married her against her will. The Venetian government annuls the marriage, returns the girl to her mother, and banishes Joseph from Venice on pain of death. Show your face here again, and it won’t end well.

After that, we don’t know exactly what happens. Brianda must finally see sense—or perhaps they simply wear her down. In any case, she and her daughter eventually join the family in Constantinople. There, Joseph is circumcised, formally returns to Judaism, and becomes Joseph Nasi. His marriage to Brianda’s daughter has been annulled, and he still has another unmarried cousin, so he marries Gracia’s daughter. Everything works out perfectly—except for the 100,000 ducats still sitting in Venice under Beatriz’s name.

Meanwhile, Beatriz and Brianda keep traveling between Constantinople, Italy, and Ferrara. They haven’t settled anywhere. In 1555, Beatriz turns fifteen, and her mother dies soon afterward. Beatriz finally inherits the last share of the capital still held for her in Venice. She’s in Ferrara by then, and the money is brought to her from Venice.

I haven’t told you yet that Joseph also has a brother, Bernardo. He now goes by Samuel because he too has returned to Judaism. Exactly one year after Brianda’s death—you have to observe a year of mourning—Samuel comes to Ferrara, where his cousin Beatriz is waiting with her 100,000 ducats, and marries her.

The sultan’s government then approaches the duke of Ferrara and asks him to let everyone leave for Constantinople with the money. The duke negotiates for a while, then finally gives in. At long last, the whole family and all its money officially move to Constantinople—
