## 9. Departure for Constantinople

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00877–U-00969 -->

They must go to Constantinople. First, however, Brianda had to be persuaded, and she did not agree. Second, what about all the money deposited in Venice while they waited for the heiress to grow up? They could hardly leave it there. They were not going to Constantinople without taking the money.

At this point we see who Gracia Nasi was and the level on which she operated. She contacted the sultan and his government, informed them that the entire family, company, and capital wished to move to Constantinople, and asked whether the Ottoman Empire could contact the Venetians and persuade them to reach an agreement. The sultan’s government began negotiating with the Most Serene Republic over the emigration of the Nasi family and its capital.

They eventually agreed. The Venetian government had frozen half the capital, an immense sum. They settled on something smaller. Gracia had to deposit 100,000 ducats in Venice—they liked round numbers, as you see: fifty million euros—in the name of her niece Beatriz, since otherwise Brianda would not consent. But they allowed her to take away the bulk of the capital. Gracia and her daughter went to Constantinople. Brianda and Beatriz returned to Venice to wait until Beatriz was fifteen and could withdraw the money.

Young Venetian patricians increasingly buzzed around her, wanting to marry her. But the young nephew Joseph Nasi reappeared; he was the man called upon to untangle alarming situations. He secretly reached Venice in January 1553. Beatriz was thirteen, with two years remaining before the capital became hers. Her cousin Joseph abducted her, took her to Ravenna, and married her there. Unfortunately, we have no precise information about whether Gracia knew; one might suppose she did.

It was a fine attempt, but matters were not so simple. Brianda sued: my nephew has carried off my daughter, abducted her, and married her against her will. The Venetian government annulled the marriage, had the girl returned to her mother, and banished Joseph from Venice on pain of death. If you show yourself again, know that it will end badly.

Then we do not know exactly what happened. Fortunately Brianda must have opened her eyes, or they finally convinced her through persistence. She and her daughter eventually joined the family in Constantinople. There Joseph was circumcised, officially returned to Judaism, and became Joseph Nasi. Since his marriage to Brianda’s daughter had been annulled and another unmarried cousin remained, he married Gracia’s daughter. Everything was settled perfectly—apart from the 100,000 ducats still in Venice in Beatriz’s name.

Beatriz and Brianda, moreover, continued traveling between Constantinople, Italy, and Ferrara; they were not settled. In 1555 Beatriz turned fifteen, and her mother died soon afterward. Beatriz finally inherited the last portion of capital still in Venice in her name. She was then in Ferrara, and the money was brought from Venice.

I have not yet told you that Joseph also had a brother, Bernardo, now called Samuel because he too had returned to Judaism. Exactly one year after Brianda’s death—one must observe a year of mourning—Samuel came to Ferrara, where his cousin Beatriz was with her 100,000 ducats, and married her.

The sultan’s government then intervened with the duke of Ferrara to obtain permission for everybody to go to Constantinople with the money. The duke negotiated for a while and finally yielded. Officially, at last, the entire family and all the money moved to Constantinople—
