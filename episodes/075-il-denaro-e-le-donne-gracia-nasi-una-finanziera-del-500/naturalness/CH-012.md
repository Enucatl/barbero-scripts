## 12. Tiberias, death, and the legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01047–U-01115 -->

A few years later, in 1558, this remarkable woman has an idea. We’re nearing the end now, and Gracia is growing old, but there’s still one more story to tell. The sultan is Süleyman the Magnificent—not yet his son Selim, the alcoholic we begin with. Gracia takes her idea to Süleyman and gets his approval.

She leases part of the Holy Land: the area around Tiberias in Galilee, which includes the ruins of the ancient city and many surrounding villages. No Jews have lived there for as long as anyone can remember, but Gracia wants to bring them back to the Holy Land. With the sultan’s approval, she leases Tiberias, builds a settlement, and starts bringing in refugees from the Christian world. In her own way, you might say she invents Zionism too: the idea of returning the Jews to the Holy Land.

The settlement works for a while, but it doesn’t last. The Jews fleeing Europe are almost all merchants and financiers. What are they supposed to do out there in the Palestinian desert? The venture doesn’t prosper, although people are still talking about the project a few years later.

By then, the project is associated with her nephew Joseph. As we’ve seen, Westerners and Christians treat him as a kind of bogeyman. Whenever his name comes up, they assume the worst. A French ambassador in Constantinople writes that the Jew Miques—Joseph Nasi—has once again received the sultan’s permission to build a city on Lake Tiberias where only Jews can live. As far as the ambassador can tell, Joseph plans to make himself king of the Jews. Once again, you can see what these people represent in the Christian world’s political imagination.

Every life comes to an end. In 1569, Doña Gracia falls ill and dies, passing the baton to her nephew. He keeps running the company for years and lives through some difficult times. In Christian history, the defeat at Lepanto goes down as the moment when Christendom triumphs over the Muslim world, over the Ottoman Empire. It’s certainly a huge and disastrous battle. But in practical terms, it changes nothing. The Ottomans ultimately win the war and get what they want: Cyprus.

Joseph Nasi, naturally, gets the monopoly on wine exports from Cyprus. As if the fortune his aunt built weren’t already enough, the family keeps making more.

It would be nice if I had some brilliant conclusion, but honestly, I don’t. That’s the story. Gracia dies. That world comes to an end, and it feels very far away. So much has changed, including the way we think about tolerance and who is capable of it. But in any case, I hope you found it a good story. Thank you.
