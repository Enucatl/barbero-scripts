## 4. Desmarets's property tax and absolutist decision-making

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00210–U-00364 -->

The finance minister, Desmarets, realizes he has to do something, so he proposes a reform. You'll notice more than once in this lecture that some of these ideas sound very familiar.

The finance minister says they need a wealth tax, because there is no real tax on wealth. Most taxes are duties on trade—basically sales taxes and excise taxes. Whenever someone buys or sells something, a cut goes to the government. There are some taxes on property, but they're assigned pretty casually. The local authorities simply decide how much they need, divide it among the residents, and say, all right, this man owes this much. No one files a declaration of income or assets. There is no tax that's truly proportional to a person's wealth. Desmarets wants to create one.

The idea has come up before, proposed by well-meaning financiers. Instead of this maze of taxes and duties, they say, let's abolish them all and create a flat tax. They don't call it that, of course, because Europe speaks French at the time, not English. But that's the idea: one tax on wealth, replacing all the others. Naturally, the tax contractors hate it. The entire financial and banking establishment fights it, and the proposal goes nowhere.

Now, with the Sun King's finances in ruins, Desmarets brings the wealth tax back—with one important difference. He won't abolish the other taxes. He'll add this one on top of them.

This gives us a good look at decision-making in an absolute monarchy. As I said, the king can do whatever he wants. Of course, he isn't an expert on everything, so he has officials and advisers. Financial matters go through a Council of Finance, chaired by the finance minister, who presents proposals to the king. The experts provide the technical advice, but the king makes the decision.

Desmarets knows that before presenting his brilliant new wealth tax, he needs a commission to study it. Then he can tell the king that the experts—the technical specialists—have approved his plan. So the finance minister appoints a commission. He chooses his brother, his son-in-law, and his two brothers-in-law. The commission approves the plan.

The minister then goes to the king and tells him: “I have here the tax-reform plan approved by the finest experts in the kingdom.”

Saint-Simon's objection is fascinating. In his memoirs, he tells us what's happening behind the scenes, and he reveals a mindset very different from ours. People are used to endless taxes. They're used to being squeezed, as he puts it, until not just blood but pus comes out. But no one has ever been forced to disclose their wealth. At most, the authorities estimate it.

This new tax requires investigations, and it requires everyone to declare what they own. That seems normal to us. To Saint-Simon, it's an unimaginable nightmare. He says: “But do you understand? This will reveal every family's secrets, the things carefully kept hidden in every family. It will provoke furious quarrels between husbands and wives, between brothers. It will expose private affairs. It will destroy credit, because who will still be able to obtain credit once everyone's true wealth is known?”

Saint-Simon says even the king is alarmed. He's used to imposing crushing taxes, but this plan still frightens him. Even his servants can tell that something is bothering him. He's unusually anxious. The thought of forcing his subjects to do this seems terrible to him.

Eventually, the king becomes so troubled that he turns to his confessor, the Jesuit Father Le Tellier. The confessor is a sly old fox. He tells the king: “I will have the question examined.”

A few days later, Father Le Tellier returns with a written opinion from the theologians of the Sorbonne, the kingdom's highest theological authority. As Saint-Simon reports it: “[Father Le Tellier] had returned with an opinion from the most learned doctors of the Sorbonne, which decided plainly that all his subjects’ goods were his own property and that, when he took them, he took only what belonged to him; [the king] admitted that this decision had greatly relieved him, removed all his scruples, and restored the calm and tranquillity he had lost. [...] The king, relieved by Father Le Tellier and his Sorbonne opinion, no longer doubted that all his subjects’ goods were his and that whatever he did not take from them and left them was a matter of pure grace.”

Everyone immediately notices that the king is calm and cheerful again. Saint-Simon is appalled: “See what becomes of a king abandoned into the hands of such a confessor and speaking only to him, and what becomes of a state abandoned into such hands.” Reassured by Father Le Tellier and the Sorbonne, the king no longer doubts that all his subjects' property belongs to him. Anything he lets them keep is simply an act of grace.

This is absolute monarchy taken to its logical extreme. The king doesn't just have the power to decide everything. He owns everything.

There is still one procedural step: the Council of Finance has to approve the plan. The council includes ministers, princes, and royal councillors. The finance minister chairs it, but it meets in the king's presence. Once again, this is how absolute monarchy works.

Everyone at court knows a plan is coming, but no one has read it, and they're all terrified. Finally, the Council of Finance meets. The minister arrives with the plan in his bag. But just as he starts to pull it out, the king speaks first.

The king says: “You know we are living through a difficult moment. The problems are enormous. The minister has prepared a plan to find new resources, and I, the king, have read it. I have to say that this plan is excellent, and I am sure that when you have heard it, you will agree.”

Then the minister takes over. It's all the war's fault, he explains. The enemy refuses to make peace, and France is in danger of invasion. Extreme measures are absolutely necessary. But once this new tax is in place, everything will be fine. The king can pay his debts and his employees, money will start circulating again, and everyone will be richer.

After this little pep talk, the minister immediately starts reading the edict. It's very long, and he reads every word. When he finishes, no one says a thing.

So the king turns to the senior councillor and asks what he thinks. The councillor says: “Your Majesty, to give an opinion I would really need to read it myself, calmly. If I may take it home and study it. Frankly, I cannot give an opinion like this. I would ask the favor of being allowed to abstain.”

The king tells him: “Yes, of course, you are right. There is no point in asking for an opinion. The minister has already studied the plan. It could not possibly be improved. So I will simply ask whether you approve it.”

The council approves it.

“Thus this bloody affair was concluded,” Saint-Simon writes, “and immediately afterward signed, sealed, registered amid stifled sobs, and published amid general weeping.” The tax doesn't bring in anything close to what that commission of cannibals had predicted. The king doesn't pay a penny more than before. Everyone gets squeezed yet again—everyone except the financiers, because no one can ever find out what they really hold in their portfolios.
