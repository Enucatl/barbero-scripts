## 10. Clugny's lottery and brief tenure

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00817–U-00855 -->

His successor, Clugny de Nuits, comes in full of confidence. A contemporary account tells this story and preserves the satirical epitaph that followed: “One day, when Monsieur de Vaines had gone to see him, he told him, to reassure him about his condition, that he should remember the well-known maxim that a controller-general never dies in office. 'Oh! Well,' he replied cheerfully, 'I shall prove the proverb wrong.' [...] They did not fail to compose an epitaph for him [...]: Here lies a controller worthy of mourning, / Loving France greatly and life not at all, / Gladly consenting that it be taken from him / Once he had extinguished the state's debts.”

They need money, and Clugny de Nuits has a brilliant idea: the lottery. It doesn't exist yet, so they invent it. It's El Dorado. Put tickets on sale, people buy them, and then hand a small share of the proceeds back as prizes. Fantastic.

And remember, this is eighteenth-century France, deeply corrupt and rotten. Yet plenty of people criticize this new lottery. They say, “It's shameful for the state to make money by preying on people's urge to gamble.” That's more criticism than you often hear today.

After that—after that—Clugny de Nuits is out of ideas. The lottery isn't enough, and the hole in the budget keeps growing. He's in trouble, but he never has to answer for it. Five months after taking office, he dies suddenly.

The gossips—no, not the gossips. The songs. Political satire and *chansonniers* are already around, and the songs make the rounds: he had promised to die in office. As for Louis XVI, looking back on his decision to appoint Clugny de Nuits, he says, “I think we've made another mistake.” Bring on the next one.
