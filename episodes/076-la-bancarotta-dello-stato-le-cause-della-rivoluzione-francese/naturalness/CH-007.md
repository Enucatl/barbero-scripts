## 7. Run, collapse, and the system's legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00586–U-00668 -->

Now that Law controls everything, he starts putting his plan into action. Gold and silver will gradually be outlawed until banknotes are the only money left. First, he has to put some pressure on people.

The parallel with our own efforts to reduce cash use and move toward electronic money is astonishing. In theory, electronic payments may someday become the only form of money, so governments make cash harder to use. Law, now the finance minister, does something similar: he bans people from keeping more than five hundred francs in gold at home. Today, you can't spend more than three thousand euros in cash, right? Law says you can't even keep it in your house.

Naturally, anonymous tip-offs start pouring in, and the police begin searching people's homes for illegal gold. Now people get nervous. The banknotes say, “1,000 francs payable at sight to the bearer.” In plain language, that means I can take this note to the bank tomorrow and get a thousand francs in gold. But if the government suddenly seems hostile to gold and may outlaw it altogether, I have reason to worry.

People start to panic. Some complain, and journalists and pamphleteers publish attacks on the minister. But this is an absolute monarchy. Anyone who complains in public is arrested and deported to the colonies. The public protests stop—officially. The fear keeps spreading.

Eventually, a major speculator with a huge pile of banknotes does the math and decides to cash them in for gold. Curiously enough, the first people to do this include several great nobles and princes of the blood—the Duke of Bourbon and the Prince of Conti—people with, let's say, excellent instincts and excellent sources. We didn't invent insider trading.

These people know what's coming. Then one day, the public sees the Duke of Bourbon, the king's cousin, arrive at the bank in person. A procession of servants follows him, carrying suitcases. He exchanges every one of his banknotes for gold.

Naturally, panic erupts. Crowds rush to the bank's offices on the rue Quincampoix in Paris—a fitting location, directly behind the stock exchange even today, in the heart of the financial district. The crowd surges toward the doors, and seventeen people are crushed to death. The bank pays out for a while, then shuts down. The regent has no choice but to declare that the banknotes are no longer legal tender. Sorry, but no one is getting paid.

The treasurer and the bank director whose signatures appear on the notes go straight to the Bastille, naturally. John Law gets away in time. He, too, has been tipped off. He flees the kingdom for Venice, where no one can touch him and where he dies years later.

Now everyone is complaining, and a great many speculators are ruined. I have to say, the regent handles the aftermath fairly well. He issues a decree that would astonish everyone if anyone tried it today. There isn't much money left, but whatever they can repay to the cheated investors will go to the small ones first. Most small investors get back what they put in. Many of the big ones are wiped out.

Meanwhile, the national debt really has evaporated. All things considered, the monarchy's finances are in better shape than before. There's just one small problem. Paper money is a good idea, but no one in France will talk about it again for two centuries. After the public's experience with Law's System, even suggesting that banknotes should circulate becomes impossible.

You may be thinking: we're still a long way from the French Revolution. When are we going to get there? And yes, it's only 1720. But after the Mississippi Bubble collapses and the chaos dies down, the monarchy decides things aren't going so badly after all. As I said, the national debt has almost disappeared. They can borrow again. They can start piling up debt all over again. And then the regency ends.
