## 13. The Estates-General and the transfer of sovereignty

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01018–U-01089 -->

The country demands that the king call the Estates-General. But what does that mean?

The Estates-General is an ancient institution. Medieval French kings called it regularly because no one in medieval France was going to hand over a penny in taxes just because the king announced, “I've decided to raise taxes.”

To impose a tax, the king had to call an assembly. The princes, barons, and nobles came. So did the bishops, archbishops, and other clergy, along with the bourgeois merchants from the towns—the people with money. These three groups, the nobility, the clergy, and the merchants, were known as the three estates.

The king and his ministers had to make their case to the whole assembly. “We need money.” They had to say how much and what for. The assembly debated the request and might approve it. Without the Estates-General, a medieval French king couldn't impose a penny in taxes.

Naturally, kings found that kind of oversight restrictive. We started with Louis XIV's France as an absolute monarchy. France became an absolute monarchy in the seventeenth century because the king grew powerful enough to impose taxes without calling the Estates-General.

It last meets in 1614. After that, the kings of France rule for a century and a half without it. But people remember that it once existed—especially the nobles, who carried a lot of weight there. They were the First Estate, and the clergy was the Second. Everyone else belonged to the Third Estate, which had little influence. Still, it had to be there because the merchants were the ones with the money.

By 1788, people in France are saying, “The Estates-General—how long has it been since we called it? Look where we've ended up. The king can't go on like this. He has to listen to the country.”

The nobles push hardest, never imagining that three or four years later they'll climb the steps of the guillotine alongside the king. “We want the Estates-General. We want the monarchy brought under control.”

The king is forced to promise he'll call the Estates-General, and he does. In desperation, he also brings Necker back into the ministry.

The king and Necker open the Estates-General by trying to discuss the hole in the budget. The delegates are stunned to learn that it's far worse than anyone thought. But then they say, “We don't know how to fix it. And that's not even the most important problem anymore.”

The real problem is that absolute monarchy can't continue. The country has to be governed differently from now on. The king must accept the Estates-General as a permanent voice in government, an assembly that represents the country. He can no longer rule alone.

You know what happens. The king hesitates, then tries to dissolve the Estates-General. It refuses. “We are the country. We are the people. We hold the power. Without us, the king is nothing.”

The king tries to use the army to break it up by force. Paris rises, and on July 14, 1789, the people storm the Bastille. From that moment on, the Estates-General becomes the National Assembly, the parliament, and quickly establishes itself as the only real legal power in France.
