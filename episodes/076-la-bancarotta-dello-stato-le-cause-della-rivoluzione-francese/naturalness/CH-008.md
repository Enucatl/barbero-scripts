## 8. Louis XV restores the debt cycle

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00669–U-00718 -->

The boy king is a young man now. He becomes Louis XV, reigns for about fifty years, and goes down in history as Louis the Beloved. Historians, though, generally agree that he's an idiot and his reign is a disaster.

For one thing, France fights and loses the most important war of the century: the Seven Years' War. It's the colonial war that costs the French Canada and drives them out of India. By now, it's clear that England is the great power of the future. But France barely notices. It goes right on telling itself that France is the great power—a habit the French have never entirely lost, naturally.

And intellectually, France is still the capital of the world. This is the Enlightenment. Think of Voltaire. French philosophers dominate every capital in Europe. France feels strong, rich, prosperous—the cradle of civilization. Louis XV, meanwhile, loves his pleasures and his many expensive mistresses, especially Madame de Pompadour. The king spends and spends, his ministers borrow, and for almost fifty years everyone pretends nothing is wrong.

So we've moved ahead quickly. In 1774, Louis XV—the Beloved—dies, and his grandson Louis XVI takes the throne. No one can imagine that less than twenty years later he'll lose his head to the guillotine. He rules an absolute monarchy that still considers itself the most powerful country in the world.

Louis XVI is a genuinely good man. Every report we have from his childhood teachers says he's a complete nonentity, but he means well. When he takes the throne, he wants to understand how things work. Enlightenment thinkers have spent years calling for reforms, saying the *Ancien Régime* needs to change. He listens to them. All right, let's make those reforms.

Then he looks at the books. After fifty years, the finances are a disaster all over again. The national debt is terrifying.

And here's how the rest of the story goes. They find an economist who claims to know how to fix everything. Then another, and another, each with his own recipe. One after another, they're welcomed with great enthusiasm and made finance minister. Before long, each one is thrown out and cursed because his recipe hasn't worked. The interesting part is that every recipe is different. We're about to see the full range of options.
