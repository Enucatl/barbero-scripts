## 6. Bank, company, and Mississippi bubble

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00472–U-00585 -->

The regent gives Law the go-ahead, and they move in stages. First, they create a private bank with the modest name of the General Bank. It issues shares that pay generous returns, so people eagerly buy them. The regent supports Law and gives him a boost: the tax authorities will accept the bank's shares as payment. Now who wouldn't want them? The shares circulate, the price climbs, and the bank keeps issuing more.

The bank, in turn, accepts the old royal IOUs—the mint notes that have been circulating for years. In other words, it accepts the national debt. As the bank takes in those old notes, the debt gradually shrinks. Apparently, John Law really is a magician. Everyone is delighted.

The next step is the banknote. The bank starts issuing not shares, but standardized printed notes that we would immediately recognize as paper money. Think of old U.S. dollar bills issued before 1963. A $100 bill carried the promise, “will pay to the bearer on demand.” Modern dollars don't say anything like that. A $100 bill is simply worth $100—an abstraction. But at least in theory, the older note promised that you could take it to the bank and redeem it on demand for $100 in gold or silver. That's what “will pay to the bearer on demand” means.

Law prints banknotes with official signatures—just as euros still carry the signature of the bank official backing them—and puts the promise right on the paper. Surviving notes read: “Banque générale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight fifty écus in specie of the weight and fineness current this day, value received at Paris on 10 June 1718. Banque royale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight one hundred livres tournois in silver specie, value received at Paris on 1 April 1719.”

What happens? People already trust the bank because its shares pay well, so they're fairly willing to accept its banknotes too. Besides, everyone is thinking the same thing: I'll keep my gold louis locked in the safe and spend these paper notes for now. That's exactly what Law wants. His ultimate plan is to outlaw gold and silver money and leave only banknotes in circulation.

And who guarantees that the bank will pay? The monarchy. The regent believes in the plan and is convinced that Law really is a magician, so the monarchy backs the notes.

Before long, they decide that if the system is working this well, they should print more notes. So they do. In theory, the bank's capital backs every note. But eventually, that starts to feel like an unnecessary limit. Printing money is wonderful. The notes circulate, and the national debt really is falling. The bank sweeps up the old IOUs and replaces them with new paper money.

Of course, there isn't enough capital in the vault to back all those banknotes. So what now? Law has another brilliant idea.

This is the beginning of colonial expansion. In the early eighteenth century, France believes it can become a great colonial power. It has Canada, Louisiana, and the Antilles. People expect the empire in America to grow enormously and produce fabulous wealth.

So now the banknotes are backed by the promise of all the riches that will eventually pour in from America. Law creates a trading company, authorized by the monarchy and tied to the bank. The monarchy gives it a monopoly on all colonial trade. Think about what that means: a monopoly on tobacco, a monopoly on sugar. It's colossal. It's El Dorado.

The public loves it and keeps trusting the system. The bank is now joined to the trading company. And while they're at it—Law and the regent understand each other perfectly—the company also takes over the government's tax-collection monopoly. Remember, private businesses collect the taxes. Now Law controls that too.

The shares circulate, and so do the banknotes. One man now runs the bank, the company with a monopoly on all colonial trade, and the company that collects every tax in the kingdom. The share price keeps rising. People are ecstatic.

This is one of the first financial bubbles in history. We know it as the Mississippi Bubble because the company created to exploit the American colonies is called the Mississippi Company. A great many people make a great deal of money. The French language even invents a new word: *millionnaire*—millionaire. Suddenly, everyone is talking about people who have made millions of francs speculating in shares.

The regent decides to go one step further. Law still doesn't control every lever of power: someone else is finance minister. So why not give him that job too? There's one small problem. France is a deeply Catholic kingdom. The regent may be a notorious atheist who holds orgies at Easter, but a Scottish Protestant still can't become a government minister.

John Law suddenly discovers a deep interest in Catholicism. He starts spending time with the Jesuits, converts, and can now become finance minister too.
