# La bancarotta dello stato: le cause della Rivoluzione Francese — corrected English script

## 1. Scope and financial thesis

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00057 -->

Good evening, everyone. Tonight, I have to admit, you're the guinea pigs. I've been working on this lecture, and it may still need a little work. You can be the judges. The financial causes of the French Revolution aren't something I usually study. But the subject has always fascinated me, so I took this opportunity to understand it a little better. I'm not entirely sure I've figured it all out. One thing is certain, though: the French Revolution breaks out for many reasons.

There are shelves of books called *The Causes of the French Revolution*. They talk about ordinary people's hatred of the privileges enjoyed by the nobility and the clergy. They talk about France's growing class of wealthy merchants and industrialists, who have no political influence and want some. And then there's the poverty at the bottom of society: bad harvests, soaring grain prices and therefore soaring bread prices, unrest, popular uprisings, and hunger.

But tonight we're focusing on something else: the colossal hole in the French monarchy's budget, created by a century of reckless spending and a disastrous tax system. That hole eventually forces the king to take the step we all learned marks the beginning of the French Revolution: he summons the Estates-General. I'm sure we all remember that phrase from school. But what does it mean, and why is it such a dramatic step? We'll get to that near the end. For now, the point is that Louis XVI has to summon the Estates-General because the monarchy's finances are a catastrophe. He has no idea that within four years, they'll cut off his head.

That's the story we're going to examine. Faced with this disastrous budget hole, the French monarchy repeatedly puts its faith in economists who claim to have the answer, who know exactly how to turn things around. So the idea that you can manage an economy—and even revive it—by following an economist's theory is nothing new. Eighteenth-century France is full of attempts to do just that.

## 2. Absolutism and Saint-Simon's opposition

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00058–U-00122 -->

We're in eighteenth-century France, an absolute monarchy. What does that mean? At least in theory, the king can do whatever he wants. He doesn't need anyone's approval. He can issue an order without explaining himself: this is the king's will. With a document called a *lettre de cachet*, he can have anyone arrested without explanation and locked in the Bastille for as long as he likes—even for life. He can make any law he wants. He answers to no parliament, no minister, no one. And he can impose whatever taxes he likes.

It isn't like this in the Middle Ages. We won't get into that now, but monarchy hasn't always worked this way. For centuries, other powers within the kingdom—especially the great nobles—act as a check on the king. But by the reign of Louis XIV, the Sun King, the French monarchy is absolute.

You know the famous line associated with the Sun King: “I am the State.” What does that mean? Of course he has advisers, secretaries, and ministers. But every one of them takes orders from him.

We have a fascinating account of the Sun King's monarchy in the memoirs of one of the great nobles of the time, the Duke of Saint-Simon. Saint-Simon lives through Louis XIV's later years and survives well into the middle of the eighteenth century. His memoirs are a vast, sprawling work—seven or eight volumes, each a thousand pages long—packed with gossip, anecdotes, and reflections on life under the absolute rule of Louis XIV.

Saint-Simon speaks for the people in the kingdom who are deeply unhappy with absolute monarchy. By the time of the Revolution, the opposition will come from the bourgeoisie, intellectuals, and Enlightenment thinkers. But this is the beginning of the eighteenth century, almost a hundred years earlier. Here, the opposition comes from great nobles—princes and dukes like Saint-Simon—men who are used to wielding tremendous influence. No one resents the king's absolute power more than they do.

Saint-Simon paints a grim picture of the Sun King's final years, when France is fighting a disastrous war against practically the whole world: the War of the Spanish Succession. The war is going terribly. Writing years later, Saint-Simon pinpoints the moment the disaster begins: the catastrophe at Turin.

Remember the siege of Turin in 1706? We've all heard the story, haven't we? Pietro Micca, and then Prince Eugene arrives and breaks the French siege. Here in Turin, everyone knows the episode. We might assume it matters only because we're from Turin—that it's really a minor, provincial affair.

But decades later, Saint-Simon says France's ruin begins with the catastrophe at Turin, when they throw the French out of Italy. From then on, France has no influence left in Italy, and that, he says, is where the country's ruin begins. It is now 1710.

## 3. Wartime insolvency, money, and tax farming

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00123–U-00209 -->

Four years have passed since the disaster at Turin, and France is broke. The country has just come through the worst winter of the century. There's no money, the budget hole is terrifying, and the king is buried in debt. So he stops paying. He doesn't pay his debts or his employees. Even the troops at the front aren't getting paid.

Well, that's not quite true. The king does pay, just not in coins. Remember, paper money—banknotes—hasn't been invented yet. Until very recently, we thought of paper money as perfectly normal and modern. Now electronic payments are making it look old-fashioned. But for a long time, paper money itself represented modernity.

In early eighteenth-century France, money is metal, preferably precious metal. The money that really matters is gold or silver coin. People accept it because they know the metal itself has value, and no one can take that value away. There are smaller coins too, of course. You can't go to the market, buy six eggs, and pay with a silver crown worth fifty euros. People accept that small change by convention—they have to. But for any serious transaction, the money that counts is gold or silver.

By 1710, those coins have almost vanished from circulation in France. Anyone who has them holds on tight. The king, meanwhile, pays people with promises—essentially bills of exchange, or IOUs. These notes are printed at the mint and known as mint notes. One surviving note reads: “MINT NOTE RENEWED AND CERTIFIED pursuant to the Declaration of 24 May 1707. In the amount of [...] 1,000 livres tournois. FOR the sum of one thousand livres, which I will pay in one year to the bearer, value received in another mint note. Made at the Paris Mint on 15 June 1707.” The king puts these notes into circulation, and if the king gives you one, you have to accept it.

But nobody wants them. No one believes that a year from now, they'll really be able to take this piece of paper to the mint and walk out with gold coins. So people try to unload them. Say I have a mint note for a thousand francs. Give me a hundred francs in gold, and I'll gladly hand it over. Anyone with real money hides it away, while the notes keep circulating and losing value. The only people making money are the bankers. They buy up the notes, play the exchange rates, use their government connections, and get rich while everyone else goes broke.

The king may not pay, but he certainly collects. Taxes nearly double every year. Saint-Simon writes: “The capitation tax, doubled and tripled at the arbitrary will of the provincial intendants; goods and foodstuffs of every kind taxed at four times their value; taxes on the well-to-do and others of every nature and on all sorts of things—all this crushed nobles and commoners, lords and churchmen, without what reached the king being enough: he drew the blood of all his subjects without distinction, squeezed them even to the pus, and enriched an infinite army of tax contractors and employees in these various kinds of taxes, in whose hands the largest and best part remained.”

There's one more thing we need to understand. Today, we're used to hearing that the modern state has too many employees and too much bureaucracy. Eighteenth-century France has the opposite problem: it has almost no bureaucracy at all. The state doesn't collect its own taxes because it doesn't have the staff. Instead, it contracts the job out to private businessmen and financiers. They pay the king an advance, then collect the taxes, duties, levies, and tithes themselves.

There is a bewildering maze of different taxes. No one else can make sense of it, but the contractors can. They advance the king as little as possible, then send out an army of employees to collect the money. These aren't government employees; they work for the contractors. Eventually, the contractors join together in one enormous company, the *Ferme générale*, or General Farm. It's a consortium of bankers that collects taxes across the kingdom, using its own methods and operating with no oversight. So, Saint-Simon says, the king keeps raising taxes, but the money always ends up in the contractors' pockets.

## 4. Desmarets's property tax and absolutist decision-making

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00210–U-00364 -->

The finance minister, Desmarets, realizes he has to do something, so he proposes a reform. You'll notice more than once in this lecture that some of these ideas sound very familiar.

The finance minister says they need a wealth tax, because there is no real tax on wealth. Most taxes are duties on trade—basically sales taxes and excise taxes. Whenever someone buys or sells something, a cut goes to the government. There are some taxes on property, but they're assigned pretty casually. The local authorities simply decide how much they need, divide it among the residents, and say, all right, this man owes this much. No one files a declaration of income or assets. There is no tax that's truly proportional to a person's wealth. Desmarets wants to create one.

The idea has come up before, proposed by well-meaning financiers. Instead of this maze of taxes and duties, they say, let's abolish them all and create a flat tax. They don't call it that, of course, because Europe speaks French at the time, not English. But that's the idea: one tax on wealth, replacing all the others. Naturally, the tax contractors hate it. The entire financial and banking establishment fights it, and the proposal goes nowhere.

Now, with the Sun King's finances in ruins, Desmarets brings the wealth tax back—with one important difference. He won't abolish the other taxes. He'll add this one on top of them.

This gives us a good look at decision-making in an absolute monarchy. As I said, the king can do whatever he wants. Of course, he isn't an expert on everything, so he has officials and advisers. Financial matters go through a Council of Finance, chaired by the finance minister, who presents proposals to the king. The experts provide the technical advice, but the king makes the decision.

Desmarets knows that before presenting his brilliant new wealth tax, he needs a commission to study it. Then he can tell the king that the experts—the technical specialists—have approved his plan. So the finance minister appoints a commission. He chooses his brother, his son-in-law, and his two brothers-in-law. The commission approves the plan.

The minister then goes to the king and tells him: “I have here the tax-reform plan approved by the finest experts in the kingdom.”

Saint-Simon's objection is fascinating. In his memoirs, he tells us what's happening behind the scenes, and he reveals a mindset very different from ours. People are used to endless taxes. They're used to being squeezed, as he puts it, until not just blood but pus comes out. But no one has ever been forced to disclose their wealth. At most, the authorities estimate it.

This new tax requires investigations, and it requires everyone to declare what they own. That seems normal to us. To Saint-Simon, it's an unimaginable nightmare. He says: “But do you understand? This will reveal every family's secrets, the things carefully kept hidden in every family. It will provoke furious quarrels between husbands and wives, between brothers. It will expose private affairs. It will destroy credit, because who will still be able to obtain credit once everyone's true wealth is known?”

Saint-Simon says even the king is alarmed. He's used to imposing crushing taxes, but this plan still frightens him. Even his servants can tell that something is bothering him. He's unusually anxious. The thought of forcing his subjects to do this seems terrible to him.

Eventually, the king becomes so troubled that he turns to his confessor, the Jesuit Father Le Tellier. The confessor is a sly old fox. He tells the king: “I will have the question examined.”

A few days later, Father Le Tellier returns with a written opinion from the theologians of the Sorbonne, the kingdom's highest theological authority. As Saint-Simon reports it: “[Father Le Tellier] had returned with an opinion from the most learned doctors of the Sorbonne, which decided plainly that all his subjects’ goods were his own property and that, when he took them, he took only what belonged to him; [the king] admitted that this decision had greatly relieved him, removed all his scruples, and restored the calm and tranquillity he had lost. [...] The king, relieved by Father Le Tellier and his Sorbonne opinion, no longer doubted that all his subjects’ goods were his and that whatever he did not take from them and left them was a matter of pure grace.”

Everyone immediately notices that the king is calm and cheerful again. Saint-Simon is appalled: “See what becomes of a king abandoned into the hands of such a confessor and speaking only to him, and what becomes of a state abandoned into such hands.” Reassured by Father Le Tellier and the Sorbonne, the king no longer doubts that all his subjects' property belongs to him. Anything he lets them keep is simply an act of grace.

This is absolute monarchy taken to its logical extreme. The king doesn't just have the power to decide everything. He owns everything.

There is still one procedural step: the Council of Finance has to approve the plan. The council includes ministers, princes, and royal councillors. The finance minister chairs it, but it meets in the king's presence. Once again, this is how absolute monarchy works.

Everyone at court knows a plan is coming, but no one has read it, and they're all terrified. Finally, the Council of Finance meets. The minister arrives with the plan in his bag. But just as he starts to pull it out, the king speaks first.

The king says: “You know we are living through a difficult moment. The problems are enormous. The minister has prepared a plan to find new resources, and I, the king, have read it. I have to say that this plan is excellent, and I am sure that when you have heard it, you will agree.”

Then the minister takes over. It's all the war's fault, he explains. The enemy refuses to make peace, and France is in danger of invasion. Extreme measures are absolutely necessary. But once this new tax is in place, everything will be fine. The king can pay his debts and his employees, money will start circulating again, and everyone will be richer.

After this little pep talk, the minister immediately starts reading the edict. It's very long, and he reads every word. When he finishes, no one says a thing.

So the king turns to the senior councillor and asks what he thinks. The councillor says: “Your Majesty, to give an opinion I would really need to read it myself, calmly. If I may take it home and study it. Frankly, I cannot give an opinion like this. I would ask the favor of being allowed to abstain.”

The king tells him: “Yes, of course, you are right. There is no point in asking for an opinion. The minister has already studied the plan. It could not possibly be improved. So I will simply ask whether you approve it.”

The council approves it.

“Thus this bloody affair was concluded,” Saint-Simon writes, “and immediately afterward signed, sealed, registered amid stifled sobs, and published amid general weeping.” The tax doesn't bring in anything close to what that commission of cannibals had predicted. The king doesn't pay a penny more than before. Everyone gets squeezed yet again—everyone except the financiers, because no one can ever find out what they really hold in their portfolios.

## 5. Regency crisis and John Law's theory

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00365–U-00471 -->

That's where things stand when the Sun King dies in 1715. His heir, the future Louis XV, is only a child. Louis XIV has lived so long that both his son and his grandson have died before him, leaving his great-grandson next in line. So France enters a regency. For eight years, from 1715 to 1723, the country is governed by the young king's uncle, the Duke of Orléans.

During the regency, it becomes clear that the financial situation is catastrophic. Something has to be done. But what? One option is bankruptcy. Other kings have done it before. The king simply announces that his debts are canceled and that no one will be paid. It will cause disaster, poverty, and ruin for countless people, but it will save the kingdom.

While the government is seriously considering bankruptcy, a gentleman arrives in Paris claiming to have the answer. He's a Scotsman named John Law—Law, as in law. He's an economist from a banking family. Bankers are everywhere in this story. Given where we are, I'll try not to speak too badly of them. You can judge for yourselves.

Law is a banker, but he's also a rather unusual man with a turbulent past. A few years earlier, he kills a man in a duel over a woman. English law is strict, so he's sentenced to death and spends a long time in prison waiting to be hanged. Then his very good lawyers manage to get the charge reduced to manslaughter, which allows him to post bail. Law gets out and decides it would be wise to leave the United Kingdom. He flees to the Continent and starts publishing works on economics.

He publishes a treatise, and in some ways his thinking is strikingly modern. Our system of gold and silver money is absurd, Law says. Not only do gold and silver have no inherent value, but no one can control what value they do have. Discover new gold mines, and gold loses value. Then people start hoarding it instead of spending it, and its value rises. The whole thing is absurd. We should use paper money.

As we've seen, royal IOUs are already circulating in France. But Law wants to abolish metal money altogether and replace it with paper—something we essentially do only in the last century, right? And if you're going to create banknotes, Law argues, you need a central bank to issue and print them.

But what guarantees the value of those banknotes? Law says the kingdom has only one true form of wealth: land. Here you can see how strangely modern this early eighteenth-century thinking can be, while still rooted in ideas that go back thousands of years. Land is wealth.

So, Law says, the king simply has to measure all the land in the kingdom and issue a proportional amount of paper money. Suppose each banknote can buy one hectare of land. Everyone will know exactly what that note is worth. Its value will be guaranteed, fixed, unchanging.

From the Continent, where he's hiding after jumping bail, Law sends his plan to London. The English government receives this proposal from a Scottish killer and fugitive and responds, naturally: “This man is mad.”

So Law tries his luck elsewhere and comes to Paris. The regent there, as I said, is the Duke of Orléans. He's a good man—likable and well-intentioned—but he has some serious flaws. He's spectacularly lazy and has no interest in work. He devotes himself to pleasure and partying because he can't resist orgies, alcohol, or women. But he means well.

He has another little quirk. In a society that's still officially and deeply Catholic, the Duke of Orléans is a complete atheist. He doesn't believe in God, and he makes sure everyone knows it because he thinks it makes him look intelligent. He loves to flaunt his atheism and irreverence. How? For example, he tries to schedule his most spectacular orgies for Good Friday.

And yet, although he doesn't believe in God, he spends his life obsessed with summoning the devil. He pays enormous sums to frauds and magicians who promise to make the devil appear. Saint-Simon writes: “The curiosity of M. le duc d’Orléans, joined to a false notion of firmness and courage, had from early on occupied him in seeking to see the devil and to make him speak. He neglected nothing, even the maddest reading, to persuade himself that there is no God, and he believed in the devil to the point of hoping to see him and converse with him. This contrast cannot be understood, and yet it is extremely common.” So he's always surrounded by swindlers and charlatans.

This is the man running France when John Law arrives with his plan. The two are made for each other. The country's finances are in ruins. The national debt now equals ten years of government revenue. For comparison, Italy's national debt today is equal to roughly three years of revenue, I believe. France owes ten times its annual revenue. But John Law has the answer.

## 6. Bank, company, and Mississippi bubble

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00472–U-00585 -->

The regent gives Law the go-ahead, and they move in stages. First, they create a private bank with the modest name of the General Bank. It issues shares that pay generous returns, so people eagerly buy them. The regent supports Law and gives him a boost: the tax authorities will accept the bank's shares as payment. Now who wouldn't want them? The shares circulate, the price climbs, and the bank keeps issuing more.

The bank, in turn, accepts the old royal IOUs—the mint notes that have been circulating for years. In other words, it accepts the national debt. As the bank takes in those old notes, the debt gradually shrinks. Apparently, John Law really is a magician. Everyone is delighted.

The next step is the banknote. The bank starts issuing not shares, but standardized printed notes that we would immediately recognize as paper money. Think of the old Italian lira, which said, “1,000 lire payable at sight to the bearer.” Euros don't say anything like that. A euro is simply a euro, an abstraction. But at least in theory, the promise behind a lira note was this: take your 1,000-lira note to the bank, and the bank will hand over 1,000 lire. That's what “payable at sight to the bearer” means.

Law prints banknotes with official signatures—just as euros still carry the signature of the bank official backing them—and puts the promise right on the paper. Surviving notes read: “Banque générale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight fifty écus in specie of the weight and fineness current this day, value received at Paris on 10 June 1718. Banque royale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight one hundred livres tournois in silver specie, value received at Paris on 1 April 1719.”

What happens? People already trust the bank because its shares pay well, so they're fairly willing to accept its banknotes too. Besides, everyone is thinking the same thing: I'll keep my gold louis locked in the safe and spend these paper notes for now. That's exactly what Law wants. His ultimate plan is to outlaw gold and silver money and leave only banknotes in circulation.

And who guarantees that the bank will pay? The monarchy. The regent believes in the plan and is convinced that Law really is a magician, so the monarchy backs the notes.

Before long, they decide that if the system is working this well, they should print more notes. So they do. In theory, the bank's capital backs every note. But eventually, that starts to feel like an unnecessary limit. Printing money is wonderful. The notes circulate, and the national debt really is falling. The bank sweeps up the old IOUs and replaces them with new paper money.

Of course, there isn't enough capital in the vault to back all those banknotes. So what now? Law has another brilliant idea.

This is the beginning of colonial expansion. In the early eighteenth century, France believes it can become a great colonial power. It has Canada, Louisiana, and the Antilles. People expect the empire in America to grow enormously and produce fabulous wealth.

So now the banknotes are backed by the promise of all the riches that will eventually pour in from America. Law creates a trading company, authorized by the monarchy and tied to the bank. The monarchy gives it a monopoly on all colonial trade. Think about what that means: a monopoly on tobacco, a monopoly on sugar. It's colossal. It's El Dorado.

The public loves it and keeps trusting the system. The bank is now joined to the trading company. And while they're at it—Law and the regent understand each other perfectly—the company also takes over the government's tax-collection monopoly. Remember, private businesses collect the taxes. Now Law controls that too.

The shares circulate, and so do the banknotes. One man now runs the bank, the company with a monopoly on all colonial trade, and the company that collects every tax in the kingdom. The share price keeps rising. People are ecstatic.

This is one of the first financial bubbles in history. We know it as the Mississippi Bubble because the company created to exploit the American colonies is called the Mississippi Company. A great many people make a great deal of money. The French language even invents a new word: *millionnaire*—millionaire. Suddenly, everyone is talking about people who have made millions of francs speculating in shares.

The regent decides to go one step further. Law still doesn't control every lever of power: someone else is finance minister. So why not give him that job too? There's one small problem. France is a deeply Catholic kingdom. The regent may be a notorious atheist who holds orgies at Easter, but a Scottish Protestant still can't become a government minister.

John Law suddenly discovers a deep interest in Catholicism. He starts spending time with the Jesuits, converts, and can now become finance minister too.

## 7. Run, collapse, and the system's legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00586–U-00668 -->

Now that Law controls everything, he starts putting his plan into action. Gold and silver will gradually be outlawed until banknotes are the only money left. First, he has to put some pressure on people.

The parallel with our own efforts to reduce cash use and move toward electronic money is astonishing. In theory, electronic payments may someday become the only form of money, so governments make cash harder to use. Law, now the finance minister, does something similar: he bans people from keeping more than five hundred francs in gold at home. Today, you can't spend more than three thousand euros in cash, right? Law says you can't even keep it in your house.

Naturally, anonymous tip-offs start pouring in, and the police begin searching people's homes for illegal gold. Now people get nervous. The banknotes say, “1,000 francs payable at sight to the bearer.” In plain language, that means I can take this note to the bank tomorrow and get a thousand francs in gold. But if the government suddenly seems hostile to gold and may outlaw it altogether, I have reason to worry.

People start to panic. Some complain, and journalists and pamphleteers publish attacks on the minister. But this is an absolute monarchy. Anyone who complains in public is arrested and deported to the colonies. The public protests stop—officially. The fear keeps spreading.

Eventually, a major speculator with a huge pile of banknotes does the math and decides to cash them in for gold. Curiously enough, the first people to do this include several great nobles and princes of the blood—the Duke of Bourbon and the Prince of Conti—people with, let's say, excellent instincts and excellent sources. We didn't invent insider trading.

These people know what's coming. Then one day, the public sees the Duke of Bourbon, the king's cousin, arrive at the bank in person. A procession of servants follows him, carrying suitcases. He exchanges every one of his banknotes for gold.

Naturally, panic erupts. Crowds rush to the bank's offices on the rue Quincampoix in Paris—a fitting location, directly behind the stock exchange even today, in the heart of the financial district. The crowd surges toward the doors, and seventeen people are crushed to death. The bank pays out for a while, then shuts down. The regent has no choice but to declare that the banknotes are no longer legal tender. Sorry, but no one is getting paid.

The treasurer and the bank director whose signatures appear on the notes go straight to the Bastille, naturally. John Law gets away in time. He, too, has been tipped off. He flees the kingdom for Venice, where no one can touch him and where he dies years later.

Now everyone is complaining, and a great many speculators are ruined. I have to say, the regent handles the aftermath fairly well. He issues a decree that would astonish everyone if anyone tried it today. There isn't much money left, but whatever they can repay to the cheated investors will go to the small ones first. Most small investors get back what they put in. Many of the big ones are wiped out.

Meanwhile, the national debt really has evaporated. All things considered, the monarchy's finances are in better shape than before. There's just one small problem. Paper money is a good idea, but no one in France will talk about it again for two centuries. After the public's experience with Law's System, even suggesting that banknotes should circulate becomes impossible.

You may be thinking: we're still a long way from the French Revolution. When are we going to get there? And yes, it's only 1720. But after the Mississippi Bubble collapses and the chaos dies down, the monarchy decides things aren't going so badly after all. As I said, the national debt has almost disappeared. They can borrow again. They can start piling up debt all over again. And then the regency ends.

## 8. Louis XV restores the debt cycle

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00669–U-00718 -->

The boy king is a young man now. He becomes Louis XV, reigns for about fifty years, and goes down in history as Louis the Beloved. Historians, though, generally agree that he's an idiot and his reign is a disaster.

For one thing, France fights and loses the most important war of the century: the Seven Years' War. It's the colonial war that costs the French Canada and drives them out of India. By now, it's clear that England is the great power of the future. But France barely notices. It goes right on telling itself that France is the great power—a habit the French have never entirely lost, naturally.

And intellectually, France is still the capital of the world. This is the Enlightenment. Think of Voltaire. French philosophers dominate every capital in Europe. France feels strong, rich, prosperous—the cradle of civilization. Louis XV, meanwhile, loves his pleasures and his many expensive mistresses, especially Madame de Pompadour. The king spends and spends, his ministers borrow, and for almost fifty years everyone pretends nothing is wrong.

So we've moved ahead quickly. In 1774, Louis XV—the Beloved—dies, and his grandson Louis XVI takes the throne. No one can imagine that less than twenty years later he'll lose his head to the guillotine. He rules an absolute monarchy that still considers itself the most powerful country in the world.

Louis XVI is a genuinely good man. Every report we have from his childhood teachers says he's a complete nonentity, but he means well. When he takes the throne, he wants to understand how things work. Enlightenment thinkers have spent years calling for reforms, saying the *Ancien Régime* needs to change. He listens to them. All right, let's make those reforms.

Then he looks at the books. After fifty years, the finances are a disaster all over again. The national debt is terrifying.

And here's how the rest of the story goes. They find an economist who claims to know how to fix everything. Then another, and another, each with his own recipe. One after another, they're welcomed with great enthusiasm and made finance minister. Before long, each one is thrown out and cursed because his recipe hasn't worked. The interesting part is that every recipe is different. We're about to see the full range of options.

## 9. Turgot, liberal reform, grain crisis, and cuts

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00719–U-00816 -->

The first finance minister Louis XVI appoints is an economist named Turgot. The king means well, though he has problems of his own too: his frivolous wife, Marie Antoinette, who spends and spends. Still, Louis XVI truly wants to go down in history as the father of his people, the man who really tried to make the kingdom more prosperous. So he puts an economist in charge of the finances.

Enlightenment thinkers adore Turgot, and so does fashionable Paris salon society. Today you sometimes hear about the salons in Rome where all the decisions get made, right? Back then, it was the salons of Paris. Turgot is a man of absolute integrity, devoted to the public good. Everyone knows it. No one has the slightest doubt.

And Turgot's time in office shows us something history proves again and again, though we don't much like being reminded of it. Putting a thoroughly good, honest, well-meaning person in power is no guarantee that things will get better. Quite the opposite.

Turgot takes office to tremendous public excitement. Let me read you part of a letter from a prominent woman in those Paris salons: “Oh! This time, be assured that good will be done, and that it will be done well, because enlightenment will guide virtue and love of the public good. Never, no never, have two men more virtuous, more disinterested, more active been brought together and more powerfully animated by a greater and higher interest. You will see: their ministry will leave a profound mark on the minds of men. [...] Oh! What bad times for rogues and courtiers!” Those are the hopes that greet Turgot when he enters the ministry.

Turgot is an ultraliberal. The state should interfere as little as possible: cut taxes, eliminate duties above all, and let goods move freely. His basic idea resembles John Law's: the real source of wealth is land. But listen to where that reasoning takes him.

Land is the true source of wealth. So if we want to tax wealth, we tax what the land produces. All wealth comes from the land—crops, metals, minerals, everything we have. Tax it at the source, and tax nothing else.

That means no tax on the people who process and trade those products: the manufacturers who turn metal into goods or the merchants who ship those goods abroad. The manufacturers and merchants are delighted.

One group is much less delighted: the members of the *Ferme générale*. Remember them? It's the private company that collects all the kingdom's taxes. Naturally, the more taxes there are—and the more kinds of taxes—the more they have to feed on. The *fermiers généraux* come out firmly against Turgot.

Turgot pushes ahead anyway. Abolish every tax on the grain trade—the most important trade in a world of poor people who mostly live on bread. Get rid of all of them.

Unfortunately, economics isn't an exact science, and sometimes it has a cruel sense of timing. That very year, the grain harvest is a disaster. Grain prices shoot through the roof, and the poor starve. Whose fault is it? Turgot's, of course, because he removed the taxes and controls on the grain trade. The result, as people see it, is that grain costs more. They don't look past that, and it's hard to blame them when they're starving.

Grain riots break out. Turgot has to put them down by force, and his popularity starts to sink.

The king asks him, “What we've done so far clearly isn't enough. What's next? Bankruptcy?”

“No, no, no.” Turgot won't hear of it. “No bankruptcy. It's dishonest.”

“Then do we raise taxes?”

“Never.” Turgot is a dogmatic liberal. Taxes are bad in themselves. He won't raise them.

“Then do we borrow even more?”

No, that's no good either. The national debt is bad and has to come down.

Now the king asks, “Then what exactly are we supposed to do?”

Well, there aren't many options left. Turgot says they have to spend less. Cut ministry budgets. Cut the lavish pensions.

They try it. Not surprisingly, Turgot makes even more enemies. All the kingdom's most important and influential people collect lavish salaries and pensions. His popularity collapses.

The king is outraged. “What? Is no one willing to make one small sacrifice?” The celebrated line is: “Only Monsieur Turgot and I love the people.”

By then, the opposition is so fierce that Turgot is dismissed. He lasts less than two years in office.

## 10. Clugny's lottery and brief tenure

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00817–U-00855 -->

His successor, Clugny de Nuits, comes in full of confidence. A contemporary account tells this story and preserves the satirical epitaph that followed: “One day, when Monsieur de Vaines had gone to see him, he told him, to reassure him about his condition, that he should remember the well-known maxim that a controller-general never dies in office. 'Oh! Well,' he replied cheerfully, 'I shall prove the proverb wrong.' [...] They did not fail to compose an epitaph for him [...]: Here lies a controller worthy of mourning, / Loving France greatly and life not at all, / Gladly consenting that it be taken from him / Once he had extinguished the state's debts.”

They need money, and Clugny de Nuits has a brilliant idea: the lottery. It doesn't exist yet, so they invent it. It's El Dorado. Put tickets on sale, people buy them, and then hand a small share of the proceeds back as prizes. Fantastic.

And remember, this is eighteenth-century France, deeply corrupt and rotten. Yet plenty of people criticize this new lottery. They say, “It's shameful for the state to make money by preying on people's urge to gamble.” That's more criticism than you often hear today.

After that—after that—Clugny de Nuits is out of ideas. The lottery isn't enough, and the hole in the budget keeps growing. He's in trouble, but he never has to answer for it. Five months after taking office, he dies suddenly.

The gossips—no, not the gossips. The songs. Political satire and *chansonniers* are already around, and the songs make the rounds: he had promised to die in office. As for Louis XVI, looking back on his decision to appoint Clugny de Nuits, he says, “I think we've made another mistake.” Bring on the next one.

## 11. Necker, the American war, and life annuities

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00856–U-00962 -->

In comes another economist with a miracle cure. This one is Jacques Necker, a Swiss man from Geneva. He's the exact opposite of Turgot: taxes and government intervention in the economy. That's how they'll set things right.

There's still that small problem: Necker is a Protestant too, so they can't make him minister. Instead, they name him director-general, put a figurehead in the minister's chair, and let Necker run the show.

Necker starts putting his theory into practice: duties, taxes, government intervention. But then a new problem comes along. The American Revolution breaks out. You remember—1776. England immediately finds itself in trouble.

Why does that matter here? Because England and France are enemies. England won the Seven Years' War just a few years earlier and stripped France of its colonies. Now the French see a chance for revenge, and they call it correctly.

People often forget this. The American Revolution—let's call it the first great moment in the birth of a democracy—wins its war against England with help from the absolute French monarchy. France sends fleets and troops to support the Americans, and the Americans ultimately win.

American independence is a great French victory too. It's 1784, and Louis XVI is the first king of France to win a major war against the country's eternal enemy, England. He has never been so loved, never been so popular. If someone had told him, “Nine years from now, they'll cut off your head in a public square,” no one would have believed it.

Naturally, there's one small problem: the war has cost a fortune. The national debt has reached staggering levels. Monsieur Necker needs to raise money, so he comes up with something new.

The government will sell treasury bonds. But instead of paying back a set amount plus interest when the bond matures, these bonds pay a life annuity right away. You put down a certain amount and immediately start collecting a small payment every year.

Necker is a serious man. He's done his homework, studied life expectancy, and calculated how long people live on average. He sets the annuity accordingly.

Then he has another idea. He still has the lottery on his mind. So here's the plan: sell these treasury bonds—let's call them that, though they're really life annuities—to the public at a fixed price, in ten-thousand-euro units. Most pay five hundred euros a year for life. I'm modernizing the figures, naturally. But there's a lottery too. A few bonds are drawn at random, and the lucky owners get half a million euros a year for life.

He has combined bonds, treasury certificates, and the lottery. It's a huge success. And every payment is calibrated precisely to his life-expectancy calculations.

But there's another problem. The annuity doesn't have to be tied to the buyer's life. A subscriber can name someone else—the measuring life—and the payments keep coming as long as that person lives. The annuity can be resold later, but the measuring life chosen at the start never changes.

The bankers in Geneva see the opportunity immediately. They buy annuities and name carefully selected young girls from Geneva as the measuring lives. Many of the girls have been inoculated against smallpox. The bankers then pool thirty or fifty of those lives and sell small shares in the combined income. If one girl dies, an investor loses only that piece of the return; all the other annuities keep paying. The French Crown hasn't adjusted its rates enough for these exceptionally young, carefully selected lives, so the loans end up costing far more than expected. [N-001]

Within a few years, this magnificent invention will push the treasury even closer to ruin. But no one sees it right away. The annuities are doing well. Everyone buys them, their value rises, everyone is happy, and Monsieur Necker looks like a magician. They've even paid for the American war without raising taxes.

Every so often, Necker checks the numbers and feels sick when he sees how high the national debt is climbing. But no one else hears about it. This is an absolute monarchy. Newspapers print only what the government wants, and a journalist who says too much winds up in the Bastille. The public knows nothing. Only Monsieur Necker, alone in his ministry office, knows the debt is rising, rising, rising.

Eventually, he too goes to the king. “Your Majesty, we have to cut spending. We have to cut the court's expenses and those lavish pensions.”

But Necker understands something people don't always understand today. You sometimes hear that there's no point going after lavish pensions or the cost of politics because they're tiny next to the national debt. I still think they should be cut—not that you care what I think. But their real value is symbolic. They create a sense that everyone belongs to the same community, everyone shares the sacrifice, and no one gets to indulge in excess. Still, cutting political expenses alone won't balance a budget.

In Necker's day, everyone can see the court's spending, so the French attack it relentlessly and come to hate Marie Antoinette and her courtiers. But it makes up just one percent of the budget. There really isn't that much to cut.

In the end, Necker's miracle cure fails. The annuities don't work, the hole in the budget is more terrifying than ever, and Necker is dismissed too.

## 12. Calonne's credit strategy reaches the wall

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00963–U-01017 -->

Then comes the last one: Calonne. He has a different theory. The national debt is essential. Trying to reduce it would strangle us. We have to keep growing the debt. We have to keep borrowing.

But how do we persuade people to lend us money when they no longer trust us? We win back their trust. No one can find out about the terrifying hole in the budget. We have to show them we're rich instead. Spend.

Calonne's cure is government spending everywhere. Not to create jobs, as Keynes will later propose in a different context. No, no—to create credit. The monarchy has to look fabulously rich so people will lend to it without a second thought. No one can be allowed to discover the truth.

So the government spends everywhere, and naturally the king and queen are delighted. The queen has wanted a new château for ages—the Château de Saint-Cloud—but her husband keeps telling her, “My dear, be patient. We can't afford it right now.”

Calonne says, “Why not? Of course we can afford it.” They buy the Château de Saint-Cloud for six million francs. Parties, receptions, court balls, music, opera, theater, concerts—spending, spending, spending.

Why? The court is happy, the bankers are happy, the stock market rises, and everyone is thrilled.

That brings us to the summer of 1786, when Calonne runs the numbers. Interest on the national debt—just the interest, not the principal—eats up fifty-one percent of the budget. For comparison, Italy today spends roughly eight percent of its state budget on debt interest. Here it's fifty-one percent.

Of course, an eighteenth-century budget looks very different from ours. The next largest item, at twenty-six percent, is the military: the army, the fleet, and the fortresses. The French monarchy spends two percent on welfare and education. But no matter how you look at it, spending fifty-one percent on interest is unthinkable.

Louis XVI—and I'm making this up; it isn't in the record—must have said, “We've made another mistake.” What we know is that he fires Calonne. And now? As the country learns the truth, it makes one demand. That's where we'll end today.

## 13. The Estates-General and the transfer of sovereignty

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01018–U-01089 -->

The country demands that the king call the Estates-General. But what does that mean?

The Estates-General is an ancient institution. Medieval French kings called it regularly because no one in medieval France was going to hand over a penny in taxes just because the king announced, “I've decided to raise taxes.”

To impose a tax, the king had to call an assembly. The princes, barons, and nobles came. So did the bishops, archbishops, and other clergy, along with the bourgeois merchants from the towns—the people with money. These three groups, the nobility, the clergy, and the merchants, were known as the three estates.

The king and his ministers had to make their case to the whole assembly. “We need money.” They had to say how much and what for. The assembly debated the request and might approve it. Without the Estates-General, a medieval French king couldn't impose a penny in taxes.

Naturally, kings found that kind of oversight restrictive. We started with Louis XIV's France as an absolute monarchy. France became an absolute monarchy in the seventeenth century because the king grew powerful enough to impose taxes without calling the Estates-General.

It last meets in 1614. After that, the kings of France rule for a century and a half without it. But people remember that it once existed—especially the nobles, who carried a lot of weight there. They were the First Estate, and the clergy was the Second. Everyone else belonged to the Third Estate, which had little influence. Still, it had to be there because the merchants were the ones with the money.

By 1788, people in France are saying, “The Estates-General—how long has it been since we called it? Look where we've ended up. The king can't go on like this. He has to listen to the country.”

The nobles push hardest, never imagining that three or four years later they'll climb the steps of the guillotine alongside the king. “We want the Estates-General. We want the monarchy brought under control.”

The king is forced to promise he'll call the Estates-General, and he does. In desperation, he also brings Necker back into the ministry.

The king and Necker open the Estates-General by trying to discuss the hole in the budget. The delegates are stunned to learn that it's far worse than anyone thought. But then they say, “We don't know how to fix it. And that's not even the most important problem anymore.”

The real problem is that absolute monarchy can't continue. The country has to be governed differently from now on. The king must accept the Estates-General as a permanent voice in government, an assembly that represents the country. He can no longer rule alone.

You know what happens. The king hesitates, then tries to dissolve the Estates-General. It refuses. “We are the country. We are the people. We hold the power. Without us, the king is nothing.”

The king tries to use the army to break it up by force. Paris rises, and on July 14, 1789, the people storm the Bastille. From that moment on, the Estates-General becomes the National Assembly, the parliament, and quickly establishes itself as the only real legal power in France.

## 14. Revolution, Terror, and the tax farmers' reckoning

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-01090–U-01116 -->

For a while, the king goes along with the new arrangement. But deep down, he can't stand the humiliation. He starts plotting with foreign powers to invade France. The plot is uncovered, and the king and queen are arrested.

The tension is overwhelming. People are terrified. Heads begin to fall, and the French Revolution enters the Terror.

One last thing before we finish. Amid all these beheadings, trial follows trial. Anyone accused of plotting with the king to overthrow the new order, restore the absolute monarchy, or profit from the old system is hauled into court.

The mood is simple: now the profiteers will answer for what they've done. If there isn't a penny left, it's because they all had their snouts in the trough. Now the people will judge them.

Then one day, all the surviving *fermiers généraux*—the financiers who ran tax collection under the monarchy—are put on trial together. On May 8, 1794, twenty-eight former *fermiers généraux* go to the guillotine together.

There's much more we could say, but by now I think the larger meaning of this story is clear to everyone. Thank you. Thank you all, have a good evening—and I'll see you next year.
