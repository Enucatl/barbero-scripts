# La bancarotta dello stato: le cause della Rivoluzione Francese

Good evening, everyone. Tonight you are, how shall I put it, the guinea pigs for a lecture I have been working on—and perhaps ought to work on a little longer. You will judge.

The subject is not one I usually deal with: the financial causes of the French Revolution. But it has always intrigued me, and I have taken the opportunity to try to understand it better. I am not entirely sure I have understood everything. What is certain, though, is that when you ask why the French Revolution broke out, you find a great many answers.

There are books and books called *The Causes of the French Revolution*. They speak of the people’s hatred of the privileges of nobles and clergy; of a prosperous bourgeoisie of merchants and industrialists, emerging in France but with no political weight and wanting more; and then of the poverty of the poor, bad harvests, the price of grain—and therefore bread—shooting up, discontent, popular unrest, hunger.

But what we are going to talk about tonight is something else: the colossal hole in the French monarchy’s budget. Not simply a century of extravagance, as one sometimes hears, but repeated wars, heavy debt service, a fragmented and politically constrained tax system, and reforms that failed or were reversed. By the 1780s, those problems had produced a fiscal crisis. [C-002]

In the end it forced the king to take the step we all remember from school as the beginning of the French Revolution: he summoned the Estates-General. Louis XVI did not know that, four years later, they would cut off his head. But the financial catastrophe was what forced the convocation; it did not, by itself, make the Revolution inevitable. [C-001]

So that is what we shall look at. Faced with this disaster, the French monarchy repeatedly tried to find a solution by entrusting itself to economists who had the right prescription, economists who knew how the situation could be put right. In short, the idea that one can manage the economy—and even revive it—on the basis of economists’ theories is certainly not a recent invention. Eighteenth-century France is full of attempts of this kind.

And the striking thing is that the prescriptions will be completely different. One minister wants freer markets and fewer taxes; another wants a lottery; another wants paper money and credit; another believes that the state must spend magnificently in order to look creditworthy. They arrive amid applause, remain a short time, and leave amid insults. The problem is not that France lacks ideas. It is that every idea collides with interests, panic, war, and a state that has already promised more than it can pay.

So, France in the eighteenth century. An absolute monarchy. What does that mean?

It means that, in theory, the king could do whatever he wished. There was no formal procedure requiring him to ask someone’s approval. He could give orders without explaining them: by the king’s will. With a document called a *lettre de cachet* (lettruh duh ka-SHAY), the king could order a person detained without trial, even in the Bastille. He was the source of legislation, and he did not need a standing national representative body to approve ordinary taxes. [C-003]

Now, “absolute” does not mean that there were literally no limits, or that every order worked without resistance. Edicts normally had to be registered; the *parlements*, provincial estates, privileges, custom, and the sheer capacity of the administration all mattered. But compared with a system in which a representative assembly must approve taxation, the concentration of authority was striking.

The point is not that the king woke each morning and personally managed every detail of a great country. Nobody could do that. The point is that the whole structure ran upward to him: ministers advised, councils discussed, officials administered—but the will at the top was his.

In the Middle Ages it had not always been like this. We will not linger over it, but monarchies had not always had this form. For centuries kings had faced powers within the kingdom that balanced their own, above all the great nobility. From the time of Louis XIV, the Sun King, the French monarchy was absolute in that sense.

You know the familiar formula: “L’État, c’est moi”—“I am the State.” [Q-001] That was the personal idea of monarchy. Of course there were advisers, secretaries, ministers. But they were the king’s servants.

And for the monarchy of the Sun King, at the beginning of the eighteenth century, we have a fascinating witness: the memoirs of a great nobleman of the age, the Duke of Saint-Simon (san-see-MON).

Saint-Simon lived through the mature years and old age of the Sun King, and then well into the middle of the eighteenth century. He wrote memoirs that are a vast work—seven or eight volumes of a thousand pages each—full of gossip, anecdotes, and reflections on what it meant to live under Louis XIV’s absolute monarchy.

He represented forces in the kingdom that were not at all happy that the monarchy should be absolute. At the time of the Revolution, protest would come from bourgeois figures, intellectuals, the Enlightenment. Here we are at the beginning of the eighteenth century, almost a century earlier. The protesters are the great nobles: princes, dukes like Saint-Simon, men accustomed to counting for a great deal in the kingdom, and above all irritated by the idea that the king was an absolute power.

We should not mistake Saint-Simon for a modern democrat. His was an aristocratic critique of monarchy, and the *Memoirs* were composed retrospectively, decades after many of the events they describe. Still, they give us a magnificent, hostile picture of the last years of the Sun King’s reign. [C-004]

These were the years when France was engaged in a disastrous war against, practically, the whole world: the War of the Spanish Succession. The war was going very badly. And Saint-Simon, writing many years later, identifies a precise moment from which it went badly: the catastrophe of Turin.

You know the siege of Turin in 1706. We have all heard of it: Pietro Micca; then Prince Eugene arrives and frees the city from the French siege. Here in Turin, we all know the episode. We might think it concerns us because we are Turinese, and that in itself it was a small, provincial affair.

But Saint-Simon, decades later, says something quite different. In his memoirs he writes: “Never did a battle cost fewer soldiers than the one at Turin, never was the enemy’s withdrawal calmer or allowed more freedom of choice, never were the consequences more dreadful or more rapid. [...] the battle of Turin cost all of Italy.” [Q-002]

For Saint-Simon, Turin is the moment when France loses Italy. The defeat opens the way to the loss of Milan and to Habsburg expansion across the peninsula. [C-005]

For Saint-Simon, though, this is where France’s ruin begins: when France is pushed out of Italy. From that point on, he says, France no longer counts for anything there. And from there begins our ruin.

We are now in 1710, four years after the disaster of Turin.

France is exhausted. It has just come through the most terrible winter of the century. There is no money; the deficit is appalling; the king is drowned in debt. So he does not pay—not his creditors, not his soldiers at the front. [C-006]

Well, not quite. He does not pay in coin. We are still in a world before banknotes have become normal money. The serious money, for large dealings, is gold and silver. Small change circulates by convention; you cannot buy six eggs with a silver crown. But gold and silver are what people trust, and by 1710 those who have them keep them locked away.

The Crown pays with promises: mint notes, in effect IOUs. One such note says: “FOR the sum of one thousand livres, which I will pay in one year to the bearer.” [Q-003]

Still, everybody understands the problem. If I hold a royal note for a thousand livres, payable next year, and you offer me a hundred in sound coin today, I may be very happy to hand you the paper. The notes circulate at a discount. Those with cash can buy them cheaply; people with access to government and the exchanges can make money from the difference. Everyone else sees the value melt away. [C-006]

It is a very simple mechanism. The state has paid you, but it has paid you with a promise that nobody trusts. You need food, rent, wages, supplies. You cannot wait a year to discover whether the Mint will honour the paper. So you sell it at a loss. The people with reserves, information, and friends in the right offices can collect these promises cheaply and play the exchange. The people who are already squeezed are forced to accept whatever they can get.

The king may not pay, but he collects. Taxes multiply. And Saint-Simon gives us his savage retrospective picture: “The capitation tax, doubled and tripled at the arbitrary will of the provincial intendants; goods and foodstuffs of every kind taxed at four times their value ... all this crushed nobles and commoners, lords and churchmen ... [the king] drew the blood of all his subjects without distinction, squeezed them even to the pus, and enriched an infinite army of tax contractors and employees ... in whose hands the largest and best part remained.” [Q-004]

It is polemic, certainly. But it points to a real institutional problem. The early modern state did not possess the large administrative machine we associate with a modern tax office. It often leased collection rights to private financiers. They advanced a fixed sum to the Crown, then collected particular taxes—the salt tax, customs, excises, and much else—and sought their profit from the operation. Over time these interests were organized in the Ferme générale (FAIRM zhay-nay-RAHL), the General Farm. It did bring revenue to the state, but its contracts, privileges, and private collectors made the system opaque and deeply unpopular. [C-007]

This is worth pausing over. We are accustomed to complaining that the modern state has too many employees and too much bureaucracy. In early eighteenth-century France the opposite problem was just as serious: the state did not have enough of its own people to collect its own taxes. It leased the job to entrepreneurs. They paid the king in advance, then went out to recover the money—and, naturally, to make a profit. The more taxes there were, and the more complicated they were, the more opportunities there were for those financiers.

So Saint-Simon's complaint is: the king raises taxes, raises them again, and yet the money still sticks in the hands of the contractors.

At this point the finance minister, Nicolas Desmarets (day-ma-RAY), decides that something must be done. And you will notice several times this evening that we are talking about things which sound unexpectedly familiar.

He proposes a tax on wealth: a proportional levy on property. Most taxes were instead taxes on transactions—on things bought and sold, rather like a forest of excises and indirect taxes. There were assessments of wealth, but often rough local allocations rather than a systematic declaration of what each person owned. [C-008]

Earlier reformers had proposed something cleaner: abolish this jungle of dues and replace it with one general tax on wealth. One tax, in other words—not called a flat tax, because Europe spoke French then, not English, but you see the family resemblance. The financiers who lived from the existing system did not like it, and it went nowhere.

Desmarets, in the desperate final years of Louis XIV, tries again. But he does not abolish the old taxes. He adds the new one to them. This is the *dixième* (dee-ZYEM), a tenth intended as an exceptional wartime levy. [C-008]

And now we see how decision-making works in an absolute monarchy. The king can decide, yes; he also has councils, advisers, technical experts. Desmarets needs a commission to examine his project so that he can say to the king: here is a plan approved by the experts.

According to Saint-Simon, he appoints a commission containing his brother, his son-in-law, and his brothers-in-law. The commission approves. It is a wonderful example of expert validation. [C-009]

Saint-Simon's objection is more revealing still. People were accustomed to endless taxation, he says, but not to declaring their property. Until then, one estimated. Now the new levy required inquiries, disclosures, a declaration of wealth. For us, this may seem ordinary. To Saint-Simon it is nightmare material: family secrets exposed, quarrels between husbands and wives and between brothers, private affairs made public, credit ruined because everyone will know what everybody else truly possesses.

He says even the king was troubled. Louis XIV, accustomed to enormous taxes, was supposedly uneasy at requiring such things of his subjects. So he turns to his Jesuit confessor, Father Le Tellier.

Le Tellier returns with an opinion from learned doctors of the Sorbonne: “all his subjects’ goods were his own property and ... when he took them, he took only what belonged to him.” The king, Saint-Simon continues, is relieved; he no longer doubts that what he leaves his subjects is “a matter of pure grace.” [Q-005]

There, says Saint-Simon, you have the summit of absolute monarchy. The king can decide anything; indeed, everything belongs to him already. It is a wonderful story because it makes the logic brutally clear: taxes are no longer presented as a bargain between ruler and country, but as the sovereign taking what is his.

The tax still has to go before the Council of Finance. And Saint-Simon gives a wonderfully theatrical account, which he says Chancellor Pontchartrain related to him the next day. Desmarets enters with the edict in his bag. Before anyone can examine it, the king announces that he has read the proposal and finds it excellent. Desmarets explains the war, the danger, the need for exceptional measures, and reads the long edict straight through.

When he finishes, silence. A councillor asks whether he might take it home and study it before offering an opinion. The king replies, in effect: the minister has already studied it; it cannot be improved; do you approve? [Q-006]

It is a perfect little theatre of power. There is a council, there are experts, there is a long document, there is a request for deliberation. And then the king makes clear that deliberation is not what this meeting is for. The plan has been approved before anyone has considered it. The councillors understand, and the machine moves on.

Saint-Simon says the affair was then “signed, sealed, registered amid stifled sobs,” and later published amid pitiful complaints. He calls the planners a “bureau of cannibals.” The yield, he says, came nowhere near what they imagined, and the king paid nobody a penny more than before. [Q-006]

The levy disappoints, ordinary taxpayers bear it, and well-placed financiers remain hard to assess. [C-010]

That is the situation in 1715, when the Sun King dies. His heir, the future Louis XV, is a child; Louis XIV has outlived son and grandson. France is governed by the Regent, Philippe, Duke of Orléans (fee-LEEP dor-lay-AHN), from 1715 to 1723.

The financial position is catastrophic. There is serious talk of bankruptcy. A sovereign default is brutally simple: the king declares that the old debts will not be paid. Many people are ruined; the kingdom obtains relief. Estimates differ with the kinds of claims counted, but the debt was commonly described as many years—roughly a decade—of royal revenue. [C-011]

Ten years of revenue: that is the scale people had in mind. It gives you some sense of the abyss. A government today may carry debt equal to several years of income and still function; but this is a monarchy whose credit is already damaged, whose taxes are hard to collect, and whose creditors have learned that royal promises may arrive in the form of paper nobody wants.

And while they discuss this, a Scotsman arrives in Paris promising a solution: John Law. Law comes from a banking family—and bankers are everywhere in this story. I shall try not to speak badly of them, naturally, given the setting in which we are meeting. You can judge for yourselves.

Law is a banker, but a rather unusual one. A few years earlier, in London, he has killed a man in a duel over a woman. English law is severe. He is condemned to death and sits in prison for a long time waiting to be hanged.

Then, because he has good lawyers, the charge is reduced to manslaughter. For manslaughter one can be released on bail. Law walks out on bail, and then thinks it wiser to slip out of the United Kingdom altogether. He takes refuge on the Continent—still, of course, a man escaping British justice—and there he begins to publish works on economics. [C-012]

It is worth telling you this because the proposal he develops is extraordinarily modern in some respects. This duellist, convicted killer, fugitive, is thinking seriously about the nature of money.

Law says: why should a country rely on gold and silver? Their quantity changes; people hoard them; new discoveries alter their value. Metal has uses and value, of course—Law did not say it was literally worthless—but it is a poor foundation for a stable monetary system.

There is a bold intuition here. If the quantity of money is too small, commerce chokes. People keep coin in chests because they are afraid; trade slows because nobody will part with the thing everyone trusts. Why should the wealth of a country be held hostage to how much silver happens to arrive from mines, or to how much gold people decide to hide? Law thinks money is a tool. If it is managed intelligently, paper can do the work of metal and allow the economy to breathe.

In his 1705 proposal, written for Scotland, Law proposes paper money supported by land and managed so that its quantity matches demand. “What I shall propose,” he writes, “is to make money of land equal to its value; and that money to be equal in value to silver-money; and not lyable to fall in value as silver-money falls.” [Q-007]

The underlying picture is wonderfully eighteenth-century. There is one true wealth in the kingdom: land. So, says Law, measure all the land in the kingdom; issue notes in proportion to it; and let everyone know that the notes rest on something fixed and real. One might say: this note will buy a hectare of land. The public will know that its value is guaranteed, fixed, immutable.

From the Continent, where he has gone after slipping away on bail, Law sends this project to London. The English government receives the scheme of this Scottish fugitive, this convicted killer, and reacts more or less as you would expect: this man is mad. England does not accept it. [C-012]

So Law decides to try his fortune somewhere else and arrives in Paris. France, staring at bankruptcy, is more receptive. And there is a Regent: Philippe, Duke of Orléans. He is, by all accounts, a likeable and well-intentioned man. He also has some large defects. He is enormously lazy; he does not much like work; he spends his time among pleasures and revels, because he cannot resist orgies, drunkenness, or women. Yet he means well.

There is another little defect. In a world still officially and deeply Catholic, Orléans is an atheist. He does not believe in God, and likes people to know it, because it makes him feel like an intelligent man. He displays this irreligion. His most spectacular parties, for example, are deliberately scheduled for Good Friday.

And Saint-Simon leaves us another character sketch. Of Orléans he writes that the Regent tried to persuade himself there was no God, yet believed in the devil enough to hope to see him and converse with him. “This contrast cannot be understood,” Saint-Simon says, “and yet it is extremely common.” [Q-008]

Saint-Simon adds that the Regent spends serious money on people who promise to summon the devil for him. In God he does not believe; in the devil, apparently, he does. So he is forever surrounded by charlatans and cheats. That is the man who governs France when John Law arrives in Paris with his grand solution.

They are made to understand each other. The financial position is ruinous: the debt is spoken of as ten years of revenue. A state can carry a great weight of debt; but ten times its annual revenue, in a monarchy whose promises already circulate at a discount—that is another thing. The Regent is not an economist. But he is curious, reckless enough, and desperate enough to let Law try. [C-013]

Law first founds the Banque générale (bahnk zhay-nay-RAHL) in 1716, a private bank. It issues notes and takes in coin. In 1718 the Crown takes it over and it becomes the Banque royale (bahnk rwah-YAHL). The notes are bearer promises: a Banque générale note says, “The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight fifty écus in specie”; a 1719 Banque royale note promises “one hundred livres tournois in silver specie.” [Q-009]

What does “pay the bearer at sight” mean? Think of the wording on older banknotes—on old dollar notes too: payable to the bearer on demand. If I have a note for a thousand, I can take it to the bank, hand it over, and say: give me the thousand. The bank promises to pay whoever bears the note; it does not need to know who originally received it.

That is what makes the paper useful. I can pass it to you; you can pass it to somebody else; and everybody accepts it because, in theory, anyone holding it can go to the bank and demand the value written on its face. Nowadays a dollar is simply a dollar, an abstract unit. But the old wording still tells you where the idea came from: this piece of paper is a promise, and the bank stands behind the promise.

At first they seem wonderfully practical. They are standardized pieces of paper, recognizable and easier to use than sacks of coin. A person can accept one in payment because the bank says it will pay the bearer on demand. People keep their gold louis safely at home and use the bank’s paper in ordinary dealings. That is exactly what Law wants: paper circulating, metal standing behind it, and trust growing from habit.

This matters because we must keep three things separate. First, there are old royal debts and paper claims. Second, there are the bank's notes: money-like promises. Third, there are shares in Law's companies: ownership claims on a commercial enterprise. In the drama of 1719 and 1720 they become entangled, but they are not the same thing. [C-014]

The bank helps turn old claims into new paper and makes its notes acceptable in dealings with the state. Old royal IOUs are gathered in; new notes are put into circulation. And for a while, wonderfully, the public debt appears to be shrinking. It really does appear to be shrinking.

There is one difficulty: the bank cannot honestly guarantee every one of the notes now being printed simply with the capital in its vault. So Law has another brilliant idea. France is at the beginning of colonial expansion. It has Canada, Louisiana, the Antilles; it imagines immense enlargements in America and immense riches arriving from there.

Alongside the bank, Law's Company of the West—soon enlarged into the Compagnie des Indes (kom-pah-NYEE day ZAND)—acquires, step by step, colonial and trading privileges, then tobacco, minting, tax-collection and debt-management functions. The Crown transfers revenues and monopolies; the company undertakes financial tasks connected with the royal debt. [C-015]

So the system advances by stages. The old royal promises are gathered in. New notes circulate. Then comes the great commercial company, with the prospect of Canada, Louisiana, the Antilles, the Indies, tobacco, sugar, trade over the oceans—an Eldorado, as it seems. What does a monopoly of colonial trade mean? Tobacco, sugar: an immense business. And the company is not merely a distant colonial venture. It is tied ever more closely to the state: it receives monopolies, handles revenues, and takes on functions connected with the debt. The same financial world appears to hold the future of trade, the machinery of taxation, and the paper in everyone’s hands.

Now imagine the attraction. You hold a dubious old claim on a bankrupt-looking state. You can exchange it for a share in a company that seems to command an imperial commercial future, and whose business is interwoven with the Crown. Louisiana, the Mississippi, the Indies, trade, monopolies, tax revenues: the whole thing glitters.

Shares rise. More shares are issued. Notes help finance purchases. Credit and confidence feed each other. The expectation is not merely that Louisiana will become rich; it is also that the state will support the system and that the price will continue to rise. That is the Mississippi bubble—not a magic trick in which paper literally creates wealth, but a feedback loop among shares, notes, debt conversion, state privileges, and belief. [C-014] [C-015]

And when the price rises, it seems to prove that everyone was right. A person who bought yesterday has made money today; therefore more people want to buy tomorrow. The new buyers need notes, the notes support the purchases, the price rises again, and every rise becomes an advertisement for the next rise. It is one of the earliest great financial bubbles: not because people had suddenly discovered that paper was wealth, but because paper claims, state power, colonial dreams, and speculation all began to reinforce one another.

This is when the word *millionnaire* enters French political language, associated with the sudden fortunes of the System. We should not picture every Parisian suddenly becoming rich. But contemporaries did see extraordinary paper fortunes, particularly around the rue Quincampoix (roo kank-pwan). [C-016]

You can picture the fever of it. A narrow Paris street behind the exchange becomes the place where everyone wants to be. Yesterday's buyer is richer today; a man who has made money in the morning can talk of making another fortune in the afternoon. It is not merely a calculation about Louisiana. It is the intoxicating sight of other people becoming rich before one's eyes.

Law himself becomes immensely powerful. There is still one lever he does not hold: the office of Controller-General of Finances. There is a small problem. In this intensely Catholic kingdom, a Protestant Scot cannot be finance minister. Law decides that Catholicism has always interested him very much, begins visiting the Jesuits, converts, and can now be made Controller-General. [C-017]

It is an extraordinary concentration of power. The Scottish adventurer is now at the centre of a bank, a company, colonial monopolies, tax collection, and the finances of the French Crown. The Regent believes he has found a magician. Paris begins to believe it too.

And then confidence begins to turn.

The government tries to keep the system alive by restricting the use and private holding of gold and silver. In February 1720, private holdings above 500 livres were restricted, with planned searches and confiscations. [C-018] In a way it has an astonishingly modern sound: if you want people to use a new form of money, put obstacles in the way of cash. Law's order goes further. It is forbidden to keep more than 500 livres in gold at home. Anonymous denunciations begin; police search houses for outlawed gold.

It is the moment at which a theory of paper money turns into compulsion. People are told, in effect: do not hide gold; do not keep silver; use the paper. But money rests on confidence. The more insistently a government orders confidence, the more it advertises that confidence is failing.

As the shares cease to rise, holders seek notes; as notes accumulate, they seek coin or another exit; and a system built on confidence discovers that confidence is not an asset one can order people to possess.

Then some large speculators decide to get their notes paid in gold. The first, naturally enough, include great princes of the blood: the Duke of Bourbon, the Prince of Conti—people with very good antennae and very good information. Insider trading, after all, was not invented in our age. One day people see the king's cousin, the Duke of Bourbon, arrive at the bank in person with a procession of lackeys carrying cases, and exchange his notes for gold.

At that point, naturally, there is panic. In July 1720 crowds gather at the Royal Bank near rue Vivienne. In the crush on 17 July, ten or twelve people are suffocated. The next day the bank closes until further order. [C-019]

The aftermath is not that the state simply prints notes and its debt evaporates. Claims are converted, restricted, devalued, and later subjected to the Visa settlement. Small claims of 500 livres or less escaped the formal haircut, but that did not mean everyone was made whole: the resulting certificates could trade far below face value. Larger claims suffered heavier reductions. [C-020]

This is the dark side of the apparent miracle. An old creditor may have exchanged a doubtful royal claim for notes or for shares in a dazzling company. On paper he may even have become rich. But when the paper loses value, or when the rules of conversion change, the loss has not vanished: it has fallen on the person holding the claim at that moment. The state has not created wealth out of nothing. It has changed the form of its obligations and, in the collapse, shifted part of the pain onto its creditors.

The Crown did reduce some burdens and reorganize the wreckage into government annuities. But the overall public-debt burden after the settlement was roughly comparable to what it had been before the System, not magically abolished. What had truly evaporated was a great deal of private wealth measured in the paper prices of the boom. And the experience left paper money deeply distrusted for much of the eighteenth century—though certainly not for two centuries; the Revolution would produce assignats within seventy years. [C-020]

The men who sign the notes are easy targets; Law has warning enough to leave France. He goes eventually to Venice, beyond French reach, where he will die years later. The immediate experiment is over. The underlying problem is not.

Louis XV reigns for a long time. He will be remembered as Louis the Beloved; historians are rather less affectionate about his abilities and about the results of his reign. France borrows again, fights again, and suffers the great defeat of the Seven Years' War. In 1763 it loses Canada and much of its imperial position. War debt, constrained credit, and an unreformed revenue system remain. [C-021]

There are recoveries, of course. France remains a great power, a brilliant country, the country of the Enlightenment. Voltaire and the French philosophers dominate drawing rooms across Europe. France feels strong, rich, prosperous, the cradle of civilization. Louis XV enjoys his pleasures and his expensive mistresses, Madame de Pompadour above all; ministers borrow. For nearly fifty years everyone pretends not to see. But that is part of the danger: a great power can look splendid while its accounts are becoming impossible. The debt that Law’s collapse had helped to cut down begins to grow again with every war and every new loan.

When Louis XV dies in 1774, his grandson Louis XVI succeeds. Nobody can imagine that not even twenty years later he will leave his head on the guillotine. He is king of an absolute monarchy which still considers itself the most powerful country in the world. The reports from his tutors say, rather unkindly, that he is a total nonentity; but he is well-intentioned. He comes to the throne wanting to understand how things work. The Enlightenment has long been talking about reforming the Ancien Régime; he listens: let us make these reforms.

When Louis XVI succeeds in 1774, he inherits a serious debt-and-credit problem—not yet exactly the terminal catastrophe of 1788, but a problem grave enough to make reform urgent. He begins with a great name of political economy: Turgot. [C-022]

Louis XVI wants to be remembered as a king who did good. He has, it is true, the burden of a court that the public sees as extravagant, and of Marie-Antoinette as an easy symbol for every complaint. But the king does not simply want to squander money. He wants a remedy. So once again France looks for the economist with the right prescription.

Turgot is greeted in the salons almost as a saviour. Julie de Lespinasse writes to Guibert in 1775, speaking of Turgot and the newly appointed Malesherbes together: “be assured that good will be done, and that it will be done well, because enlightenment will guide virtue and love of the public good. Never, no never, have two men more virtuous, more disinterested, more active been brought together.” And she adds: “What bad times for rogues and courtiers!” [Q-010]

You can hear the atmosphere: the Enlightenment at the door of power. If intelligent, virtuous people govern, surely the country can be repaired.

This is one of those moments history regularly offers us. A man arrives with a reputation for integrity, intelligence, and devotion to the public good. Everyone says: at last, an honest man. At last, someone who understands. And yet good intentions and personal virtue are not a guarantee that policy will succeed. Sometimes they make a collision with reality even more dramatic.

Turgot is a physiocratic-leaning reformer. He believes agriculture and the net product of land are central to wealth, but his programme is broader than a simple slogan. He seeks freer grain and labour markets, abolition of the corvée and guild restrictions, fiscal reform that reaches privileged landowners, and administrative change. [C-023]

The reasoning has an austere elegance. Real wealth comes from the land: crops, timber, minerals, everything that is drawn from the earth. Tax the source of wealth, and stop smothering commerce with a forest of tolls, duties, restrictions, and petty controls. Merchants and manufacturers have every reason to welcome this. The people who collect the old maze of taxes, the *fermiers généraux*, have every reason to hate it.

He liberalizes the internal grain trade in September 1774. The idea is that freer circulation will bring grain where it is needed and prevent local scarcity from becoming catastrophe. But bad harvest conditions, high prices, long-standing suspicion of grain merchants, and popular expectations that authorities should protect the food supply create an explosive situation. In the spring of 1775 come the subsistence riots known as the Flour War. [C-024]

The reform becomes politically toxic in the midst of a crisis of bread. Grain has gone up in price. People see that, and no further than that—and it is hard to blame them when they are hungry. Whose fault is it? The minister who has abolished controls on the grain trade. Riots begin; Turgot has to repress them by force. Governments are rarely judged by the elegance of their theory when people cannot afford food.

Turgot's immediate fiscal rule is famous: “No bankruptcy, no increase of taxation, no borrowing.” [C-025] The way out, he says, is economy and reform. That means attacking expenditures, privileges, offices, pensions—many people with influence, in other words. And it means confronting opposition from the parlements, privileged orders, court politics, and those threatened by his edicts.

The king can ask the obvious question: if not bankruptcy, not new taxes, and not more borrowing, then what exactly are we to do? Turgot’s answer is: cut. Cut ministerial budgets, cut pensions, cut offices, cut the golden incomes of the people who have always lived well from the state. It is economically coherent. Politically, it manufactures enemies with astonishing speed.

There is a famous line: “There are only Monsieur Turgot and I who love the people.” [Q-011]

Turgot is dismissed in May 1776 after roughly twenty-one months. It is not only pensions: the grain crisis, the corvée replacement tax, guild abolition, privileged resistance, and court politics have made his position impossible. [C-025]

His successor, Clugny de Nuits (kloo-NYEE duh NWEE), arrives confidently saying that he knows the proverb: a Controller-General never dies in office. It means that finance ministers do not last; they are thrown out too quickly. “You will see,” says Clugny, “I shall disprove the saying.”

He offers a different remedy: the royal lottery. Not the invention of lotteries—those already exist—but the creation or reorganization of a Loterie royale as a revenue device. People will buy tickets, the state will take the proceeds, and somebody will win. [C-026]

It has the beauty of simplicity. Sell tickets in enormous numbers, pay a small part back in prizes, and keep the rest. A state discovering the lottery can feel that it has discovered El Dorado. Yet even at the time there are people who find it shameful: the state is making money from its subjects’ taste for gambling. The lottery brings in revenue, but it does not make the hole in the budget disappear.

There is a contemporary anecdote. During Clugny's final illness, someone reminds him of the proverb that a Controller-General never dies in office. “Oh! Well,” he replies cheerfully, “I shall prove the proverb wrong.” After his death, satirists provide an epitaph: here lies a controller who would gladly have died after extinguishing the state's debts. [Q-012]

The lottery does not suffice; the hole grows wider. Clugny does not have to answer for it, because five months after taking office he dies suddenly. The political satirists had their songs even then, and he had promised to die in office. Louis XVI is said to have concluded: I think we have made another mistake. The joke survives better than the finance policy. [C-026]

Then comes Jacques Necker (zhahk NEK-air). A Protestant banker, he cannot initially hold the formal title of Controller-General, but he becomes the dominant figure in finance. And France has just made a decision of enormous consequence: to support the American insurgents against Britain.

Necker is almost the opposite of Turgot. He is a Genevan banker, comfortable with credit, public intervention, and the practical business of raising money. Because he is Protestant, Catholic France cannot initially make him Controller-General; a nominal minister stands in front, but everyone knows who is directing the finances.

France gives covert aid first and enters the war openly in 1778. French military, naval, and financial help is important to the American victory, especially in the coalition campaign that ends at Yorktown. But the war is paid for largely by borrowing, and it greatly aggravates a fiscal system that was already fragile. [C-027]

It is a glorious revenge on Britain, and it is terribly expensive. France helps the insurgents with money, matériel, ships, soldiers, and eventually open war. Yorktown is a triumph. When the United States become independent, Louis XVI is the first French king in a very long time to have won a great war against the hereditary enemy. He has never been more popular. If anyone had said that nine years later his head would fall in a public square, nobody would have believed it. But the triumph is bought on credit. The monarchy wants the prestige of defeating Britain without asking its own country for a great new tax; so the bills are sent into the future.

Necker tries to finance the war without a new general tax. One instrument is the life annuity: you pay the state a capital sum; the state pays you an annual income for as long as a specified person lives. There are different loans and different terms, so we must not pretend every issue is identical. But the vulnerability is real.

For an investor it sounds attractive: give the government a capital sum now, and receive an income every year for a lifetime. The government receives immediate cash; the investor receives a handsome return; the war can be fought without a new tax that everyone will notice. For a while, everybody is pleased.

And Necker makes the offer more tempting still. Some of these loans are tied to a lottery. Imagine buying a government annuity: most subscribers receive the normal yearly income, while a few fortunate tickets receive a spectacular one. It is a mixture of public debt, investment income, and gambling. The public loves it. The Treasury receives cash at once. Necker seems to have financed a war almost without asking the country to feel it.

The owner of an annuity and the person whose life measures it need not be the same person. The subscriber can designate a third party—the *tête*, the measuring life—when the contract is issued. The Crown's terms did not sufficiently distinguish the age of that person. That is the flaw. [C-028]

Genevan bankers see the opportunity immediately. At subscription they choose exceptionally healthy young girls, often from good circumstances and inoculated against smallpox. They pool thirty or fifty nominated lives, so that the early death of one does not destroy the whole investment, and sell shares in the pool. [C-028]

If a government offers nearly the same annual return on the life of an older adult and on the life of a selected child, the child is a far more expensive promise for the government. The bankers had found the weak point in the pricing. It is not that an existing annuity could be sold and magically re-registered on a newborn; its measuring life was fixed. The opportunity lay in choosing the life at the outset, and in choosing it exceptionally well. By 1789 life-annuity interest alone was a grave fiscal burden. [C-028]

The bankers can then sell participations in the pool. No single family has to gamble everything on the life of one child; the pool makes the investment look safer, more divisible, more marketable. For the Crown, however, the obligation is now tied to lives chosen precisely because they are likely to last. It has borrowed today and committed itself to paying tomorrow for far longer than a careless calculation expected.

At first, naturally, the loans sell. Everybody is pleased; Necker looks like a magician; the American war has been financed without a new tax. But every now and then Necker goes to look at the numbers and feels ill: the public debt is rising, rising, rising.

Financial information is not simply locked in Necker's desk. In 1781 he publishes the famous *Compte rendu au roi* (kont ruhn-DYOO oh RWAH), an unprecedented public account which reports an ordinary surplus. The trouble is that it excludes the extraordinary, war-financed account. So it is disclosure, but partial disclosure; a reassuring picture that does not show the whole position. [C-029]

The book is a sensation. For the first time the public is invited to look at the finances of the monarchy. It appears to say that the state is in surplus. That is politically brilliant: Necker presents himself as the man who has restored order. But the war loans are in another account. The public has been shown a carefully lit room, not the whole house.

Necker asks to cut court expenditure and golden pensions. He understands something that is still worth remembering: cutting political costs has a symbolic value—everyone should share the sacrifice, excess should not be flaunted—but it cannot alone put a state budget back on its feet. At the time court expenditure, so visible and so fiercely blamed on Marie-Antoinette and her circle, is only a tiny part of the budget. Necker loses office in 1781. [C-029]

The last great figure is Calonne (kah-LON). His initial insight is brutal: a state that must borrow must appear creditworthy. If lenders think the monarchy is finished, they will not lend. So confidence must be restored.

Calonne borrows, supports public works and visible expenditure, and tries to make the monarchy look solvent enough to sustain its credit. It is too narrow to call the whole policy mere court luxury, though the purchase of Saint-Cloud (san-KLOO) for Marie-Antoinette becomes an obvious target for criticism. The strategy of confidence-building deepens the deficit before Calonne turns toward broader reform. [C-030]

This is not Keynes before Keynes. Calonne is not saying that public spending will create jobs and lift demand in a modern economy. His thought is more immediate: lenders must see a court that is alive, a government that pays, a kingdom that looks prosperous. If France appears poor and finished, nobody will lend it money. If it appears magnificent, perhaps the credit will continue a little longer.

Spend, spend, spend—not in the later Keynesian sense of creating employment, but because a glittering court and an active state may persuade financiers that this is a government worth lending to. The queen has long wanted the château of Saint-Cloud. Her husband says: my dear, with the situation as it is, we cannot afford it. Calonne replies: why not? Of course we can afford it. Buy Saint-Cloud. Six million livres. Parties, receptions, court balls, music, opera, theatre, concerts: spending, spending, spending. The court is happy, the bankers are happy, the market rises, and enthusiasm is general.

And then, by the late 1780s, the figures become unbearable. Debt service absorbs about half of the central financial burden; military expenditure about a quarter. Interest is swallowing the state. [C-031]

Think of what that means. Before the government has paid for roads, administration, justice, relief, or any reform at all, a huge share of what it raises is already promised to creditors. Another immense share goes to the army and navy. Education and relief receive almost nothing by comparison. A state can survive an occasional deficit. It cannot indefinitely survive when so much of its income has already left the room before any new decision is made.

Louis XVI dismisses Calonne. The country is now learning how desperate the position is, and it asks for something the absolute monarchy has not called for generations: the Estates-General.

What are the Estates-General? An old representative assembly, summoned by the king, made up of the three orders: clergy, nobility, and the Third Estate. In earlier centuries kings consulted it, particularly over extraordinary taxation. It embodied the idea that a ruler asking the country for money ought to explain why. [C-032]

The last Estates-General had met in 1614. Since then the monarchy had governed without calling it. By 1787 and 1788, bankruptcy, failed fiscal reform, opposition from notables and parlements, and a widening public political argument leave the Crown with no credible exit from the deadlock. Louis announces the Estates-General on 8 August 1788. They open on 5 May 1789. [C-033]

In the medieval kingdom, this would have been familiar. A king who needed an extraordinary tax assembled princes, nobles, bishops, clergy, and the prosperous people of the towns. He explained what he needed, why he needed it, and how much. They discussed it; they might approve it. That was what the three estates meant: clergy, nobility, and everyone else—the Third Estate, including the people who actually had money in the towns.

Absolute monarchy had made this old arrangement unnecessary. By the seventeenth century the Crown was strong enough to tax without convening the assembly, and after 1614 it simply stopped calling it. But an institution can be asleep without being forgotten. In 1788 people begin to say: look where a century and a half of government without consultation has brought us. The king must hear the country.

Notice the irony. The nobles insist most loudly on the assembly, imagining that they will put the monarchy under control. They do not imagine where the process will lead.

They think of an old political instrument, one in which their own rank and influence will count. They imagine that the king will be forced to listen to the leading people of the kingdom. They do not imagine that the Third Estate will ask a much more dangerous question: if the assembly speaks for the country, who exactly is the country?

Necker is recalled in desperation. He and the king open the Estates-General trying to discuss the hole in the budget. The deputies listen in astonishment: the deficit is much worse than they had thought. But then they say, in effect, we do not have the remedy for that—and in any case it is no longer the most important question. The more important question is that France cannot go on with absolute monarchy.

The opening concern is finance. But the deputies discover a problem larger than the deficit: who represents the nation, and how is France to be governed from now on?

At first it seems perfectly possible that they will argue about taxes, approve a loan, perhaps devise a reform, and then go home. But the procedure itself becomes explosive. Do the three orders sit and vote separately, so that clergy and nobility can always outvote the Third Estate? Or do the deputies vote by head, as representatives of one nation? A dispute that begins with accounts becomes a dispute about sovereignty.

First comes the dispute over verification and voting by order or by head. On 17 June the deputies of the Third Estate, joined by some clergy, declare themselves the National Assembly. On 20 June comes the Tennis Court Oath. On 23 June, after a royal session ordering separate deliberations, they refuse to disperse; the king yields on 27 June. [C-034]

Their position can be summarized this way: the nation is represented here; the Assembly has the right to state the national will; it will not leave “except by the force of bayonets.” [Q-013]

It is an extraordinary claim of representative sovereignty. And in the Paris crisis—troop movements, the dismissal of Necker, fear, hunger, popular mobilization—the Bastille is stormed on 14 July. The Assembly was already calling itself national before the Bastille; after July it becomes impossible to treat it as a temporary consultation on finance. [C-034]

The king continues for a time within the new order. But confidence in him is destroyed in stages. The royal family's failed flight to Varennes (vah-REN) in June 1791 is decisive. War, the insurrection of 10 August 1792, and the fall of the monarchy lead to imprisonment. Papers found in the iron safe in November strengthen the case that Louis had maintained secret contacts with foreign enemies. [C-035]

The movement becomes harder and more violent. A king who has accepted an assembly is no longer the unquestioned source of power; a king suspected of calling in foreign armies against his own country becomes something worse. Fear, war, betrayal, hunger, and political conflict now drive the Revolution into a different register.

The Terror does not spring automatically from that one discovery. It belongs to 1793 and 1794: foreign war, internal revolt, economic crisis, and bitter political conflict. But the Revolution is now asking who profited from the old order, who helped its enemies, and who is to pay. [C-035]

And so we return to the tax farmers with whom we began. The Ferme générale had been abolished in 1791. In May 1794, a summary political trial condemns twenty-eight former *fermiers généraux* (fairm-YAY zhay-nay-ROH) and associated defendants—including Lavoisier—on charges of conspiracy to aid France's enemies. They are guillotined on 8 May. [C-036]

In the revolutionary imagination it closes the circle. The old monarchy had squeezed the country, relied on private finance, borrowed, reformed and failed; now the men associated with that fiscal world are called to account in the most terrible way.

There would be much more to say. But I think the overall sense of the story is clear enough. Thank you. Good evening.
