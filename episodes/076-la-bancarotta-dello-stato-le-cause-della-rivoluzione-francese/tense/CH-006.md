## 6. Bank, company, and Mississippi bubble

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00472–U-00585 -->

The regent authorizes him. They proceed in small steps. First they create a private bank with the modest name of the General Bank. This bank begins issuing shares and paying a large return on them. People are happy with the large return and readily buy the bank's shares. Since the regent agrees with Law, he helps him: the tax authorities will accept the bank's shares in payment of taxes. At that point, who would not want them? The shares circulate, their price rises, and the bank keeps printing new ones.

The bank, in turn, accepts the old IOUs in payment—the old mint notes that have been circulating for so long, in other words the national debt. And so the national debt gradually falls, because the bank accepts the old bills. Apparently John Law truly is a magician. Everyone is delighted.

The next step is to invent the banknote. The bank begins issuing not shares, but what we too would recognize as banknotes: identical, standardized, printed notes. Think of our old lira banknotes, which said, “1,000 lire payable at sight to the bearer.” Today's euros no longer say anything of the kind. The euro is the euro; it is an abstract thing. But with the lire, at least in theory, the idea was still this: this 1,000-lira note is 1,000 lire. The bank will give you the 1,000 lire whenever you take it the note: payable at sight to the bearer.

Law's system is to print banknotes with all the signatures—just as euros still carry the signature of the bank official who guarantees them—with the promise written on them. Surviving notes read: “Banque générale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight fifty écus in specie of the weight and fineness current this day, value received at Paris on 10 June 1718. Banque royale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight one hundred livres tournois in silver specie, value received at Paris on 1 April 1719.”

What happens then? People have grown accustomed to the bank's shares paying well, so the public is also fairly willing to accept the banknotes. Especially because, deep down, everyone says: meanwhile I will keep my gold louis locked in the safe and avoid spending them. For now, let us spend these banknotes. And that is precisely what John Law wants, because his idea is eventually to outlaw gold and silver money so that only banknotes circulate.

Who guarantees that the bank will pay? The monarchy. The regent agrees with the plan and is convinced that Law truly is a magician, so the monarchy guarantees the bank's notes.

After a while they tell themselves: since the system is working so well, let us print notes. And they print notes. In theory the notes are guaranteed by the bank's capital, but eventually they begin to think it is a shame to limit themselves to that. Printing notes is wonderful. They circulate, and the national debt is falling. It is marvelous; it really is falling. They sweep up the old IOUs and put the new notes into circulation.

But honestly, they can't guarantee all those banknotes with the capital in the vault. So what do they do? Law has another brilliant idea.

We are at the beginning of colonial expansion. At the start of the eighteenth century France thinks it can become a great colonial power. It has Canada, Louisiana, and the Antilles. It expects immense expansion in America, and people expect America to produce phenomenal riches.

So the bank's notes are guaranteed by the promise that enormous riches will eventually arrive from America. Law creates a trading company authorized by the monarchy and twinned with the bank. The monarchy grants this trading company a monopoly over all trade with the colonies. Do you understand what that means? A monopoly on tobacco, a monopoly on sugar. It is colossal—El Dorado.

The public is pleased and continues to trust the system. The bank is now paired with this company. And while they are at it—John Law and the regent understand each other perfectly—the company also obtains the government's monopoly on tax collection. I told you that private entrepreneurs collect the taxes. Law gets his hands on that too.

And so the bank's shares circulate and its banknotes circulate. The same man is president of the bank, president of the company that holds the monopoly on all colonial trade, and president of the company that collects every tax in the kingdom. The share price rises. People are thrilled.

It is one of the first financial bubbles in history. It has gone down in history as the Mississippi Bubble, because the company meant to exploit the American colonies is known as the Mississippi Company. A great many people make a great deal of money. For the first time in the history of the French language, a new word is invented: *millionnaire*, millionaire. Suddenly people begin talking about the millionaires who have amassed millions of francs by speculating in shares.

The regent decides to go a little further. John Law still does not control absolutely every lever; the position of finance minister remains. So they will make him finance minister too. Here a small problem arises. This is the profoundly Catholic Kingdom of France. The regent may be a notorious atheist who holds orgies at Easter, but in the Catholic Kingdom of France a Scottish Protestant cannot become a minister.

John Law decides that Catholicism has always interested him greatly. He begins spending time with the Jesuits, is converted, and converts to Catholicism. At that point he can become finance minister as well.
