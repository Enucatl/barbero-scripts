## 11. Necker, the American war, and life annuities

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00856–U-00962 -->

Another economist arrives with the miraculous recipe. This time his name is Jacques Necker, a Swiss man from Geneva. He is the complete opposite of Turgot: taxes and state intervention in the economy, and that is how they will put things right.

There is still that little problem that Necker too is a Protestant, so they cannot make him minister. But they make him director-general of the ministry and install a figurehead as minister. In practice, Necker is in charge.

Necker begins applying his theory: work on duties, taxes, and state intervention. But a new problem arises. The American Revolution has broken out. You remember? 1776. The American Revolution breaks out in 1776, and England immediately finds itself in trouble.

Why does this matter to us? Because England and France are enemies. England won the Seven Years' War a few years earlier and took the colonies away from the French. The French see an opportunity for revenge, and they get it right.

This is something people often forget. The American Revolution—the first great moment, let us say, in the birth of a democracy—wins its war against England thanks to the help of the absolute French monarchy. France sends fleets and troops to support the Americans, and in the end the Americans will win their war.

When the Americans win and the United States becomes independent, it is a great victory for France too. It is 1784, and Louis XVI is the first king of France to win a great war against the perennial enemy, England. Louis XVI has never been so loved or so popular. If anyone had told him, “In only nine years they will cut off your head in a public square,” nobody would have believed it.

But naturally there is one small problem: this war has cost a fortune. The national debt has reached staggering levels. Monsieur Necker has to find a way to raise money, and he invents something.

They will sell treasury bonds, but instead of guaranteeing the payment of a certain sum with interest at maturity, these bonds guarantee a life annuity from the outset. You spend a certain amount and immediately begin receiving a small annual payment.

Necker is a serious man and he has done his research. He has studied life expectancy and calculated how long people live on average, and he sets the amount of the annuity on that basis.

Then, while he is at it, he has another idea. The lottery has remained in his mind. So they will do this: they will sell these treasury bonds—let us call them that, these life annuities—to the public at a fixed price, in ten-thousand-euro tranches. Most will guarantee an annuity of five hundred euros—I am modernizing the figures, naturally—five hundred euros a year for life. But they will also hold a lottery. Some of these treasury bonds will be drawn at random, and the lucky holders will receive an annuity of half a million euros a year.

So he combines bonds, treasury certificates, and the lottery. It is enormously successful. Everything is calibrated precisely according to the life-expectancy calculation.

But there is another problem. When someone buys one of these annuities, the income does not have to depend on the buyer's own life. The subscriber can nominate another person—the measuring life—and the payments continue for as long as that person remains alive. The annuity can later be resold, but that does not change the measuring life named at the beginning.

The Genevan bankers understand the opportunity perfectly. When they subscribe, they nominate carefully selected young girls from Geneva, many of them protected against smallpox by inoculation. Then they combine thirty or fifty of these lives in a pool and sell small shares in the resulting income. If one girl dies, the investor loses only one part of the return; the other annuities continue paying. The French Crown has not adjusted its rates sufficiently for the age of these exceptionally young, carefully selected lives, and so the loans become far more expensive than expected. [N-001]

As a result, within a few years this magnificent invention will ruin the treasury still further. But they do not notice immediately. The annuities are doing well, everyone buys them, their value rises, everyone is happy, and Monsieur Necker is a magician. They have even paid for the American war without having to raise taxes.

Every so often Necker looks at the figures and feels ill when he realizes how high the national debt is climbing. But nobody is told. This is an absolute monarchy. The newspapers publish only what the government wants. If a journalist talks too much, he ends up in the Bastille. So the public knows nothing. Only Monsieur Necker, alone in his office at the ministry, knows that the national debt is rising, rising, rising.

Eventually he too goes to the king and tells him: “Your Majesty, we have to reduce spending. We have to reduce court expenditure and the lavish pensions.”

But Necker knows something people do not always know today. Today it is sometimes said that there is no point fighting lavish pensions and the cost of politics because they are negligible compared with the national debt. That does not mean they should not be cut, in my opinion—not that you care what I think. In my view these things should be done, but they have symbolic value. They create a sense of community in which everyone shares the sacrifices and excesses are avoided. But cuts to the cost of politics certainly do not restore a budget by themselves.

In Necker's time, the court expenditure everyone sees—and that the French therefore criticize fiercely, making them hate Marie Antoinette and her courtiers—accounts for one percent of the budget. So there is not all that much to cut.

The fact remains that, when it becomes clear that Necker's miraculous recipe has ultimately failed, the annuities do not work, and the hole in the budget is more terrifying than ever, Necker too is dismissed.
