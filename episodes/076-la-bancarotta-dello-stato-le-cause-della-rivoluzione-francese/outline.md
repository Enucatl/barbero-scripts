# Structural outline

## Overview

Barbero isolates a financial cause of the French Revolution without denying social inequality,
bourgeois political ambition, hunger, or bad harvests. His thesis is that a century of war,
inefficient and inequitable taxation, private tax farming, borrowing, and failed reform left the
monarchy unable to service its debt. An absolute king could normally impose taxes without consent,
but fiscal collapse forced Louis XVI to summon the Estates-General; that concession reopened the
question of sovereignty and turned a budget crisis into revolution.

The narrative begins under Louis XIV with Saint-Simon's hostile view of absolutism and wartime
finance. It follows Desmarets's property tax, John Law's paper-money and Mississippi schemes, and
Louis XV's renewed borrowing. Under Louis XVI, Turgot, Clugny, Necker, and Calonne offer opposed
recipes—liberalization and cuts, a lottery, state intervention and life annuities, then conspicuous
spending to sustain credit. Each fails politically or financially. By 1786 debt interest consumes
most revenue, public trust collapses, and demands for the Estates-General transform fiscal
accountability into representative government. Modern analogies illuminate mechanisms but are not
part of the historical causal chain.

## 1. Scope and financial thesis

- **Range:** `U-00001`–`U-00057`
- **Original audio:** 04:13–07:12
- **Argument:** Among many causes of the Revolution, the lecture follows the monarchy's accumulated
  deficit and argues that it compelled Louis XVI to summon the Estates-General.
- **Steps/examples:** Conventional explanations (privilege, bourgeois exclusion, harvests, bread
  prices, hunger); century of spending and taxation; repeated reliance on economists.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Barbero calls the audience test subjects for a lecture outside his usual
  field and compares eighteenth-century reliance on experts to recent economic government.
- **Quotation targets:** None.
- **Claim targets:** `C-001`, fiscal collapse forced the convocation that initiated the Revolution
  (`U-00025`–`U-00044`); `C-002`, the deficit accumulated through a century of spending and defective
  taxation (`U-00027`–`U-00044`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — retain the explicit causal boundary and self-qualified framing.

## 2. Absolutism and Saint-Simon's opposition

- **Range:** `U-00058`–`U-00122`
- **Original audio:** 07:17–11:15
- **Argument:** French absolutism concentrated law, coercion, and taxation in the king, alienating
  older aristocratic powers; Saint-Simon supplies the lecture's critical eyewitness voice.
- **Steps/examples:** Lettres de cachet and the Bastille; contrast with medieval constraints; the
  nobles' opposition; War of the Spanish Succession and the defeat at Turin.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Turin's locally familiar siege is reframed as an event of European
  consequence.
- **Quotation targets:** `Q-001`, Louis XIV's “Lo Stato sono io” (`U-00074`–`U-00082`); `Q-002`,
  Saint-Simon's claim that France's ruin began with the catastrophe of Turin (`U-00103`–`U-00122`).
- **Claim targets:** `C-003`, the institutional powers attributed to French absolutism
  (`U-00058`–`U-00076`); `C-004`, Saint-Simon's position, memoirs, and aristocratic opposition
  (`U-00083`–`U-00104`); `C-005`, Turin's place in the French failure in the War of the Spanish
  Succession (`U-00105`–`U-00122`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — essential institutional and narrative setup; clarify that “absolute”
  describes Barbero's model and requires historical qualification.

## 3. Wartime insolvency, money, and tax farming

- **Steps/examples:** Unpaid troops and debts; metallic currency and billets de monnaie; discounting
  royal paper; multiplying taxes; creation and operation of the Ferme générale.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Electronic money, a silver coin valued in euros, and modern public
  bureaucracy explain unfamiliar institutions.
- **Quotation targets:** `Q-003`, wording on the royal mint notes promising future payment
  (`U-00155`–`U-00166`); `Q-004`, Saint-Simon's description of the king drawing blood and pus from
  subjects while enriching tax contractors (`U-00176`–`U-00185`).
- **Claim targets:** `C-006`, the crown's arrears and use of discounted mint notes around 1710
  (`U-00123`–`U-00176`); `C-007`, private tax farming and the Ferme générale diverted revenue and
  lacked effective control (`U-00186`–`U-00209`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — central explanation; `clarify` distinctions among notes, shares, and
  later banknotes.

## 4. Desmarets's property tax and absolutist decision-making

- **Range:** `U-00210`–`U-00364`
- **Original audio:** 16:36–26:05
- **Argument:** Desmarets adds a property tax through a formally consultative but substantively
  predetermined process; the measure invades private wealth, yields less than promised, and leaves
  financiers comparatively protected.
- **Steps/examples:** Earlier single-tax proposals; family-filled expert commission; Saint-Simon's
  objections to disclosure; Le Tellier and the Sorbonne reassure Louis XIV; staged Finance Council
  approval; disappointing proceeds.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Patrimoniale, flat tax, VAT, declarations of assets, experts and
  technocrats.
- **Quotation targets:** `Q-005`, the Sorbonne opinion that subjects' property belonged to the king
  (`U-00284`–`U-00310`); `Q-006`, Saint-Simon's account of the council, public grief, “commissione di
  antropofagi,” and failed yield (`U-00312`–`U-00364`).
- **Claim targets:** `C-008`, Desmarets proposed a new proportional property levy in the fiscal
  emergency (`U-00210`–`U-00239`); `C-009`, the commission and council procedure described by
  Saint-Simon (`U-00240`–`U-00357`); `C-010`, the tax underperformed and burdened most taxpayers while
  financiers escaped (`U-00358`–`U-00364`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — the episode dramatizes absolutist fiscal governance; mark Saint-Simon's
  polemical language as testimony.

## 5. Regency crisis and John Law's theory

  conviction, escape, writings, land-backed currency proposal, rejection in Britain, and reception

- **Digressions/analogies:** Comic characterization of bankers and the Regent; comparison with
  contemporary Italian debt.
- **Quotation targets:** `Q-007`, Law's argument against metallic money and for paper backed by land
  (`U-00405`–`U-00428`); `Q-008`, Saint-Simon's quip that Orléans disbelieved in God but believed in
  the devil (`U-00446`–`U-00459`).
- **Claim targets:** `C-011`, the Regency considered sovereign default amid a debt of roughly ten

  monetary theory (`U-00391`–`U-00434`); `C-013`, the characterization of Orléans and his adoption of
  Law's proposal (`U-00435`–`U-00472`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — retain biography and theory; compress sensational detail if needed.

## 7. Run, collapse, and the system's legacy

- **Range:** `U-00586`–`U-00668`
- **Original audio:** 40:00–45:17
- **Argument:** Coercion against metallic money destroys confidence, informed elites trigger a run,
  the bank collapses, and repudiation reduces royal debt at the cost of investors and long-term
  French distrust of paper currency.
- **Steps/examples:** Gold-possession limits and searches; repression of critics; Bourbon and Conti
  withdrawals; rue Quincampoix crowd deaths; repudiation, arrests, Law's escape; partial repayment
  favoring small holders.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Cash limits, electronic money, and insider trading.
- **Quotation targets:** None.
- **Claim targets:** `C-018`, Law restricted private holdings of specie and critics were punished
  (`U-00586`–`U-00611`); `C-019`, elite redemptions precipitated a run with seventeen deaths and
  suspension of payment (`U-00612`–`U-00636`); `C-020`, the settlement protected small holders,
  reduced public debt, and discredited paper money (`U-00637`–`U-00668`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — central cautionary outcome; modern analogies may be compressed.

## 12. Calonne's credit strategy reaches the wall

- **Range:** `U-00963`–`U-01017`
- **Original audio:** 63:37–66:47
- **Argument:** Calonne tries to sustain credit through conspicuous spending and concealment, but by
  1786 debt service consumes 51 percent of the budget and the strategy becomes untenable.
- **Steps/examples:** Public confidence theory; Saint-Cloud purchase and court display; 1786 accounts;
  debt interest, military, assistance and education shares; dismissal.
- **Digressions/analogies:** Contrast with Keynes and contemporary Italian debt-service costs;
  Barbero explicitly labels an imagined statement by Louis XVI.
- **Quotation targets:** None; the invented royal reaction is not a historical quotation target.
- **Claim targets:** `C-030`, Calonne promoted visible spending to maintain public credit
  (`U-00963`–`U-00995`); `C-031`, 1786 budget proportions were 51 percent interest, 26 percent
  military, and 2 percent assistance/education (`U-00996`–`U-01012`).
- **Adaptation:** `preserve` — core climax; omit the explicitly invented royal line or flag it as
  narration.
