# La bancarotta dello stato: le cause della Rivoluzione Francese — corrected English script

## 1. Scope and financial thesis

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00057 -->

Good evening, everyone. Tonight, how can I put this, you're the guinea pigs for a lecture I have worked on and perhaps ought to work on a little more. You will be the judges. The thing is, the subject—the causes, the financial causes of the French Revolution—is not one of the subjects I usually deal with. But it has always intrigued me, and here, as it were, I have seized the opportunity to try to understand a little more about it. I am not entirely sure I have understood everything. But one thing is certain: when you look at why the French Revolution broke out, you realize there were many reasons, of course.

There are books and books entitled *The Causes of the French Revolution*. They talk about the people's hatred of the privileges of the nobility and the clergy; about the wealthy business and industrial bourgeoisie emerging in France, who counted for nothing politically and wanted to count for more. Then you read that the poverty of the poor also mattered, along with bad harvests, the price of grain—and therefore bread—shooting through the roof, discontent, popular uprisings, and hunger.

But what we are going to talk about tonight is really something else. We will try to tell the story of the colossal hole in the French monarchy's budget, built up over a century of mad spending and insane taxation. In the end it forces the king to take the step we all know as the beginning of the French Revolution: summoning the Estates-General. I would swear we all remember that formula from school. What does it mean? Why is it so dramatic? We will try to explain that toward the end of this talk. But if King Louis XVI eventually has to summon the Estates-General, without knowing that within four years they will cut off his head, it is certainly because of the catastrophe in the French monarchy's finances.

So that is what we will try to examine. We will see how, faced with the disaster of this budget hole, the French monarchy repeatedly seeks a solution by putting its trust in economists with the right recipe—economists who know what must be done to turn the situation around. In short, the idea that the economy can be managed, and even revived, on the basis of economists' theories is certainly not a recent invention. Eighteenth-century France is full of precisely these attempts.

## 2. Absolutism and Saint-Simon's opposition

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00058–U-00122 -->

So we are in eighteenth-century France. This is an absolute monarchy. What does an absolute monarchy mean? It means that, at least in theory, the king can do whatever he wants. There is no formal step requiring him to ask anyone's approval. He can issue orders without having to justify them: by the king's will. With a note called a *lettre de cachet*, the king can have anyone he wants arrested, without explanation, and locked up in the Bastille for as long as he wants—even for life. The king can issue any laws he wants without being subject to any parliament, any minister, anything at all. He can impose taxes as he pleases.

It is not like that in the Middle Ages. We will not dwell on it, but monarchies have not always been this way. For centuries, kings have had powers within the kingdom that balanced their own—the great nobility. But from the time of Louis XIV, the Sun King at least, the monarchy in France is absolute.

You know the Sun King: “I am the State.” What does “I am the State” mean? Of course there are advisers, secretaries, and ministers. It's not that they don't exist. But they all take their orders from the king.

For the French monarchy at the beginning of the eighteenth century, the monarchy of the Sun King, we also have a fascinating account in the memoirs of a great nobleman of the period, the Duke of Saint-Simon. Saint-Simon is a nobleman who lives through the Sun King's mature years and old age, and then lives on well into the middle of the eighteenth century. He writes his memoirs, a vast, sprawling work—seven or eight volumes of a thousand pages apiece—full of gossip, anecdotes, and reflections on what it means to live under the absolute monarchy of the Sun King, Louis XIV.

And Saint-Simon represents those forces in the kingdom that are not at all happy about the monarchy being absolute. At the time of the Revolution it will be the bourgeoisie, the intellectuals, and the thinkers of the Enlightenment who protest. Here we are at the beginning of the eighteenth century, almost a century earlier. The protesters are the great nobles, princes, dukes like Saint-Simon, men accustomed to counting for a great deal in the kingdom. The idea that the king is now an absolute power irritates them above all.

Saint-Simon paints a terrible picture of the final years of the Sun King's reign, the years when France is engaged in a disastrous war against practically the whole world: the War of the Spanish Succession. The war is going very badly. Saint-Simon, writing many years later, identifies a precise moment when it begins to go badly: the catastrophe at Turin.

You remember the siege of Turin in 1706? We have all heard about it, haven't we? Pietro Micca; then Prince Eugene arrives and frees the city from the French siege. Here in Turin we all know the episode, and we might think it matters to us because we are Turinese, but that in itself it is a somewhat minor, provincial affair.

Saint-Simon, decades later, says that France's ruin begins with the catastrophe at Turin, when they threw us out of Italy. From that moment on, France no longer counts for anything in Italy, and that is where our ruin begins. We are in 1710.

## 3. Wartime insolvency, money, and tax farming

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00123–U-00209 -->

Four years have passed since the disaster at Turin. France is destitute. It has just emerged from the most terrible winter of the century. There is no money, the budget hole is terrifying, and the king is up to his neck in debt. So he does not pay. He does not pay his debts and he does not pay salaries. The army fighting at the front is not being paid.

To tell the truth, it's not that the king doesn't pay at all. He does not pay in coin. You have to remember that this is a period when paper money—banknotes—has not yet been invented. Until yesterday, paper money was a normal, modern form of money. Now we have discovered that it is an archaic, backward form and we are moving beyond it thanks to electronic money. But until yesterday, paper money was modernity itself.

In early eighteenth-century France, paper money has not yet been invented. Money is made of metal, preferably precious metal. The money that really counts is gold or silver coin, and it circulates and people accept it because they know gold and silver have a value that nobody can ever take away from them. Of course smaller change circulates too, because you cannot go to the market, buy six eggs, and pay with a silver crown worth fifty euros. So smaller coins circulate as well, and people accept those by convention—they have to accept them. But the serious money used for important business is gold or silver.

In France in 1710, that money has almost completely stopped circulating. Those who have it hold on to it tightly. And the king pays with promises. He essentially pays with bills of exchange, with IOUs. The king pays with notes printed at the mint, known as mint notes. One surviving note reads: “MINT NOTE RENEWED AND CERTIFIED pursuant to the Declaration of 24 May 1707. In the amount of [...] 1,000 livres tournois. FOR the sum of one thousand livres, which I will pay in one year to the bearer, value received in another mint note. Made at the Paris Mint on 15 June 1707.” The king puts them into circulation, and when the king gives them to you, you have to accept them.

But nobody wants these bills. Nobody believes that a year later they will really be able to go to the mint and have their gold coins counted out in exchange for this piece of paper. So what happens? People try to get rid of them. I have a mint note here for one thousand francs, but if you give me one hundred francs in gold, I am happy to hand the note over to you. So those who have real money keep it hidden, while the notes circulate and continually lose value. The only people who profit are the bankers, who can sweep up these bills and then, by playing the exchange rates and having connections in government, manage to enrich themselves while everyone else is destitute.

The king does not pay; on the other hand, he collects. Taxes almost double every year. Saint-Simon writes: “The capitation tax, doubled and tripled at the arbitrary will of the provincial intendants; goods and foodstuffs of every kind taxed at four times their value; taxes on the well-to-do and others of every nature and on all sorts of things—all this crushed nobles and commoners, lords and churchmen, without what reached the king being enough: he drew the blood of all his subjects without distinction, squeezed them even to the pus, and enriched an infinite army of tax contractors and employees in these various kinds of taxes, in whose hands the largest and best part remained.”

Because there's something else we have to explain. Today we are used to thinking that one of the problems of the modern state is that it has too much staff, too many public employees, too much bureaucracy. That problem does not exist in eighteenth-century France. There is very little bureaucracy. So the state does not collect taxes directly; it does not have the employees to do it. The collection of taxes, duties, and levies is contracted out to private entrepreneurs, to financiers. They advance the money and then take care of collecting the taxes, duties, levies, and tithes themselves.

There is a forest of completely different taxes; it is impossible to make sense of them, but the contractors know how. Once they have advanced the bare minimum to the king, they do the collecting themselves with an army of tax employees. But these are not public employees; they work for the private contractors. Eventually those contractors organize themselves into one great company, the *Ferme générale*, the General Farm: a consortium of bankers that collects taxes throughout the kingdom by its own methods and without any supervision. And so, Saint-Simon says, the king keeps raising and raising taxes, but in the end the money always remains in the contractors' pockets.

## 4. Desmarets's property tax and absolutist decision-making

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00210–U-00364 -->

At this point the finance minister, Desmarets, realizes that something has to be done. He decides to propose a reform. More than once during this lecture, you'll notice that we are talking about things that sound familiar to us.

The finance minister says: we need a wealth tax. We need a wealth tax because there are no real taxes on wealth. Most taxes are, you understand, duties on every kind of trade—practically VAT, right? Excise taxes. Whatever someone buys or sells, a share disappears in duties. Then there are taxes on wealth, but they are distributed rather casually. The municipality decides, all right, let us divide this among the inhabitants; we will make that man pay this much. Nobody has to file an income or asset declaration. There is no wealth tax truly proportional to a person's wealth. Minister Desmarets proposes introducing one.

The proposal has been made before, but then it came from well-intentioned financiers. They said: instead of this forest of taxes and duties, let us abolish everything and introduce a flat tax. They did not call it that, because at the time Europe spoke French, not English, but they said: let us have one single tax, on wealth. Let us introduce the wealth tax and abolish all the others. It was a well-intentioned proposal. Naturally, the tax contractors were not pleased. The entire world of finance and banking opposed it, and the proposal failed.

Now, in the disastrous situation during the final years of the Sun King's reign, Minister Desmarets proposes introducing the wealth tax again—but without abolishing all the others. He will add it to all the others.

Here we see how decision-making works in an absolute monarchy. As I said, the king can do whatever he wants. Naturally, the king does not understand everything. He has a series of associates and advisers. For finance there is a Council of Finance, chaired by the finance minister, who presents proposals to the king. The king decides, but the technical experts are there.

So Minister Desmarets, who has had this brilliant idea of introducing a wealth tax, knows first of all that he must have a commission study it. That way he can go to the king and say: I have a plan approved by the experts, by the technical specialists. The finance minister therefore appoints a commission of experts to examine the plan. He puts his brother on it, his son-in-law, and his two brothers-in-law. The commission approves the plan.

At this point the minister goes to the king and tells him: “I have here the tax-reform plan approved by the finest experts in the kingdom.”

The objection Saint-Simon makes at this point is interesting. In his memoirs, he tells us what happens behind the scenes. It's interesting because it helps us understand a mentality different from our own. They are used to paying endless taxes, to being squeezed, as he says, until not only blood but pus comes out. But until now nobody has been forced to disclose their wealth. At most, it has been estimated.

This new tax, however, requires investigations and requires everyone to declare their wealth. We have grown used to that as something normal. To Saint-Simon it seems nightmarish, inconceivable. He says: “But do you understand? This will reveal every family's secrets, the things carefully kept hidden in every family. It will provoke furious quarrels between husbands and wives, between brothers. It will expose private affairs. It will destroy credit, because who will still be able to obtain credit once everyone's true wealth is known?”

Saint-Simon says that even the king is frightened. Although the king is accustomed to the most enormous taxes, he cannot help being frightened by this plan. Even his servants notice that something is eating at him. The king is unusually worried. He finds it terrible to force his subjects to do such a thing.

In the end he is so tormented that he turns to his confessor, a Jesuit, Father Le Tellier. The king's confessor is an old fox. He tells him: “I will have the question examined.”

A few days later Father Le Tellier comes to King Louis with a written opinion from the theologians of the Sorbonne, the kingdom's highest theological authority. The opinion, as Saint-Simon reports it, says: “[Father Le Tellier] had returned with an opinion from the most learned doctors of the Sorbonne, which decided plainly that all his subjects’ goods were his own property and that, when he took them, he took only what belonged to him; [the king] admitted that this decision had greatly relieved him, removed all his scruples, and restored the calm and tranquillity he had lost. [...] The king, relieved by Father Le Tellier and his Sorbonne opinion, no longer doubted that all his subjects’ goods were his and that whatever he did not take from them and left them was a matter of pure grace.”

From that moment on, everyone notices that the king is serene and in a good mood again. Saint-Simon is appalled: “See what becomes of a king abandoned into the hands of such a confessor and speaking only to him, and what becomes of a state abandoned into such hands.” The king, reassured by Father Le Tellier and the Sorbonne's opinion, no longer doubts that all his subjects' property is his, and that whatever he does not take from them and leaves to them is an act of pure grace.

Here we truly reach the summit of the idea of absolute monarchy. The king can not only decide anything; everything belongs to him.

At this point, however, procedure still requires the Council of Finance to approve the plan. The Council of Finance is made up of ministers, princes, and councillors. It is chaired by the finance minister, but it meets in the king's presence. Once again: this is how absolute monarchy works.

Everyone at court knows that this plan is being prepared, but nobody has read it. They are all terrified. Then one fine day the Council of Finance meets. The minister arrives with the plan in his bag, but as he is taking it out, the king speaks first.

The king says: “You know we are living through a difficult moment. The problems are enormous. The minister has prepared a plan to find new resources, and I, the king, have read it. I have to say that this plan is excellent, and I am sure that when you have heard it, you will agree.”

At this point the minister speaks. He explains that it is all the war's fault, that the enemy refuses to make peace, that France is in danger of invasion. It is absolutely essential to resort to extreme measures. But if we introduce this new tax, everything will be fine. The king will be able to pay his debts, he will be able to pay salaries, money will begin circulating, and everyone will be richer.

After this little pep talk, the minister immediately begins reading the edict, which is very long. He reads the whole thing. When he finishes, nobody speaks.

Since nobody speaks, the king turns to the most senior councillor and asks for his opinion. The most senior councillor says: “Your Majesty, to give an opinion I would really need to read it myself, calmly. If I may take it home and study it. Frankly, I cannot give an opinion like this. I would ask the favor of being allowed to abstain.”

The king tells him: “Yes, of course, you are right. There is no point in asking for an opinion. The minister has already studied the plan. It could not possibly be improved. So I will simply ask whether you approve it.”

The council approves.

“Thus this bloody affair was concluded,” Saint-Simon writes, “and immediately afterward signed, sealed, registered amid stifled sobs, and published amid general weeping.” The proceeds do not come anywhere near what they imagine in that commission of cannibals. And the king does not pay a penny more than before. Everyone, meanwhile, is squeezed once again. Only the financiers escape, because it is impossible to know what they really have in their portfolios.

## 5. Regency crisis and John Law's theory

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00365–U-00471 -->

This is the situation when the Sun King dies in 1715. The heir is a child, the future Louis XV, the Sun King's great-grandson. The Sun King has lived so long that his son has died, then his grandson has died, and now the heir is his great-grandson. He is a child, so there will be a regency. For eight years, from 1715 to 1723, France is governed by a regent, the Duke of Orléans, the young king's uncle.

During the regency it becomes clear that the financial situation is catastrophic. Something absolutely has to be done. What do we do? One proposal is to declare bankruptcy. It can be done; other kings have done it in the past. The king declares bankruptcy simply by declaring that his debts are canceled. He will pay no one. The measure will cause disasters, impoverishment, and the ruin of many people, but it will save the kingdom.

While they are seriously discussing whether to declare bankruptcy, a gentleman turns up in Paris promising that he has the solution. He is a Scotsman named John Law—law, like the law. John Law is an economist from a banking family. Bankers are everywhere in this story. I will try not to speak badly of them, naturally, given the place where we are. You can judge for yourselves.

John Law is a banker, but a strange sort of man with a turbulent past. A few years earlier he killed a man in a duel over a woman. Having killed a man in a duel, and English law being rather severe, he was sentenced to death and spent a long time in prison waiting to be hanged. Then, because he had good lawyers, he managed to have the charge reduced to manslaughter. You can be released on bail for manslaughter. So John Law got out on bail, after which he thought it best to slip out of the United Kingdom. He sought refuge on the Continent, where he began publishing works on economics.

He published a treatise, and from certain points of view John Law is fascinatingly modern. Law says: our system of using money made of gold or silver is absurd. Gold and silver not only have no value in themselves, he says, but theirs is a value you cannot control. New gold mines are discovered and the value of gold falls. For some reason people then refuse to put much of it into circulation and hoard it, and the value of gold rises. It is all absurd. We ought to have paper money.

As I told you, these royal IOUs are already circulating in the Kingdom of France. But John Law argues that metal money should be abolished and only paper money introduced—something we essentially did only in the last century, right? Naturally, if you manufacture banknotes—and that is precisely John Law's theory—you need to create a central bank that produces and prints them.

And how do you guarantee that these banknotes have value? John Law says there is only one true form of wealth in the kingdom: land, landed property. You can see how, at the beginning of the eighteenth century, there are aspects of extraordinary modernity in their thinking, while at the same time they still live in a world that has reasoned differently for thousands of years. Land is wealth.

So Law says the king need only have all the land in the kingdom measured, and then we will issue banknotes in proportion to that land. Let us suppose that each banknote allows you to buy one hectare of land. Then people will know that the value of the notes is guaranteed, fixed, immutable.

From the Continent, where he has fled to avoid being arrested again for manslaughter after skipping out once he had posted bail, John Law sends this plan to London. The English government receives the proposal from this Scottish killer and fugitive and reacts, naturally, by saying: “This man is mad.”

John Law decides to try his luck elsewhere and arrives in Paris. As I was telling you, the regent in Paris is the Duke of Orléans. The Duke of Orléans is a very good man, likable and well-intentioned, but he has some major flaws. He is enormously lazy and does not want to work. He spends his time in pleasure and revelry because he is weak and cannot resist orgies, drunkenness, or women. But he is very well-intentioned.

Another little flaw is that, like many people by his time, in a world that is still officially and profoundly Catholic, the Duke of Orléans is an absolute atheist. He does not believe in God, and he is pleased that people know it because he thinks it makes him look intelligent. So he flaunts his atheism and irreverence. What does flaunting it mean? Well, he tries to schedule his most spectacular orgies for Good Friday, for example.

And yet, although he does not believe in God, one of his lifelong obsessions is that he would like to summon the devil. He has spent enormous sums paying frauds and magicians who promise to summon the devil for him. Saint-Simon writes: “The curiosity of M. le duc d’Orléans, joined to a false notion of firmness and courage, had from early on occupied him in seeking to see the devil and to make him speak. He neglected nothing, even the maddest reading, to persuade himself that there is no God, and he believed in the devil to the point of hoping to see him and converse with him. This contrast cannot be understood, and yet it is extremely common.” And so he is always surrounded by swindlers and frauds.

This is the man governing France when John Law arrives in Paris with his plan. They are made for each other. The financial situation is ruinous. At that moment the national debt equals ten years of revenue. To give you a comparison, in Italy today the national debt is roughly equal, I believe, to three years of state revenue. Here it is ten times annual revenue. But John Law has the solution.

## 6. Bank, company, and Mississippi bubble

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00472–U-00585 -->

The regent authorizes him. They proceed in small steps. First they create a private bank with the modest name of the General Bank. This bank begins issuing shares and paying a large return on them. People are happy with the large return and readily buy the bank's shares. Since the regent agrees with Law, he helps him: the tax authorities will accept the bank's shares in payment of taxes. At that point, who would not want them? The shares circulate, their price rises, and the bank keeps printing new ones.

The bank, in turn, accepts the old IOUs in payment—the old mint notes that have been circulating for so long, in other words the national debt. And so the national debt gradually falls, because the bank accepts the old bills. Apparently John Law truly is a magician. Everyone is delighted.

The next step is to invent the banknote. The bank begins issuing not shares, but what we too would recognize as banknotes: identical, standardized, printed notes. Think of our old lira banknotes, which said, “1,000 lire payable at sight to the bearer.” Today's euros no longer say anything of the kind. The euro is the euro; it is an abstract thing. But with the lire, at least in theory, the idea was still this: this 1,000-lira note is 1,000 lire. The bank will give you the 1,000 lire whenever you take it the note: payable at sight to the bearer.

Law's system is to print banknotes with all the signatures—just as euros still carry the signature of the bank official who guarantees them—with the promise written on them. Surviving notes read: “Banque générale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight fifty écus in specie of the weight and fineness current this day, value received at Paris on 10 June 1718. Banque royale: The Bank promises to pay the bearer at sight one hundred livres tournois in silver specie, value received at Paris on 1 April 1719.”

What happens then? People have grown accustomed to the bank's shares paying well, so the public is also fairly willing to accept the banknotes. Especially because, deep down, everyone says: meanwhile I will keep my gold louis locked in the safe and avoid spending them. For now, let us spend these banknotes. And that is precisely what John Law wants, because his idea is eventually to outlaw gold and silver money so that only banknotes circulate.

Who guarantees that the bank will pay? The monarchy. The regent agrees with the plan and is convinced that Law truly is a magician, so the monarchy guarantees the bank's notes.

After a while they tell themselves: since the system is working so well, let us print notes. And they print notes. In theory the notes are guaranteed by the bank's capital, but eventually they begin to think it is a shame to limit themselves to that. Printing notes is wonderful. They circulate, and the national debt is falling. It is marvelous; it really is falling. They sweep up the old IOUs and put the new notes into circulation.

But honestly, they can't guarantee all those banknotes with the capital in the vault. So what do they do? Law has another brilliant idea.

We are at the beginning of colonial expansion. At the start of the eighteenth century France thinks it can become a great colonial power. It has Canada, Louisiana, and the Antilles. It expects immense expansion in America, and people expect America to produce phenomenal riches.

So the bank's notes are guaranteed by the promise that enormous riches will eventually arrive from America. Law creates a trading company authorized by the monarchy and twinned with the bank. The monarchy grants this trading company a monopoly over all trade with the colonies. Do you understand what that means? A monopoly on tobacco, a monopoly on sugar. It is colossal—El Dorado.

The public is pleased and continues to trust the system. The bank is now paired with this company. And while they are at it—John Law and the regent understand each other perfectly—the company also obtains the government's monopoly on tax collection. I told you that private entrepreneurs collect the taxes. Law gets his hands on that too.

And so the bank's shares circulate and its banknotes circulate. The same man is president of the bank, president of the company that holds the monopoly on all colonial trade, and president of the company that collects every tax in the kingdom. The share price rises. People are thrilled.

It is one of the first financial bubbles in history. It has gone down in history as the Mississippi Bubble, because the company meant to exploit the American colonies is known as the Mississippi Company. A great many people make a great deal of money. For the first time in the history of the French language, a new word is invented: *millionnaire*, millionaire. Suddenly people begin talking about the millionaires who have amassed millions of francs by speculating in shares.

The regent decides to go a little further. John Law still does not control absolutely every lever; the position of finance minister remains. So they will make him finance minister too. Here a small problem arises. This is the profoundly Catholic Kingdom of France. The regent may be a notorious atheist who holds orgies at Easter, but in the Catholic Kingdom of France a Scottish Protestant cannot become a minister.

John Law decides that Catholicism has always interested him greatly. He begins spending time with the Jesuits, is converted, and converts to Catholicism. At that point he can become finance minister as well.

## 7. Run, collapse, and the system's legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00586–U-00668 -->

Now that he controls everything, Law decides to put his plan into effect. Little by little, gold and silver will be outlawed and only banknotes will remain. They have to begin by applying pressure.

There is an astonishing analogy with our present problem of reducing the use of cash and encouraging the move to electronic money, which in theory is one day supposed to become the only form of money. So obstacles are placed in the way of using cash. The minister—still Law—prohibits people from keeping more than five hundred francs in gold at home. Today it is forbidden to spend more than three thousand euros in cash, right? He says it is forbidden to keep it at home.

Naturally, anonymous denunciations begin, and the police start searching people's homes for illegal gold. At this point people begin to worry, because the banknotes say, “1,000 francs payable at sight to the bearer.” In plain French, that means I can go to the bank tomorrow and they will give me one thousand francs in gold. But if I begin to suspect that the government has something against gold and is threatening to outlaw it, then I might become worried.

The public becomes worried. Some people complain. Journalists and pamphleteers begin publishing complaints against the minister. This is an absolute monarchy: anyone who complains publicly is arrested and deported to the colonies. After that, public opinion officially stops protesting, but the anxiety continues to spread.

Finally, after doing the sums, a major speculator who holds an enormous quantity of banknotes decides to go to the bank and have them paid out in gold. The first to do so are, curiously enough, several great nobles and princes of the blood—the Duke of Bourbon, the Prince of Conti—people with, let's say, the right instincts and the right information. We did not invent insider trading.

These people have the right information. Then one day, people see the Duke of Bourbon, the king's cousin, personally arrive at the bank with a train of lackeys carrying suitcases and exchange all his banknotes for gold coins.

At that point, naturally, there is panic. The crowd rushes to the bank's offices in the rue Quincampoix in Paris, appropriately a street directly behind the stock exchange, even today, in the business district. The crowd surges forward. Seventeen people are crushed to death at the bank's doors. The bank pays for a while, then closes. The regent has no alternative but to declare the banknotes no longer legal tender. We are sorry, but we will pay no one.

The treasurer and the bank director whose signatures appear on the notes naturally go straight to the Bastille. John Law escapes in time. He too has received a tip. He leaves the kingdom and goes to Venice, where nobody can lay hands on him and where he will die years later.

Now there are complaints everywhere and a great many ruined speculators. I have to say that the regent handles the matter decently. For example, he decrees something that, if it could be done today, would astonish everyone: with the little that remains, they will obviously manage to repay something to the defrauded savers, and they begin with the small ones. Most small savers manage to recover what they spent. Many of the large ones are ruined.

But meanwhile the national debt has indeed evaporated. On the whole, the monarchy's finances are sounder than they were before. There is only one small problem. Paper money is a good idea, but for two centuries nobody in France will speak of paper money again. After the French public's experience with Law's System, it will be absolutely impossible even to mention the idea of circulating banknotes.

You will say: we are a very long way from the French Revolution. When are we going to get there? We are indeed still in 1720. But in reality, the Mississippi Bubble and its collapse, with all this general chaos, ultimately leave the monarchy feeling that things are not going so badly after all. As I said, the national debt has almost disappeared. They can begin borrowing again. They can begin running up debt again. The regency ends.

## 8. Louis XV restores the debt cycle

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00669–U-00718 -->

The boy king has become a young man. He will be King Louis XV and will reign for about fifty years. He will go down in history as Louis the Beloved. In reality, historians generally agree that he is an idiot and that his reign is disastrous.

Among other things, France fights and loses the most important war of the century, the Seven Years' War. This is the colonial war in which the French lose Canada and are driven out of India, making it clear that England is the great power of the future. But France hardly notices. It continues to delude itself that it is the great power—a habit the French have never entirely lost, naturally.

And after all, France is still the capital of the world from an intellectual point of view. This is the age of the Enlightenment—think of Voltaire. French philosophers dominate every capital in Europe. France feels strong, rich, prosperous, the cradle of civilization. King Louis XV loves his pleasures and his many expensive mistresses, Madame de Pompadour in particular. Louis XV spends and spends, and his ministers borrow. For almost fifty years they pretend nothing is wrong.

As you see, we have moved ahead quickly. In 1774 Louis XV, the Beloved, dies. His grandson Louis XVI comes to the throne. Nobody can imagine that less than twenty years later he will leave his head on the guillotine. He is the king of an absolute monarchy that continues to consider itself the most powerful country in the world.

Louis XVI is a thoroughly good man. Every report we have from his childhood teachers says he's a complete nonentity. But he is well-intentioned. He comes to the throne and wants to understand how things work. The thinkers of the Enlightenment have been talking for a long time about reforms and about an *Ancien Régime* that has to be improved. He listens to them: let us make these reforms.

Then he looks at the state of the finances. After fifty years, the finances are catastrophic once again. The national debt is terrifying.

At this point, the script for the remaining part of our lecture is as follows: let us find an economist with the recipe for putting everything right. One after another, the economists arrive, each with his own recipe. One after another, amid general enthusiasm, they become finance minister. And within a very short time they are driven out amid insults because the recipe has failed. But the interesting thing is that all their recipes are different. We will see the whole range of possible options.

## 9. Turgot, liberal reform, grain crisis, and cuts

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00719–U-00816 -->

The first man whom Louis XVI, full of good intentions, makes finance minister is an economist named Turgot. Louis XVI certainly has dead weights of his own: this frivolous wife, Marie Antoinette, who spends and spends. But Louis XVI truly wants to go down in history as the father of his people, as the man who genuinely tried to make the kingdom more prosperous. So he chooses an economist as finance minister.

Turgot is greatly loved by the thinkers of the Enlightenment and by the fashionable society of the Paris salons. Today people sometimes talk about the Roman salons where decisions are made, right? At the time, it is the Paris salons. Turgot is a man of absolute integrity, devoted to the public good. Everyone is certain of it and knows it; there is not the slightest doubt.

This is very interesting because Turgot's ministry shows us something history often demonstrates, although it is not pleasant to be reminded of it: the fact that someone who is a thoroughly good person, a man of integrity with the best intentions, comes to power is absolutely no guarantee that things will improve. Quite the opposite.

Turgot becomes minister amid the enthusiasm of a public that says—here, I will read you a passage from the letter of a great lady, from the Paris salons: “Oh! This time, be assured that good will be done, and that it will be done well, because enlightenment will guide virtue and love of the public good. Never, no never, have two men more virtuous, more disinterested, more active been brought together and more powerfully animated by a greater and higher interest. You will see: their ministry will leave a profound mark on the minds of men. [...] Oh! What bad times for rogues and courtiers!” These are the hopes with which Turgot is welcomed into the ministry.

Turgot is an ultraliberal. The state must intervene as little as possible: reduce taxes, above all abolish duties, and let goods circulate freely. Fundamentally, he too has an idea similar to John Law's: the true source of wealth is land. But listen to the reasoning that follows from it.

The true source of wealth is land. So we have to tax wealth, which means taxing the products of the land. The land produces all existing wealth. Everything comes out of the land, whether agricultural products, metals, or minerals. Everything we have comes from the land. We will tax it there, and then tax nothing else.

So the people who trade and transform those products—the industrialists who use metals to manufacture things, and then the merchants who carry them abroad—will pay no tax on any of it. The industrialists and merchants are delighted.

There is, however, one group that is less delighted: the members of the *Ferme générale*. Remember? It is the private company that collects all the kingdom's taxes and grows richer the more taxes there are, naturally, and the more varied they are—the more there is to feed on. The *fermiers généraux* firmly oppose Turgot.

Turgot presses ahead anyway. Let us abolish all taxes on the grain trade, the most important trade in a world of poor people where most people eat bread. Let us abolish all taxes on the grain trade.

Unfortunately, economics is not an exact science, and every so often it produces ironies. That very year the grain harvest is disastrous. The price of grain shoots through the roof and the poor starve. And whose fault is it? Minister Turgot's, because he wanted to abolish all taxes and controls on the grain trade. The result? Grain has become more expensive. People see nothing beyond that, and it is difficult to blame them when they are starving.

So riots begin, uprisings over grain. Minister Turgot is forced to suppress them by force. His popularity begins to fall.

The king tells him: “What we have done so far is clearly not enough. What is the next step? Bankruptcy?”

“No, no, no.” Turgot is absolutely opposed. “No bankruptcy. It is dishonest.”

“Then do we raise taxes?”

“Never.” Turgot is a dogmatic liberal. Taxes are inherently bad; they will not raise them.

“Then do we go even deeper into debt?”

No, that is no good either. The national debt is a bad thing and must be reduced.

At this point the king says: “But then, excuse me, what exactly are we going to do?”

And here, alas, there are not many solutions left. Turgot says: reduce spending. Cut ministry budgets. Cut the lavish pensions.

They try. It is no surprise that he makes still more enemies. All the most important and influential people in the kingdom receive lavish salaries and lavish pensions. Turgot's popularity rapidly collapses.

The king is indignant: “What? Is no one willing to make a small sacrifice?” Louis XVI's exact words: “Only Monsieur Turgot and I love the people.”

After that, the opposition is so great that Turgot is dismissed. He has spent less than two years in the ministry.

## 10. Clugny's lottery and brief tenure

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00817–U-00855 -->

His successor, Clugny de Nuits, arrives very confidently. A contemporary account records the anecdote and the satirical epitaph that follows: “One day, when Monsieur de Vaines had gone to see him, he told him, to reassure him about his condition, that he should remember the well-known maxim that a controller-general never dies in office. 'Oh! Well,' he replied cheerfully, 'I shall prove the proverb wrong.' [...] They did not fail to compose an epitaph for him [...]: Here lies a controller worthy of mourning, / Loving France greatly and life not at all, / Gladly consenting that it be taken from him / Once he had extinguished the state's debts.”

They need to find money. Clugny de Nuits has a brilliant idea. Here too, they invent the lottery. The lottery does not exist yet. They invent it now. It is El Dorado: people pay. You put lottery tickets on sale and people buy them. Then you give a small part of the proceeds back as prizes. Fantastic.

So here we are with the idea of the lottery. This is eighteenth-century France, profoundly corrupt and rotten, and yet many people criticize the innovation. They say: “It is shameful for the state to enrich itself by exploiting people's desire to gamble like this.” That is more than you often hear said about it today.

Beyond that—beyond that—Clugny de Nuits has no other ideas besides the lottery. It turns out the lottery is not enough. The hole in the budget keeps growing. Clugny de Nuits finds himself in trouble, but he will not have to answer to anyone, because five months after entering the ministry he suddenly dies.

The malicious—no, not the malicious, the songs: political satirical songs and *chansonniers* already exist at the time. The songs go around, and he had promised that he would die in the ministry. And indeed, speaking of the choice he had made in appointing Clugny de Nuits, Louis XVI says: “I think we have made another mistake.” Bring on the next one.

## 11. Necker, the American war, and life annuities

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00856–U-00962 -->

Another economist arrives with the miraculous recipe. This time his name is Jacques Necker, a Swiss man from Geneva. He is the complete opposite of Turgot: taxes and state intervention in the economy, and that is how they will put things right.

There is still that little problem that Necker too is a Protestant, so they cannot make him minister. But they make him director-general of the ministry and install a figurehead as minister. In practice, Necker is in charge.

Necker begins applying his theory: work on duties, taxes, and state intervention. But a new problem arises. The American Revolution has broken out. You remember? 1776. The American Revolution breaks out in 1776, and England immediately finds itself in trouble.

Why does this matter to us? Because England and France are enemies. England won the Seven Years' War a few years earlier and took the colonies away from the French. The French see an opportunity for revenge, and they get it right.

This is something people often forget. The American Revolution—the first great moment, let us say, in the birth of a democracy—wins its war against England thanks to the help of the absolute French monarchy. France sends fleets and troops to support the Americans, and in the end the Americans will win their war.

When the Americans win and the United States becomes independent, it is a great victory for France too. It is 1784, and Louis XVI is the first king of France to win a great war against the perennial enemy, England. Louis XVI has never been so loved or so popular. If anyone had told him, “In only nine years they will cut off your head in a public square,” nobody would have believed it.

But naturally there is one small problem: this war has cost a fortune. The national debt has reached staggering levels. Monsieur Necker has to find a way to raise money, and he invents something.

They will sell treasury bonds, but instead of guaranteeing the payment of a certain sum with interest at maturity, these bonds guarantee a life annuity from the outset. You spend a certain amount and immediately begin receiving a small annual payment.

Necker is a serious man and he has done his research. He has studied life expectancy and calculated how long people live on average, and he sets the amount of the annuity on that basis.

Then, while he is at it, he has another idea. The lottery has remained in his mind. So they will do this: they will sell these treasury bonds—let us call them that, these life annuities—to the public at a fixed price, in ten-thousand-euro tranches. Most will guarantee an annuity of five hundred euros—I am modernizing the figures, naturally—five hundred euros a year for life. But they will also hold a lottery. Some of these treasury bonds will be drawn at random, and the lucky holders will receive an annuity of half a million euros a year.

So he combines bonds, treasury certificates, and the lottery. It is enormously successful. Everything is calibrated precisely according to the life-expectancy calculation.

But there is another problem. When someone buys one of these annuities, the income does not have to depend on the buyer's own life. The subscriber can nominate another person—the measuring life—and the payments continue for as long as that person remains alive. The annuity can later be resold, but that does not change the measuring life named at the beginning.

The Genevan bankers understand the opportunity perfectly. When they subscribe, they nominate carefully selected young girls from Geneva, many of them protected against smallpox by inoculation. Then they combine thirty or fifty of these lives in a pool and sell small shares in the resulting income. If one girl dies, the investor loses only one part of the return; the other annuities continue paying. The French Crown has not adjusted its rates sufficiently for the age of these exceptionally young, carefully selected lives, and so the loans become far more expensive than expected. [N-001]

As a result, within a few years this magnificent invention will ruin the treasury still further. But they do not notice immediately. The annuities are doing well, everyone buys them, their value rises, everyone is happy, and Monsieur Necker is a magician. They have even paid for the American war without having to raise taxes.

Every so often Necker looks at the figures and feels ill when he realizes how high the national debt is climbing. But nobody is told. This is an absolute monarchy. The newspapers publish only what the government wants. If a journalist talks too much, he ends up in the Bastille. So the public knows nothing. Only Monsieur Necker, alone in his office at the ministry, knows that the national debt is rising, rising, rising.

Eventually he too goes to the king and tells him: “Your Majesty, we have to reduce spending. We have to reduce court expenditure and the lavish pensions.”

But Necker knows something people do not always know today. Today it is sometimes said that there is no point fighting lavish pensions and the cost of politics because they are negligible compared with the national debt. That does not mean they should not be cut, in my opinion—not that you care what I think. In my view these things should be done, but they have symbolic value. They create a sense of community in which everyone shares the sacrifices and excesses are avoided. But cuts to the cost of politics certainly do not restore a budget by themselves.

In Necker's time, the court expenditure everyone sees—and that the French therefore criticize fiercely, making them hate Marie Antoinette and her courtiers—accounts for one percent of the budget. So there is not all that much to cut.

Still, when it becomes clear that Necker's miraculous recipe has ultimately failed, the annuities do not work, and the hole in the budget is more terrifying than ever, Necker too is dismissed.

## 12. Calonne's credit strategy reaches the wall

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00963–U-01017 -->

The last one arrives. His name is Calonne. Calonne has another theory: the national debt is indispensable. It is absurd to think of reducing it; we would strangle ourselves. We have to keep increasing the national debt. We have to keep borrowing money.

How do we get people to lend us money? The public no longer trusts us very much, so we have to make the public trust us. No one must know that there is a terrifying hole in the budget. And to keep them from knowing, we have to show them that we are rich instead. Spend.

Calonne's prescription is public spending in every field—not, as Keynes will later say in another context, to create employment. No, no: to create credit. The monarchy must appear fabulously rich so that everyone will lend to it without worrying. Heaven forbid anyone discover the true situation.

So there is public spending everywhere, and naturally the king and queen are pleased. For a long time the queen has wanted to buy herself a new château, the Château de Saint-Cloud, while her husband the king has been telling her: “But my dear, be patient. In the present situation we cannot afford it.”

Minister Calonne says: “Why not? Of course we can afford it.” They buy the Château de Saint-Cloud: six million francs. Parties, receptions, court balls, music, opera, theater, concerts—spending, spending, spending.

Why? Because this way the court is happy, the bankers are happy, the stock exchange rises, and the enthusiasm is universal.

That takes us to the summer of 1786. In the summer of 1786, Calonne does the accounts. Interest on the national debt—interest, not repayment of the principal—consumes fifty-one percent of the budget. For comparison, in Italy today interest on the debt is running at roughly eight percent of the state budget. Here it is fifty-one percent.

Naturally, a budget of that period looks very different from ours. The next item, at twenty-six percent, is military spending: the army, the fleet, and fortresses. The eighteenth-century French monarchy spends two percent of its budget on welfare and education. But however you look at it, fifty-one percent on interest payments is inconceivable.

Louis XVI—and I am inventing this; it is not in the record—must surely have said: “We have made another mistake.” What is certain is that he dismisses Calonne. And now? Now the country, which is discovering the truth, demands one thing. That is where we will conclude today.

## 13. The Estates-General and the transfer of sovereignty

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01018–U-01089 -->

The country demands that the king summon the Estates-General. What does summoning the Estates-General mean?

The Estates-General is an ancient institution. The French kings of the Middle Ages summon it regularly, because in medieval France nobody pays a penny in taxes merely because the king announces, “I have decided to raise taxes.”

To impose taxes, a medieval French king has to convene an assembly attended by princes, barons, and nobles; by bishops, archbishops, and the clergy; and also by the bourgeois merchants of the towns, the people who have money. These people gathered together—the nobility, the clergy, and the merchants—are also called the three estates.

The king and his ministers have to explain matters to everyone gathered there. “We need money.” They have to say how much money they need and why. The assembly discusses it and might then approve it. Without the Estates-General, a medieval French king cannot impose a penny in taxes.

Clearly, the kings find this control rather restrictive. We began by saying that the France of Louis XIV is an absolute monarchy. Seventeenth-century France becomes an absolute monarchy because the king is powerful enough to impose taxes without having to summon the Estates-General.

They summon it for the last time in 1614. After that, for a century and a half, the kings of France govern without the assembly. But people remember that it once existed in the past. The nobles remember above all, because they count for a great deal in the Estates-General. They are the First Estate, while the clergy is the Second Estate. Everyone else is the Third Estate, which counts for little. But it has to be summoned because the merchants are the people who had the money.

And in France in 1788, people begin to say: “The Estates-General—how long has it been since we summoned them? And look where we have ended up. The king cannot go on like this. He must listen to the country.”

Those who insist most strongly are the nobles, without knowing that in three or four years they will climb the steps of the guillotine along with the king. “We want the Estates-General. We want to bring the monarchy under control.”

The king is forced to promise that he will summon the Estates-General, and he summons it. In desperation, he has also recalled Necker to the ministry.

The king and Necker open the Estates-General by trying to talk about the hole in the budget. The Estates-General listens in astonishment as it learns that the hole is far more terrifying than anyone thought. But then its members say: “We do not have the remedy. But that is no longer even the most important problem.”

The most important problem is that the absolute monarchy cannot continue. In the future, this country has to be governed differently. The king has to agree to proceed with the constant advice of the Estates-General. It must be an assembly representing the country, and the king can no longer continue governing alone.

You know the result. The king hesitates for a while, then tries to dissolve the Estates-General. The Estates-General refuses: “We are the country. We are the people. We hold the power. Without us, the king is nothing.”

The king tries to have the army dissolve it by force. Paris rises. On July 14, 1789, the Bastille is stormed. From that moment, the Estates-General becomes the National Assembly, the parliament, and rapidly establishes itself as the only true legal power in France.

## 14. Revolution, Terror, and the tax farmers' reckoning

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-01090–U-01116 -->

For a while the king lives with this arrangement, coexists with it. But deep down he cannot bear the humiliation. He begins plotting with foreign powers to have them invade France. The plot is discovered. The king and queen are arrested.

The situation is obviously extremely tense. People are frightened. Heads begin to fall, and the Terror phase of the French Revolution begins.

To conclude, one very last thing. Among all these beheadings, there are constant trials of everyone considered guilty of plotting with the king to overthrow the Estates-General, plotting to restore the absolute monarchy—or of having profited from it.

The mood is this: now all the profiteers have to answer for what they have done, because if there is not a penny left, it is because they have all been feeding at the trough. Now the people will judge them.

Then one day a major trial opens against all the *fermiers généraux*: all the financiers still alive who managed tax collection under the monarchy. They are tried together, and on May 8, 1794, twenty-eight financiers, former *fermiers généraux*, go to the guillotine together.

There are many other things to tell, but I think that by now the overall meaning of this story has escaped no one. Thank you. Thank you all, have a good evening—and until next year, then.
