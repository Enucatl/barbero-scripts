# Come scoppiano le guerre: La guerra delle Falkland — accuracy-corrected English translation

## 1. The islands, their names, and 150 years of dispute

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00119 -->

Good evening. This is episode three of *How Wars Break Out*: the Falklands War. Over the last few days, several people have asked me: why follow the two world wars with the Falklands War? First, of course, because even though Pope Francis is rightly warning us, the Third World War hasn’t broken out yet, so we can’t tell that story yet. The Falklands War is the only war between two major Western countries, capitalist countries, allies of the United States; it’s the only war between two major Western countries that has been fought from 1945 to today. And that seems pretty significant to me. It’s also the only war between two major Western countries that I remember in my own lifetime. It made a powerful impression on me, which is another reason I chose it.

And then there’s another reason as well. The Falklands War—but I’m talking as if we all remembered it; many of us do—the war between Britain and Argentina, 1982. Well, thirty-two years have passed. British law opens government records after thirty years. They get “declassified,” as people rather horribly put it. The important thing is that we now have the documents. Of course, the truly secret, truly ugly files may still have disappeared. Two years ago, the British government released a mountain of confidential and secret records—or at least documents stamped “top secret.” Now they’re online for anyone to find. The Margaret Thatcher Foundation runs a fantastic site where you can find all of it, if you read English: transcripts of telephone calls with Reagan, notes, comments on telegrams, minutes of Cabinet meetings. Any historian would love this stuff: the minutes of confidential ministerial meetings, not necessarily Obama’s or Putin’s—maybe even Renzi’s today. Imagine knowing what they say when the doors are closed. Perhaps we’ll know in thirty years. Anyway, we know what they said to one another in 1982. That may be the main reason I wanted to see how this war broke out. I call it the Falklands War because you have to choose one name or the other. That’s a little awkward, because the name itself already puts you slightly on one side. You know, the British call them the Falklands, the Argentines call them the Malvinas, and deciding to call them one thing or the other already means taking a position. Well, officially they were called the Falklands at the time and they’re called the Falklands today. Still, I won’t pretend otherwise: in this war, I think the British had a point. And so I don’t mind too much calling it the Falklands War.

To understand how the war broke out, the first thing you need to know is what the Falklands are—which is already more than most British people knew at the time. If the British journalists who wrote at the time are right, the great majority of the British people thought the Falklands were islands somewhere off Scotland. The Falklands are a sizable archipelago, covering an area about half the size of Sicily. They sit far out in the South Atlantic, down toward Antarctica. The nearest country is Argentina. The islands lie five hundred kilometers off the Argentine coast. Their people are of English and Scottish descent, speak English, and hold British citizenship. By 1982, that has been true for almost exactly 150 years. In 1833 Her Britannic Majesty’s fleet took possession of the Falklands. So the 150th anniversary was due in 1983. Big anniversaries get people talking and thinking. In Britain, many people still imagine some little islands off Scotland, like the Orkneys or Shetlands. In Argentina, the approaching anniversary—150 years of British occupation of the Malvinas—gets people talking. Forgive me if I occasionally slip into the usual Italian shorthand for Britain.

The other thing you need to know is that the Malvinas hold an almost mythical place in Argentine popular culture. For 150 years, schoolchildren in Argentina have been taught that the wicked British imperialist colonizers occupied the Malvinas and one day will have to spit them back out, because, as the slogan puts it, “The Malvinas are Argentine.” Everyone in Argentina knows this; everyone has heard it since childhood, like Trent and Trieste in Italy at the time of the First World War. There’s one small difference: the inhabitants of Trent and Trieste actually were Italian, whereas the Falkland Islanders are British. But the argument here is about territory. So the decision to do something before this blessed 150th anniversary arrives—

This is the Argentina of the generals, a military dictatorship. You don’t need me to tell you this becomes one of the bloodiest dictatorships of the twentieth century: the dictatorship of the “dirty war,” as they called it, the *guerra sucia* against the communists, the terrorists, the left in general, the dictatorship of the *desaparecidos*. The exact number is still debated, but the junta makes tens of thousands disappear.

By December 1981, the junta is at a crossroads. Argentina is in crisis, and the military dictatorship’s popularity is at rock bottom. The economy is a disaster: inflation is at 600 percent, poverty is widespread, and the unions protest in the streets every day even though the police break up every demonstration. The economic crisis comes with a political one. The crimes against the *desaparecidos* are getting harder to hide. The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo are demanding answers—the women later known as the Grandmothers, those who are still alive. Every Thursday they gather outside the Casa Rosada, the presidential palace in Buenos Aires, and ask: where are our children? The police break up the demonstration, and the next Thursday the women come back. Argentina is boiling over. The junta is tottering and has no idea what to do next. By the end of 1981, the junta effectively stages a coup against itself. The military pushes out General Viola and replaces him with another general who has secured its backing. His name is Leopoldo Galtieri. General Galtieri takes power in Argentina on December 22, 1981, proclaims himself president of Argentina for life, with absolute powers and the right to choose his successor.

So who is General Galtieri? Here is the short version. Like a great many Argentines, he is the son of extremely poor Italian migrants. You know that Argentina is half an Italian country; half of Argentines have at least one Italian ancestor. Galtieri is a career officer of Italian descent. How does he rise through the ranks? One episode proves crucial to his career. In 1949, as a young lieutenant, he is chosen for a course at a very particular school. In Panama there’s a school run by the United States Army for officers from South American armies: the School of the Americas. American instructors train young Chilean, Brazilian, Argentine, Peruvian, and Colombian officers there—apparently less in conventional warfare than in repression and counterinsurgency. Many future South American dictators and their collaborators pass through the school. Second Lieutenant Leopoldo Galtieri attends too. Now for a piece of gossip—and I do think it’s gossip. When Galtieri died about ten years ago, the British newspaper *The Economist* published a venomous obituary claiming, among other things, that Galtieri was apparently the only officer ever to fail the Panamanian school. But again, I think that is gossip. It may not be true. 

Gossip or not, Galtieri keeps rising. When the junta takes power, he is one of its leaders and one of its most visible figures abroad. Ten months before he takes power in December 1981, Galtieri visits the United States. President Ronald Reagan’s administration welcomes him with open arms, especially because Argentina is pursuing a fiercely anti-communist policy and crushing the left-wing opposition at home. Reagan openly calls the generals’ regime a bulwark against communism. If you heard the previous episode, you may be feeling the same déjà vu I am. Remember Lord Halifax, the British foreign secretary, who goes to Berlin in 1937 and tells Hitler, of course, Germany, a bulwark against communism... Well, history sometimes repeats itself in its own way. General Galtieri is welcomed enthusiastically in Washington. American officials gush about him. One Reagan adviser—accounts differ on who—calls him a magnificent general. 

In December of that same year, 1981, the “magnificent general” takes power in Argentina with the support of the admirals and generals he has made a deal with. We’ll do something that fixes everything, they decide. We’ll occupy the Falklands—or rather, the Malvinas. We’ll take back the Malvinas. His deal with the admirals and generals includes a plan to take the Malvinas by force within months. As in the previous episodes, the plan rests on two gambles. The first gamble is that when the Argentine people hear that the Malvinas have been occupied—occupied, liberated—they’ll be wild with enthusiasm, mad with patriotic fervor, and will forget inflation, the *desaparecidos*, and so on. And that first gamble pays off exactly as expected. The second gamble is that the world will accept the fait accompli. The British will never have the courage to react and President Reagan is our friend and will therefore make sure that there are no serious problems.

## 3. Failed negotiations and Britain's blind spot

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00233–U-00365 -->

At first, of course, the Argentine military keeps the decision secret. And no one suspects much because for several years the Falklands-Malvinas dispute has seemed to be moving, slowly, toward some kind of solution. For about fifteen years, Britain and Argentina have held occasional talks about the islands’ future. The talks produce progress and practical agreements. Britain has never put sovereignty on the table, but many people sense that even that may one day be negotiable. Both sides accept forms of joint management, and Argentina makes substantial concessions too. The atmosphere is cordial enough that Argentina guarantees the islands’ air and sea links. Without them, the Falklands would be completely isolated: Argentina is five hundred kilometers away, but everything else is thousands of kilometers away.

The latest round takes place in New York in February 1982. The UN has pushed Britain and Argentina to negotiate. And in diplomacy, the final press release tells you how things went. This one says: “The meeting took place in a cordial and positive spirit.” That is the end of February. Then, on March 2, the Argentine government issues its own statement: Argentina has negotiated with Britain over sovereignty with patience, loyalty, and good faith for more than fifteen years. Enough. No more scheduling meetings one at a time, the Argentines say. They want what we would now call a road map. Nobody uses that term yet in 1982. We want a firm deadline and monthly meetings leading to the transfer of sovereignty to Argentina. Britain must set a deadline for transferring sovereignty. The communiqué ends with a warning: if Britain refuses, Argentina will end the talks and choose whatever course best serves its interests.  Diplomatic language is always delicate, is it not? But read between the lines.

The communiqué comes out March 2. The next day, the Argentine press goes wild. The Buenos Aires papers predict a break in diplomatic relations with Britain. We might also cut communications to the Falklands. How will they manage without us, when our planes bring them medicines and supplies? Then the press goes beyond anything the government has said. The government only demanded a deadline. The newspapers go much further: Britain has three or four months to recognize Argentine sovereignty and set a date for returning the islands. Otherwise, the papers say what the government has not: we may take the islands back ourselves. The Americans would understand.

That morning, the British ambassador in Buenos Aires gathers the papers, copies out their headlines and stories, and cables the prime minister in London. On her copy of this telegram, the prime minister in London notes, “We must make contingency plans.” The date is March 3, 1982. The news hits London like a bolt from the blue. No one expected anything. This is also a story of diplomatic and intelligence failures. The British Foreign Office has seen none of this coming. A British journalist explained it this way. The Foreign Office, Britain’s foreign ministry, he says, is packed with Arabists who are always ready to sort out the Middle East. But almost no one there knows South America or pays much attention to it. The one thing it never imagines is an Argentine crisis over the Falklands. The intelligence services keep reassuring the prime minister throughout March. Argentines, they say—you know what they are like. Half Italian. Plenty of talk, but they will never act. We have the reports now. This is documented. Her Majesty’s Intelligence Service, 007, is convinced that the Argentines are bluffing.

The prime minister is uneasy anyway. The British prime minister, of course, is Margaret Thatcher. She is the first woman I have discussed in this lecture series, the first whose management of a crisis we can examine. It would be too easy to ask whether she handles it differently from a man—never mind. Just remember this: for the first time in British history, perhaps since Elizabeth I in the sixteenth century, a woman will decide whether to go to war.

But be careful. Thatcher died last year, after a career that put her firmly in the history books. She changed the world through her reforms: liberalization, the fight against the unions, the destruction of union power, the end of nationalized industries, privatization. Thatcherism left a global mark. Everyone can decide for themselves whether Thatcher changed the world for better or worse. Either way, she is enormously important. In the spring of 1982, though, she is not yet the Thatcher of history. She has not won any of those battles. Britain is in an economic crisis. She has not defeated the unions or privatized industry. She seems to have achieved nothing. And like the British government in 1914, she is also burdened by Britain’s great open wound: Ireland.

When the Falklands crisis erupts, only a few months have passed since an event many of us remember: the hunger strike by IRA prisoners in Northern Ireland. Remember Bobby Sands? They demand better conditions and recognition as political prisoners, then starve themselves to death one after another. Ten die while asking for political-prisoner status, and the British government—Margaret Thatcher—says no. A crime is a crime is a crime; politics has nothing to do with it. Signed, Margaret Thatcher. So Thatcher is extremely weak. She has few accomplishments, and everyone expects her to lose the next election. Now, on top of everything else, reports arrive about the Buenos Aires papers raging over the Falklands. The intelligence services tell her not to worry. Mrs. Falkland—Mrs. Falkland, goodbye. Thatcher waits. During March the Argentines begin maneuvering in the area to raise the tension.

## 4. South Georgia and the escalation of March 1982

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00366–U-00457 -->

I have to tell you one more thing. I kept thinking, should I include it? No, leave it out. But no—it belongs here, because it’s the only thing that happens in March. Near the Falkland Islands there’s another archipelago that’s even more wretched, even colder, where no one lives, only penguins. It’s called South Georgia, and it belongs to the British Crown. The only people there are a platoon of Marines at a kind of weather station. When I say that the South Georgia islands are near the Falklands, you’ll laugh at me: they’re two thousand kilometers from the Falklands. But they’re still the nearest patch of dry land. There’s nothing else around. Go home and look at the map: cosmic emptiness. So South Georgia and the Falklands belong to the same strategic area.

During March, strange things begin happening on these South Georgia islands. A civilian scrap-metal crew arrives first. Argentine naval personnel then reinforce it, including a detachment commanded by Alfredo Astiz. [N-003] The workers raise the Argentine flag. The Marines on guard don’t quite know what to do. Here is one detail to remind us what kind of regime we’re dealing with. The commander of this Argentine detachment that lands on South Georgia in March 1982 is Lieutenant Alfredo Astiz, who is now serving a life sentence in Argentina. He was one of the chief torturers at Buenos Aires’s main torture center, the Navy Mechanics School. In March 1982, Astiz takes a break from his work at the navy torture center to land on South Georgia. That completes the picture. He is serving life now, though the sentence is fairly recent. First the French sentenced him to life imprisonment, but in absentia, because among the many people Alfredo Astiz tortured and killed were two French nuns. Then, finally, three years ago, Argentina sentenced him to life as well.

Anyway, that ends the aside. Back in London, officials are asking: what do the Argentines want? The intelligence services say they’re concerned about the Falklands, but there’s no danger until they decide to land. Thatcher wants to offer talks and see how Argentina responds. She leaves most of it to the British foreign secretary. And here we need a quick aside. The British foreign secretary is Peter Carrington, Lord Carrington, whom you may remember from his later stint as NATO secretary general. He is loyal to Thatcher, a close friend, and one of the first people to recognize what a formidable figure she is.

There’s a famous story in Britain about a journalist interviewing Carrington. Thatcher looks weak, and everyone expects her to lose the next election, so the journalist presses him to name her Conservative successor. Carrington refuses. No, he says, she is staying. End of discussion. Finally, the exasperated journalist says: all right, suppose a bus runs Thatcher over tomorrow. And Lord Carrington answers, “Nor if a bus dared to run her over would I be prepared to stand.”

Anyway, Margaret trusts Carrington, and the Foreign Office keeps assuring her there should be no problem. The Foreign Office under Lord Carrington first starts worrying on March 24. That day Carrington sends the prime minister a note. We’re discussing talks with the Argentines, he says, but the atmosphere is poisonous. Their press keeps insulting and pressuring us. Can we negotiate under those conditions? We may be heading for a confrontation. What exactly does that mean? For now, at least, the prime minister has been warned.

Carrington starts worrying just in time. It’s March 24, and we now know that on March 26 Galtieri, president of Argentina for life, gives the go-ahead for the invasion of the Falklands. Operation Rosario. A plan to land in the Malvinas on April 2. No one knows; it’s top secret. Thatcher sees the tension rising. What do you do? You keep offering talks, yes—but eventually you also have to show that you’re serious. On March 29, Thatcher does what she can: she orders a nuclear submarine to leave Gibraltar for the Falklands immediately. The submarine will need two weeks to reach the Falklands, but somehow the news leaks to the press. Thatcher is delighted. In her memoirs she will say, “My instinct was that the time had come to show the Argentines that we meant business.” Sometimes you have to flex your muscles too.

## 5. Thatcher learns the invasion is imminent

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00458–U-00524 -->

Does this remind you of the previous two episodes? It certainly does me. Fortunately everything is on a much smaller scale, but Mrs. Thatcher is worried. The Foreign Office and the intelligence services keep reassuring her. Or rather, that is how she tells it later in her memoirs, so take it with a grain of salt. People do polish the story afterward. But in her memoirs Thatcher says: “However, we knew that they were unpredictable and unstable, and that a dictatorship might not behave in ways we would consider rational.” You can’t quite tell whether she means these dictators or these Latins—you know how it goes. Then she continues: “By now I was deeply uneasy. Yet still I do not think that any of us expected an immediate invasion of the Falklands themselves.”

Everything changes on the evening of March 31. British Defense Secretary John Nott asks Thatcher for an emergency meeting, and a handful of ministers gather. Nott rushes to Downing Street with a report from American intelligence, which has finally pieced together what is happening. The warning is unequivocal: The Argentine fleet is at sea, loaded with troops, and heading for the Falklands.

This March 31 meeting is one of the decisive moments. Nott lays out the situation: once the Argentines occupy the islands, Britain will never be able to retake them. Thatcher’s hair stands on end. What do you mean, we can’t do it? Out of the question. If they land, we have to take the islands back. Where are the commanders? Where is Sir Henry Leach, the First Sea Lord? He still hasn’t arrived. Eventually they find out why. He was in civilian clothes. He tried to take a shortcut through Parliament, and security detained him because he was in civilian clothes. Finally an MP recognized him—wait, that’s the admiral—and got him to the prime minister.

Thatcher gets straight to the point: if the Argentines land, can Britain mount an expedition and take the islands back? Leach runs the numbers. Of course we can, he tells her. I can send this carrier and that one, destroyers, frigates—I have everything I need. If you give the order, I’ll begin assembling the fleet. Who has the authority to give the order? Does Parliament have to vote? They check. No—the prime minister can do it. This isn’t war yet; it’s only assembling the fleet. That evening Thatcher tells Leach to start assembling it. Cabinet can decide later whether it actually sails. But this is March 31, and the landing is expected April 2. There’s only a day and a half—one day, two nights—to stop it.

How do you stop them? The nuclear submarine is already on its way. In the postwar world, there is only one option. What would you do? You call Big Brother. You call Ronald Reagan. Thatcher doesn’t phone him; she fires off an urgent telegram over the secure line. I’ll read you Thatcher’s telegram to Reagan on the night of March 31. “Dear Ron, You will know about the disturbing intelligence reports which we have received indicating that the Argentine Navy could be preparing to invade the Falkland Islands within the next 48 hours.” Among other things, she writes, “I am asking you to intervene with President Galtieri and to seek his immediate assurance that there will be no Argentine landing on the Falkland Islands and no hostilities against our troops.” Fondest regards, Margaret.

## 6. Reagan's call and the American choice

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00525–U-00621 -->

Now a new player enters: the United States, the White House. Reagan calls Galtieri, but Galtieri makes himself unavailable. We’ve seen this move before. Reagan keeps trying, and eventually even Galtieri can’t duck a call from the President of the United States.

We have a real-time source for the call: the diary of White House aide Jim Rentschler. He is the official assigned to tell Reagan what the Falklands are and what is happening before Reagan calls Galtieri. While the two presidents talk, Rentschler jots down notes. He already knows this diary may matter someday, so he writes carefully. It’s a good diary.

While Reagan is on the phone, Rentschler starts with a joke about a Bell System ad: “Reach out and touch someone,” and Rentschler writes, “The Bell System never had to deal with General Leopoldo Galtieri.” Then he explains that the president is on a long-distance call to Buenos Aires, trying to talk the Argentine strongman out of invading the Falklands. Never heard of them, right? Neither had Rentschler until the night before, when the British prime minister’s request arrived. And this is the man who has to brief Ronald Reagan.

Then comes his assessment of Galtieri. Remember, Washington welcomed this man with open arms a year earlier: a magnificent general. Now Rentschler is listening to him on the phone. This fellow Galtieri, who doesn’t wait for the interpreter, is “speaking first in broken mafioso-type English.” You see the association. Italian, Argentine—his English is shaky, and to these Americans he sounds like the Latin-accented mobster from a TV drama. This Galtieri, speaking in this broken English—“he sounds like a thug.” And Rentschler notes: the president is speaking with great eloquence, I admire him greatly, but Galtieri is a man “whose machismo,” he fears, “will propel him forward in whatever direction he is headed.” Machismo: by now they have decided, and they can’t lose face; they’ll go ahead.

Poor Reagan does what he can. One aide later recalls him asking why everyone is fighting over that little frozen patch of land way down there—an “ice-cold bunch of land.” But when Reagan has to choose a side, he commits. Remember Argentina’s calculation: the Americans are our close friends. They will stay out, and they will keep everyone else out too.

That evening Reagan debates the crisis with his advisers. Some tell him to let Argentina proceed. They are our friends, after all. You may remember Jeane Kirkpatrick, the US ambassador to the UN. Kirkpatrick is famous for arguing that all dictatorships are bad, but some can be reformed and others cannot. Left-wing regimes, in her view, are beyond recovery. Right-wing dictatorships can change, so the United States can work with them. Kirkpatrick would rather do nothing here. America should stay out and let the two sides handle it. A colorful detail: the next day Mrs. Kirkpatrick will be the guest of honor at a dinner at the Argentine embassy.

Secretary of State Alexander Haig takes the opposite view: we can’t let Argentina get away with this. Reagan sides with Haig and writes to “Dear Margaret.” Dear Margaret, he says, I have just spoken at length with General Galtieri about the Falklands. I told him how concerned I am about a possible invasion, warned him about starting military operations, offered my good offices, and so on. The general listened but promised nothing. He left Reagan with the clear impression that Argentina is already committed to armed conflict. And Reagan concludes his telegram to Thatcher: “While we have a policy of neutrality on the sovereignty issue, we will not be neutral on the issue of Argentine use of military force.” All the best, Ron. The telegram tells Thatcher two things: Argentina is not backing down, and Washington will not cover for it. If Argentina uses force, the United States will side with Britain.

## 7. Invasion, Cabinet, and national humiliation

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00622–U-00712 -->

Now Thatcher convenes the government on the morning of April 2. The Argentines have already landed in the Falklands; they don’t know it yet. The Falklands governor has sent a message, but the transmission vanishes and never reaches London. Word finally gets through when a British ship picks up a local ham-radio broadcast and relays it. The British government meets before that confirmation arrives, but everyone knows the crisis can no longer be avoided. What do they do? Thatcher tells her ministers she has already ordered the fleet to assemble. Now they have to decide whether to send it.

Cabinet debates that question at length. Meanwhile, it has plenty of other decisions to make. Here is one detail from Friday, April 2. The aircraft carrier *Invincible* will lead the fleet, and Prince Andrew, the Queen’s son, serves aboard her as an officer. What do they do about him? If the aircraft carrier sails for war, does Prince Andrew remain at his post or is it better to keep him at home? Downing Street and Buckingham Palace discuss it, and the Queen makes her view clear. As long as Prince Andrew is serving on the aircraft carrier *Invincible*, he will take part in the expedition. His grandfather, King George VI, fought at Jutland. A member of the royal family cannot be treated differently from any other officer.

Once that is settled, Thatcher sees no other major obstacle. That evening, the government decides to send the fleet. It will sail in two days, as soon as it’s ready. The Foreign Office is raising a great many objections. In Thatcher’s memoirs you can see her exasperation with the Foreign Office, which, she says, gave her advice that “summed up the flexibility of principle characteristic of that Department.” Notice the Thatcher style. “I was presented with the dangers of a backlash against the British expatriates in Argentina, problems about getting support in the UN Security Council, the lack of reliance we could place on the European Community or the United States, the risk of the Soviets becoming involved, the disadvantage of being looked at as a colonial power.” All valid points, but when you’re at war you can’t allow difficulties to dominate your thinking; you must go ahead with an iron will to overcome them. And anyway, what was the alternative? “That a common or garden dictator should rule over the Queen’s subjects and prevail by fraud and violence? Not while I was Prime Minister.” Remember, she writes this afterward. But it certainly captures her position.

On April 2, after the Argentines have landed, Reagan calls Galtieri again. Galtieri’s answer is simple: Britain has refused to return the Falklands for 149 years. Time has run out. Remember Thatcher’s position at home: she is unpopular, seen as weak, and widely expected to lose the next election. When the April 3 papers announce the occupation, Britain feels nationally humiliated. Another disaster. We have made fools of ourselves again. The newspapers say it’s Britain’s worst humiliation since Suez.

What does Suez mean here? Go back to 1956. The Egyptians nationalize the Suez Canal, which Britain relies on. The British send paratroopers to occupy the canal. The Americans tell the British, forget it, go home. And the British go home with their tails between their legs. In British national memory, this is the moment Britain discovers it is no longer a world power—and can make itself look ridiculous by pretending otherwise. On April 3, 1982, the British newspapers say we’re facing another Suez. That same day, Thatcher has to face Parliament. It’s a Saturday, and Parliament has not been recalled on a Saturday in decades. The House of Commons is packed.

You know what the House of Commons looks like? It’s small, with fewer seats than MPs. That is deliberate; they are rarely all there at once. When they do all show up, something important is happening. They pack the chamber, standing or sitting on the steps, listening to every word. And remember another feature of the British Parliament, especially if you are used to the Italian one: many MPs vote their own judgment rather than the party line. Cross-party majorities on major issues are perfectly normal. You have to persuade the MPs themselves, so a powerful speech really matters.

## 8. Parliament tests the Iron Lady

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00713–U-00864 -->

Thatcher makes one of the great speeches of her career, telling Parliament: “I am sure that the whole House will join me in condemning totally this unprovoked aggression by the Government of Argentina against British territory. It has not a shred of justification and not a scrap of legality.” Thatcher takes command of the room. She nearly loses it only once, when she tries to score a point against Labour by recalling one of its own failures in government. She begins, may I remind the honorable members opposite that when they were in government—and a Labour MP cuts in: we did not lose the Falklands. For a moment, the mood in the chamber turns against her. Then she wins it back, and Parliament ultimately votes to support the fleet’s departure. The decision is made: the fleet will sail, and Parliament is behind it.

In her memoirs, Thatcher admits she is terrified because this apparent majority is not really solid. Some MPs, like her, are ready to go all the way to war. Many others think the fleet will scare Argentina back to the negotiating table. Very few, she says, are prepared to fight. Others have the opposite doubt: she is putting on a strong show now, but will she have the nerve to follow through?

One speech from that debate becomes famous in Britain, so of course I have to tell you about it. The speaker is Enoch Powell, a famous and popular political maverick. He comes from the right but has left the Conservatives and now operates on his own. He is fiercely nationalist and anti-immigration, yet takes left-wing positions on other issues. A very strange figure. When Powell rises in the Commons on April 3, he stares straight at Thatcher and speaks, as she remembers it, in a sepulchral voice: “The Prime Minister, shortly after she came into office, received a soubriquet as the ‘Iron Lady’.” The Soviets coined the nickname soon after she took office, recognizing how tough she was. Powell explains that it arose in the context of relations with the Soviet Union and its allies, and that Thatcher seemed pleased and proud of it. Then he concludes: “In the next week or two this House, the nation and the right hon. Lady herself will learn of what metal she is made.”

That is the challenge Thatcher carries out of Parliament: we’ll see what you are made of. Thatcher knows she is in tune with the country. And the polls? The public strongly opposes handing sovereignty to Argentina. Support for risking British service members’ lives starts below fifty percent in mid-April, then climbs above fifty percent as the campaign goes on. [N-007] Thatcher has real public backing. The fleet will sail.

Now people start saying: this is a war, so declare one. A committee of Conservative MPs meets, and we have those wonderful records I mentioned: the minutes, even the handwritten original with doodles and underlining. One MP after another weighs in. Lord Onslow’s contribution is simple: sink their entire fleet. Some MPs are worried. One says the public is alarmed, and Britain must not look weak. At the moment the Foreign Office stinks; it stinks of appeasement. If you heard the previous episode, you remember appeasement: Chamberlain’s attempt in 1938 and 1939 to keep Hitler quiet through concessions. Appeasement, peace at any price. In the British political vocabulary it has become a dirty word. Now, in 1982, MPs hear the Foreign Office call for more talks and say: this is dangerous. It reeks of appeasement. Move forward.

Most MPs insist they are already at war. Carrington says no: there is no obligation to issue a declaration, and Britain is not formally at war. Others keep pressing him to declare one. Declaring war is difficult, he says. There are legal questions; we have to consult the lawyers. It is all in the minutes. What that really means is that the government sees a declaration as a needless complication. These are new times. In 1914, and even in 1939, governments still sent an ambassador with a formal declaration—perhaps one full of lies, but they sent it. Now the rules have changed. Governments may be more cynical, more pragmatic, perhaps a little more cunning. Who knows? Probably all of that at once. But there’s no reason to declare war, as Mrs. Thatcher officially reiterates two days later, on April 5.

Two days later, on April 5, a journalist asks Thatcher directly: are you really about to go to war with Argentina? Thatcher answers: “Our objective is to recover the Falkland islands. We have to do what is necessary.” And then: “I wouldn’t talk in terms of war. A declaration of war is something different.” She refuses to get lost in legal technicalities. Britain has to recover the islands for itself and for the people who live there. There are several ways to do that; let’s hope Britain has not chosen the worst. “When you stop a dictator there are always risks but there are great risks in not stopping a dictator. My generation learned that a long time ago.”

And here we have to stop and think. We all knew Thatcher, but now she is dead and she was very old. Thatcher lived through appeasement and Hitler and the Second World War. In 1982, those are not distant history to her. She lived through them and remembers them clearly. So 1939 shapes how she reads 1982. What does Britain do when a dictator challenges it? Refuse to stop him and relive the shame of Munich? No. It stops him.

The Foreign Office, as you know, disagrees. And on April 5 Carrington resigns. My ministry was supposed to manage this crisis, he says. We failed, and now we are at war. Honor requires me to go. Carrington is Thatcher’s close friend and loyal colleague, but politics is politics. Now listen to Thatcher’s response when an interviewer asks about Carrington’s resignation. She calls it a serious blow and says she hates to lose such a valuable minister. But what can she do? Thatcher explains: “He felt that the policy had failed and therefore it was a matter of honour that he should go.” She is deeply sorry. Then she adds: “It is rather a wonderful thing in politics to have people who feel strongly about honour and who resign.” And with that, Lord Carrington exits.

## 9. Defeat transforms Argentina and Thatcher

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00865–U-00965 -->

We are almost done. I’m not telling you the story of the war; I’m telling you only how Margaret decided to send the fleet. For the next month, American diplomats shuttle between Argentina and Britain, urging both sides to stop. Both sides hold their ground, and they end up at war. But in Argentina, well before the British landings and the fighting, morale collapses. The Argentine press keeps declaring, we’re ready, let them come. But panic is spreading through the country’s upper ranks.

We have a secret American intelligence report about a senior Argentine politician. He has served on the military junta, still supports it, and tells an American confidant: by now it’s do or die. In other words, if Argentina keeps the Falklands, fine. If not, Galtieri falls. We can’t start negotiating now; it would be worse. Galtieri would fall immediately. For him, if anything, it’s better to lose the war. The American observer notes that his Argentine source is sure Argentina will lose. Every politician in the country, he says, knows the invasion was a colossal mistake. Everyone knows the president and his advisers are incompetent.

Then comes a detail I found fascinating as an Italian. The Argentine politician tells the American he is forty-one and now knows he will never see a “serious Argentina” in his lifetime. The funny part is that the American does not understand the phrase, so he explains it in his report: a stable, democratic, prosperous country. But as an Italian, I know exactly what the Argentine means. Why can’t my country be serious? Will I ever see it become one? No, he says. Maybe my children will see a serious Argentina. I now know I never will.

The war ends, as you know, with the British winning decisively in a matter of weeks. The toll is 649 Argentine dead, 255 members of the British task force, and three Falkland Island civilians. [N-008] The war is never formally declared, so no peace treaty follows. It leaves no formal trace—but it leaves major consequences. On June 14 the Argentine flag is lowered over Port Stanley and the last Argentine troops surrender. Galtieri resigns on June 17, three days after the surrender; another general takes over, but defeat accelerates the junta’s collapse and Argentina returns to elected civilian government in December 1983. [N-009] Raúl Alfonsín becomes president, and the military dictatorship ends.

Galtieri goes to prison from 1986 to 1991, receives a pardon, and later faces prosecution again. In 2002 he has a heart attack and dies under house arrest—the way elderly political defendants sometimes do. When Galtieri dies in 2003, *The Economist* writes that venomous obituary I already quoted at the beginning. It concludes: “He might have been forgiven by at least some of his fellow Argentines for his ruthlessness, but not for his stupidity.”

So the Falklands War brings down Argentina’s military junta—and creates the Margaret Thatcher we remember. This is the turning point in her political career. Afterward, Thatcher is Britain’s strongest politician in years. She wins a landslide in 1983 and goes on to do everything we associate with her—for better or worse, depending on your view.

Remember Enoch Powell, who said the country would soon learn what metal Thatcher was made of? After the war, in another very British parliamentary moment, he rises and declares: “It shows that the substance under test consisted of ferrous matter of the highest quality, that it is of exceptional tensile strength, is highly resistant to wear and tear and to stress, and may be used with advantage for all national purposes.”

And Thatcher becomes, well, Thatcher. During the war, at the Scottish Conservative Party Conference on May 14, Thatcher says: “What really thrilled me, having spent so much of my lifetime in Parliament, and talking about things like inflation, Social Security benefits, housing problems, environmental problems and so on, is that when it really came to the test, what’s thrilled people wasn’t those things, what thrilled people was once again being able to serve a great cause, the cause of liberty.” [N-011] Perhaps it would have been more candid to say that what excited people was going to war again. But Thatcher finds a formulation that, rhetorically at least, serves the cause of liberty.

Like so much about Margaret Thatcher, that statement—and her handling of the Falklands crisis as a whole—may thrill you or send a chill down your spine. Maybe both. That is what it did to me. Looking back over these three episodes has taught me a great deal. It has fascinated and excited me, and it has also given me chills: this is how wars start; this is how politicians handle crises. I hope it has done the same for you. Thank you, and I’ll see you next year.
