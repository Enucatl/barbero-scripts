## 5. Thatcher learns the invasion is imminent

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00458–U-00524 -->

Does this remind you of the previous two episodes? It certainly does me. Fortunately everything is on a much smaller scale, but Mrs. Thatcher is worried. The Foreign Office and the intelligence services keep reassuring her. Or rather, that is how she tells it later in her memoirs, so take it with a grain of salt. People do polish the story afterward. But in her memoirs Thatcher says: “However, we knew that they were unpredictable and unstable, and that a dictatorship might not behave in ways we would consider rational.” You can’t quite tell whether she means these dictators or these Latins—you know how it goes. Then she continues: “By now I was deeply uneasy. Yet still I do not think that any of us expected an immediate invasion of the Falklands themselves.”

Everything changes on the evening of March 31. British Defense Secretary John Nott asks Thatcher for an emergency meeting, and a handful of ministers gather. Nott rushes to Downing Street with a report from American intelligence, which has finally pieced together what is happening. The warning is unequivocal: The Argentine fleet is at sea, loaded with troops, and heading for the Falklands.

This March 31 meeting is one of the decisive moments. Nott lays out the situation: once the Argentines occupy the islands, Britain will never be able to retake them. Thatcher’s hair stands on end. What do you mean, we can’t do it? Out of the question. If they land, we have to take the islands back. Where are the commanders? Where is Sir Henry Leach, the First Sea Lord? He still hasn’t arrived. Eventually they find out why. He was in civilian clothes. He tried to take a shortcut through Parliament, and security detained him because he was in civilian clothes. Finally an MP recognized him—wait, that’s the admiral—and got him to the prime minister.

Thatcher gets straight to the point: if the Argentines land, can Britain mount an expedition and take the islands back? Leach runs the numbers. Of course we can, he tells her. I can send this carrier and that one, destroyers, frigates—I have everything I need. If you give the order, I’ll begin assembling the fleet. Who has the authority to give the order? Does Parliament have to vote? They check. No—the prime minister can do it. This isn’t war yet; it’s only assembling the fleet. That evening Thatcher tells Leach to start assembling it. Cabinet can decide later whether it actually sails. But this is March 31, and the landing is expected April 2. There’s only a day and a half—one day, two nights—to stop it.

How do you stop them? The nuclear submarine is already on its way. In the postwar world, there is only one option. What would you do? You call Big Brother. You call Ronald Reagan. Thatcher doesn’t phone him; she fires off an urgent telegram over the secure line. I’ll read you Thatcher’s telegram to Reagan on the night of March 31. “Dear Ron, You will know about the disturbing intelligence reports which we have received indicating that the Argentine Navy could be preparing to invade the Falkland Islands within the next 48 hours.” Among other things, she writes, “I am asking you to intervene with President Galtieri and to seek his immediate assurance that there will be no Argentine landing on the Falkland Islands and no hostilities against our troops.” Fondest regards, Margaret.
