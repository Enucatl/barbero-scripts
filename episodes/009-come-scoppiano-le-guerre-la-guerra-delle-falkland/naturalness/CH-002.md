This is the Argentina of the generals, a military dictatorship. You don’t need me to tell you this becomes one of the bloodiest dictatorships of the twentieth century: the dictatorship of the “dirty war,” as they called it, the *guerra sucia* against the communists, the terrorists, the left in general, the dictatorship of the *desaparecidos*. The exact number is still debated, but the junta makes tens of thousands disappear.

By December 1981, the junta is at a crossroads. Argentina is in crisis, and the military dictatorship’s popularity is at rock bottom. The economy is a disaster: inflation is at 600 percent, poverty is widespread, and the unions protest in the streets every day even though the police break up every demonstration. The economic crisis comes with a political one. The crimes against the *desaparecidos* are getting harder to hide. The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo are demanding answers—the women later known as the Grandmothers, those who are still alive. Every Thursday they gather outside the Casa Rosada, the presidential palace in Buenos Aires, and ask: where are our children? The police break up the demonstration, and the next Thursday the women come back. Argentina is boiling over. The junta is tottering and has no idea what to do next. By the end of 1981, the junta effectively stages a coup against itself. The military pushes out General Viola and replaces him with another general who has secured its backing. His name is Leopoldo Galtieri. General Galtieri takes power in Argentina on December 22, 1981, proclaims himself president of Argentina for life, with absolute powers and the right to choose his successor.

So who is General Galtieri? Here is the short version. Like a great many Argentines, he is the son of extremely poor Italian migrants. You know that Argentina is half an Italian country; half of Argentines have at least one Italian ancestor. Galtieri is a career officer of Italian descent. How does he rise through the ranks? One episode proves crucial to his career. In 1949, as a young lieutenant, he is chosen for a course at a very particular school. In Panama there’s a school run by the United States Army for officers from South American armies: the School of the Americas. American instructors train young Chilean, Brazilian, Argentine, Peruvian, and Colombian officers there—apparently less in conventional warfare than in repression and counterinsurgency. Many future South American dictators and their collaborators pass through the school. Second Lieutenant Leopoldo Galtieri attends too. Now for a piece of gossip—and I do think it’s gossip. When Galtieri died about ten years ago, the British newspaper *The Economist* published a venomous obituary claiming, among other things, that Galtieri was apparently the only officer ever to fail the Panamanian school. But again, I think that is gossip. It may not be true. 

Gossip or not, Galtieri keeps rising. When the junta takes power, he is one of its leaders and one of its most visible figures abroad. Ten months before he takes power in December 1981, Galtieri visits the United States. President Ronald Reagan’s administration welcomes him with open arms, especially because Argentina is pursuing a fiercely anti-communist policy and crushing the left-wing opposition at home. Reagan openly calls the generals’ regime a bulwark against communism. If you heard the previous episode, you may be feeling the same déjà vu I am. Remember Lord Halifax, the British foreign secretary, who goes to Berlin in 1937 and tells Hitler, of course, Germany, a bulwark against communism... Well, history sometimes repeats itself in its own way. General Galtieri is welcomed enthusiastically in Washington. American officials gush about him. One Reagan adviser—accounts differ on who—calls him a magnificent general. 

In December of that same year, 1981, the “magnificent general” takes power in Argentina with the support of the admirals and generals he has made a deal with. We’ll do something that fixes everything, they decide. We’ll occupy the Falklands—or rather, the Malvinas. We’ll take back the Malvinas. His deal with the admirals and generals includes a plan to take the Malvinas by force within months. As in the previous episodes, the plan rests on two gambles. The first gamble is that when the Argentine people hear that the Malvinas have been occupied—occupied, liberated—they’ll be wild with enthusiasm, mad with patriotic fervor, and will forget inflation, the *desaparecidos*, and so on. And that first gamble pays off exactly as expected. The second gamble is that the world will accept the fait accompli. The British will never have the courage to react and President Reagan is our friend and will therefore make sure that there are no serious problems.
