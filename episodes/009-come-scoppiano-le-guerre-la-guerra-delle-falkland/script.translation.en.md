# Come scoppiano le guerre: La guerra delle Falkland — sourced English translation

## 1. The islands, their names, and 150 years of dispute

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00119 -->

Good evening. So, how wars break out, episode three. The Falklands War. Over the last few days more than one person has asked me, why the Falklands War after the two world wars? Why? First of all, naturally, because even though Pope Francis is rightly warning us, the Third World War has not broken out yet, so we cannot tell that story yet. The Falklands War is the only war between two major Western countries, capitalist countries, allies of the United States; it is the only war between two major Western countries that has been fought from 1945 to today. And all things considered, that does not seem like a small thing to me. It is also the only war between two major Western countries that I remember in my lifetime, and at the time it made quite an impression on me and fascinated me, so that will also be why I decided to tell its story. And then there is another reason as well. The Falklands War—but I am talking as if we all remembered it; many of us do—the war between England and Argentina, 1982. Well, thirty-two years have passed. Now, English law establishes that after thirty years documents are made public; they are, as people say horribly nowadays, declassified, downgraded. In short, the important thing is that the documents are there. Then, for all anyone knows, they probably make the truly secret and rather ugly ones disappear anyway. But in fact, two years ago the English government made an enormous quantity of confidential or secret documents available—at any rate, they have “top secret” written on them. And now they are all on the internet, all online, you can find them. There is a fantastic site—you need to have some command of English—run by the Margaret Thatcher Foundation, where you can find this mass of documents: transcripts of telephone calls with Reagan, notes, comments on telegrams, minutes of Cabinet meetings. An endless number of things, in other words, that one would rather like to have, all things considered: the minutes of confidential ministerial meetings, not necessarily Obama’s or Putin’s, perhaps even Renzi’s today, really, to know what they say to one another when they are completely in private. Perhaps we will know in thirty years. In any case, we know what they said to one another in 1982, and this is perhaps the main reason why I felt like going to see how that war broke out. I call it the Falklands War because one has to choose, in any event. It is a little sad, because even deciding to call it the Falklands War already means taking sides a little. You know, the English call them the Falklands, the Argentines call them the Malvinas, and deciding to call them one thing or the other already means taking a position. Well, officially they were called the Falklands at the time and they are called the Falklands today. But I do not deny, how shall I put it, that in my opinion—no, in my opinion, let us say—in regard to that war, I find that the English were not entirely wrong, all things considered. And so I do not mind too much calling it the Falklands War.

So, in order to understand, to try to understand and tell how that war broke out, the main thing, really, is to know what the Falklands are, which is more than the majority of English people knew at the time. If the English journalists who wrote at the time are right, the great majority of the British people thought the Falklands were islands somewhere off Scotland. The Falkland Islands are a fairly large archipelago; all of them together amount to half the size of Sicily. They are a remote archipelago in the South Atlantic, very far south toward Antarctica, and the nearest place is Argentina. The Falklands are five hundred kilometers from the Argentine coast and are inhabited by a population of English and Scottish origin, who speak English and have British citizenship. And this state of affairs has lasted—had lasted, now do not make me do the calculation—but at the time I am speaking about, for exactly 150 years. In 1833 Her Britannic Majesty’s fleet took possession of the Falklands. So the 150th anniversary was due in 1983. You know that 150th anniversaries are serious things that then create discussions, make people think, and so on. So in Argentina—not so much in England; you will forgive me, I will often say England instead of Great Britain, that is our Italian habit—not so much in England, where people thought they were little Scottish islands like the Orkneys and Shetlands, but in Argentina the approaching anniversary, 150 years of English occupation of the Malvinas, made people think and discuss.

Because the other thing you need to know in order to understand what I will tell you this evening is that in Argentina the Malvinas are a popular myth. For 150 years, schoolchildren in Argentina have been taught that the wicked English imperialist colonizers occupied the Malvinas and one day will have to spit them back out, because, as the slogan puts it, “The Malvinas are Argentine.” Everyone in Argentina knows this; everyone has heard it since childhood, like Trent and Trieste in Italy at the time of the First World War. Perhaps, if you like, with this small difference: the inhabitants of Trent and Trieste actually were Italian, whereas in the Falklands they are English. But here it is a question of territory. So the decision to do something before this blessed 150th anniversary arrives—

## 2. Galtieri, the dictatorship, and the invasion gamble

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00120–U-00232 -->

is taken by the people who are in command in Argentina at that moment. The decision was made, as we know today, in December 1981. Who are the people in command in Argentina then? It takes us no time to remember. That is the Argentina of the generals. It is the dictatorship of the generals. You do not need me to tell you that it was one of the bloodiest dictatorships of the twentieth century: the dictatorship of the “dirty war,” as they called it, the *guerra sucia* against the communists, the terrorists, the left in general, the dictatorship of the *desaparecidos*. Even today people debate how many there were, but in any case tens of thousands were made to disappear by this military junta.

Now, in December, during 1981, the military junta is at a crossroads, because the situation in Argentina is catastrophic and the military dictatorship’s popularity is at its lowest level. The situation in Argentina is catastrophic for many reasons. To begin with, it is catastrophic economically: inflation is at 600 percent, poverty is widespread, and despite the military dictatorship the unions are in the streets protesting every day, despite the fact that every demonstration is suppressed by the police. And the economic crisis is coupled with a political crisis. The problem of the *desaparecidos* is coming out. There are the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, who today have become the Grandmothers, if any of them are still alive. Every Thursday in the Plaza de Mayo they demonstrate in front of the Casa Rosada, the presidential palace in Buenos Aires, to ask, where have our children gone? And those demonstrations too are suppressed by the police, broken up, and every Thursday they are there again. In substance, Argentina is boiling, the military junta is tottering and no longer knows which way to turn. So much so that at the end of 1981 the military junta stages a coup against itself. The dictator who had been there until then, General Viola, is deposed by another general who, with the consent of the majority of the military, takes power. This other general is called Leopoldo Galtieri. General Galtieri takes power in Argentina on December 22, 1981, proclaims himself president of Argentina for life, with absolute powers and the right to choose his successor.

So who is General Galtieri? A brief profile. Like a great many Argentines, he is the son of extremely poor Italian migrants. You know that Argentina is half an Italian country; half of Argentines have at least one Italian ancestor. General Galtieri is a military man of Italian origin who has risen through the ranks. How did Leopoldo Galtieri make his career? There is a crucial episode in his military career. In 1949, as a young lieutenant, he is selected to attend a course at a particular school. In Panama there is a school run by the United States Army for officers from South American armies: the School of the Americas. This school of the Americas in Panama is a school where young officers from the Chilean, Brazilian, Argentine, Peruvian, and Colombian armies are taught by American instructors, from what one can gather, not so much to wage war as to carry out repression and counterinsurgency. Many South American dictators and collaborators of dictators pass through this school in Panama. Second Lieutenant Leopoldo Galtieri of the Argentine Army passes through it as well. Here I will tell you a piece of gossip, but in my opinion it is gossip. When Galtieri died about ten years ago, the English newspaper *The Economist* published a venomous obituary in which, among other things, it said that apparently Galtieri was the only officer in history to fail at the Panamanian school for South American officers. But this, I repeat, is gossip in my view; it is not necessarily true. 

The fact remains that Galtieri advances in his career, and when the military junta takes power in Argentina, Galtieri is one of its leading figures, and one of its most visible figures internationally as well. At the start of 1981—the coup takes place in December, when he becomes president for life—ten months earlier he had been in the United States, received by Ronald Reagan, President of the United States, with kisses and embraces, especially now that Argentina is pursuing this fiercely anti-communist policy and crushing the left-wing opposition at home. Reagan sees in the regime of the generals, and openly describes it as, a bulwark against communism. If any of you were here yesterday, I am experiencing déjà vu. Remember Lord Halifax, the English foreign secretary, who goes to Berlin in 1937 and tells Hitler, of course, Germany, a bulwark against communism... Well, history sometimes repeats itself in its own way. General Galtieri is welcomed enthusiastically in Washington. There are various declarations about how good this Argentine general is; a magnificent general, declares some Reagan adviser whose identity is no longer clear—there are different versions—but in short, a magnificent general. 

In December of that same year, 1981, the magnificent general stages his coup and takes power in Argentina with the support of the admirals and generals with whom he has reached an agreement. We will do something that will put everything right. We will occupy the Falklands—or rather, the Malvinas. We will occupy the Malvinas. The pact that brings Galtieri to power provides that in the coming months this occupation of the Malvinas by force will be planned. It is based—I have always spoken to you on these evenings about gambles—on two gambles here as well. The first gamble is that when the Argentine people hear that the Malvinas have been occupied—occupied, liberated—they will be wild with enthusiasm, mad with patriotic fervor, and will forget inflation, the *desaparecidos*, and so on. The second gamble—exactly this will happen. The second gamble is that the world will accept the fait accompli. The English will never have the courage to react and, in any event, President Reagan is our friend and will therefore make sure that there are no serious problems.

## 3. Failed negotiations and Britain's blind spot

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00233–U-00365 -->

The Argentine military’s decision is of course secret at first; nothing leaks out. Nothing leaks out also because, in reality, for some years the impression has been that the Falklands-Malvinas question is, how shall I put it, more or less being resolved. You need to know that for about fifteen years there have occasionally been Anglo-Argentine meetings in which the future of the Falklands is discussed. And at these meetings progress is made, agreements are reached. Great Britain has never put sovereignty over the islands on the table. But the widespread feeling is that perhaps one day it too may be discussed. Forms of joint management are accepted. Argentina also makes substantial concessions, to be clear. In those years, since negotiations and discussions are taking place in a cordial climate, Argentina guarantees air and sea links to the Falklands, which would otherwise be completely isolated, because Argentina is five hundred kilometers away but every other place in the world is thousands of kilometers from the Falklands. So there is a cordial climate.

The last meeting—these meetings take place in New York, because it is the UN that has compelled the English and Argentines to negotiate—is in February 1982. At the end of February 1982 there is another meeting, and the final communiqué—you know, in diplomatic meetings the language of the final press release is fundamental, because it tells you how things went—the final communiqué says, “The meeting took place in a cordial and positive spirit.” That is at the end of February. On March 2, a statement by the Argentine government comes out. The Argentine government declares: Argentina has negotiated with Great Britain over the problem of sovereignty of the islands with patience, loyalty, and good faith for more than fifteen years. Enough now. The Argentines now say, enough of meetings decided one at a time. We want what today would be called a road map. They did not use the term yet in 1982. We want a precise deadline, a schedule of monthly meetings for the purpose of transferring sovereignty over the islands to Argentina. England must tell us by when it will transfer sovereignty. The communiqué concludes in this way: if this is not accepted, Argentina intends to end the negotiations and freely choose the procedure that best suits its interests.  Diplomatic language is always so delicate, is it not? But if one reads between the lines.

The communiqué is dated March 2; on March 3 the Argentine press loses its mind. The Buenos Aires newspapers run wild and predict a break in diplomatic relations with England. We might also cut communications to the Falklands. How will they manage without us, when our planes bring them medicines and supplies? The Argentine press says—here is something the Argentine government did not say. The Argentine government said a date needs to be fixed. The press goes further. England has three or four months to recognize Argentine sovereignty over the islands and set a date for their return. Otherwise, the press says what the government did not say: we might even take the islands back. The Americans would understand.

The English ambassador in Buenos Aires takes all the newspapers that morning, transcribes the headlines and articles, and sends a telegram to London, to the prime minister. On her copy of this telegram, the prime minister in London notes, “We must make contingency plans.” It is March 3, 1982. The arrival of this news in London is a bolt from the blue. No one expected anything. This story is also in part a story of intelligence and diplomatic failures. The English Foreign Office foresaw nothing. An English journalist explained it this way. The Foreign Office, the English Ministry of Foreign Affairs, he says, is crammed with Arabists who are always ready to go and put things right in the Middle East. By contrast, at practically no level is there anyone who knows South America or cares anything about it. So the Foreign Office thought of everything except the Argentines going into crisis over the Falklands. The secret services not only reassured the government, but would continue reassuring the prime minister for the whole month of March. Yes, Argentines, we know what sort of people they are, half Italian, they talk, but they will never do anything. We have the reports now, so this is documented history. Her Majesty’s Intelligence Service, 007, is convinced that the Argentines are bluffing.

The prime minister is a little uneasy all the same. The English prime minister is of course Mrs. Thatcher, Margaret Thatcher. The first woman I have occasion to speak about in these lectures of ours, the first woman of whom I have occasion to say, let us go and see how she behaves in managing a crisis. It would be too easy to say, let us see whether she behaves differently from—well, never mind. Just keep this in mind. This time, for the first time in history, perhaps since the time of Queen Elizabeth in the sixteenth century, it is a woman who will decide whether war should be made or not.

But be careful, because Margaret Thatcher, who died last year and has now completed her career and her life, has gone down in history. She is a historical figure of enormous importance who left her mark on the world, because Thatcher’s reforms—the reforms, liberalizations, the struggle against the unions, the destruction of union power, the end of nationalized industries, privatizations—Thatcherism truly changed the world. Each person, in their heart and also officially and openly, knows whether we should be happy or not about the way Mrs. Thatcher changed the world. But she is certainly a figure of enormous importance to us. In the spring of 1982, Mrs. Thatcher is not yet the woman who has gone down in history. She has not yet won any of her battles. England is in an economic crisis. Thatcher has not defeated the unions, she has not privatized, she has not managed to do anything, and on top of that she is crushed—as the English government also was in 1914, for that matter—by that enormous wound England carries with it: Ireland.

When the crisis with Argentina over the Falklands explodes, only a few months have passed since an episode all of us remember, when the Northern Irish IRA prisoners begin their hunger strike. Remember Bobby Sands? They demand better conditions and recognition as political prisoners, and they let themselves starve to death one after another. Ten die of hunger while asking the English government to recognize them as political prisoners, and the English government—that is, Margaret Thatcher—says no. A crime is a crime is a crime; politics has nothing to do with it. Signed, Margaret Thatcher. So Thatcher is extremely weak at that moment; she has not yet accomplished anything and everyone predicts that she will lose the next election. Among the many things she has to think about, she also receives these reports that the Buenos Aires newspapers are running wild over the Falkland Islands, but the secret services say not to worry. Mrs. Falkland—Mrs. Falkland, goodbye. Mrs. Thatcher waits. During March the Argentines begin maneuvering in the area in order to raise the tension in any case.

## 4. South Georgia and the escalation of March 1982

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00366–U-00457 -->

Now here I have to tell you something. I had told myself: shall I tell it? No, let us leave it out. No, it belongs here, because in March the only thing that happens is what I am about to tell you. Near the Falkland Islands there is another archipelago that is even more wretched, even colder, where no one lives, only penguins. It is called South Georgia and belongs to the British Crown. There is only a platoon of Marines with a sort of meteorological observation station. When I say that the South Georgia islands are near the Falklands, you will laugh at me: they are two thousand kilometers from the Falklands. But they are the nearest bit of dry land all the same. There is nothing around them. Go home and look at the map: cosmic emptiness. So South Georgia and the Falklands belong to the same strategic area.

During March, strange things begin happening on these South Georgia islands. Argentines land there. They are little groups of workers; there is a contract to recover scrap metal. But at a certain point these Argentine workers raise the Argentine flag. The Marines on guard do not quite know what to do. In reality, we know today that some of these Argentine workers are army commandos. Just one detail to remind us of the period. It would not be necessary, but I will give it to you anyway. The commander of this group of Argentine marines disguised as workers who lands on South Georgia in March 1982 is an officer named Lieutenant Alfredo Astiz, who is currently serving a life sentence in Argentina and is known as one of the principal torturers active in what was the main torture center in Buenos Aires, the Navy Mechanics School. In March 1982 Lieutenant Alfredo Astiz temporarily suspends his activity at the navy torture center in order to land on South Georgia, just to complete the picture. He is serving life, but only for a few years now. First the French sentenced him to life imprisonment, but in absentia, because among the many people Alfredo Astiz tortured and killed were two French nuns. Then, finally, three years ago the Argentines too sent him to prison for life.

In any case, close parenthesis. All this happens, and in London they ask themselves, what do the Argentines want? The intelligence services say they are concerned about the Falklands, but there is no danger until they decide to land. Thatcher thinks about negotiating, offering talks, seeing what they say. She does not even deal with it that much herself; the English foreign secretary handles it. Let us open a small parenthesis. The English foreign secretary is Lord Carrington, whom some may remember because he was also secretary general of NATO. Lord Carrington, Peter Carrington, is a minister very loyal to Thatcher, a great friend, someone very sensitive to charisma, one of the first people to recognize a formidable figure in Thatcher.

There is a famous anecdote in England about a journalist interviewing Lord Carrington. Since Thatcher seems very weak and everyone is convinced that she will be thrown out at the next election, the journalist wants Lord Carrington, her foreign secretary, to say who the Conservative Party will put in her place if Thatcher falls. Lord Carrington does not want to say. No, no, he says, she stays; there is nothing to discuss. In the end the journalist, exasperated, says, all right, but suppose a bus runs Mrs. Thatcher over tomorrow. And Lord Carrington answers, “Nor if a bus dared to run her over would I be prepared to stand.”

In any case, Lord Carrington is someone Margaret trusts, and the Foreign Office guarantees that there should be no problems. The first day on which the Foreign Office led by Lord Carrington begins to worry is March 24. That day Lord Carrington sends a note to the prime minister and says: we are discussing whether we can negotiate with the Argentines, but bear in mind that the atmosphere is crazy; their press keeps insulting us. I do not know whether we can negotiate with someone who insults us and puts pressure on us in this way. It may even be that we will have to reach a confrontation. What might reaching a confrontation mean? But in the meantime the prime minister has been warned.

In fact, Lord Carrington has arrived just in time: it is March 24. We know today that on March 26 Galtieri, president of Argentina for life, gives the go-ahead for the invasion of the Falklands. Operation Rosario. A plan to land in the Malvinas on April 2. No one knows; it is top secret. Thatcher has realized that the tension is mounting. What do we do? What does one do when tension mounts? On the one hand, perhaps you keep saying, let us negotiate, yes. But when you keep saying that we are willing to negotiate, the moment comes when you have to say, but understand that we are serious. On March 29 Mrs. Thatcher does what she can do: she orders a nuclear submarine to leave Gibraltar for the Falklands immediately. It will take the nuclear submarine two weeks to reach the Falklands, but in the meantime the news leaks to the press, no one knows how. Mrs. Thatcher is very pleased that the press has learned about it. In her memoirs she will say, “My instinct was that the time had come to show the Argentines that we meant business.” In short, the moment comes when we too have to flex our muscles.

## 5. Thatcher learns the invasion is imminent

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00458–U-00524 -->

Does this remind you of the things we talked about yesterday evening and the evening before? Yes, it reminds me of them. Fortunately everything is on a much smaller scale, but Mrs. Thatcher is worried. Despite the assurances from the Foreign Office and the secret services, she notes—no, actually she does not note this; she writes it in her memoirs after the fact, so take it with a grain of salt, because in memoirs one may adjust things a little afterward. But in her memoirs Thatcher says: “However, we knew that they were unpredictable and unstable, and that a dictatorship might not behave in ways we would consider rational.” There one cannot tell whether she means these dictators or these Latins—you know how it is. Then she continues: “By now I was deeply uneasy. Yet still I do not think that any of us expected an immediate invasion of the Falklands themselves.”

Events come to a head on the evening of March 31. On the evening of March 31, the English defense minister, John Nott, asks Thatcher for an emergency meeting. They summon a few ministers. The defense minister rushes to Downing Street with a report from the American intelligence services, who have finally helped the English make some sense of things. The American intelligence services have absolutely certain information that evening, March 31. The Argentine fleet is at sea, loaded with troops, and heading for the Falklands.

This meeting on the evening of March 31 is naturally one of the decisive moments. The English defense minister sets out the situation and tells Mrs. Thatcher that if the Argentines occupy the islands, it is unthinkable to imagine taking them back. We could never manage it. Thatcher’s hair stands on end. What do you mean, we could never manage it? That is completely out of the question. We must take them back if they land. And do not come and tell me we cannot do it. Where are the military men? Where is the admiral who is chief of the naval staff? The admiral who is chief of the naval staff has not arrived yet. Why is Sir Henry Leach, chief of staff of Her Majesty’s fleet, not arriving? He does not arrive. Eventually they discover what happened. He was in civilian clothes. He thought he would take a shortcut through Parliament; they arrested him while he was crossing Parliament dressed in civilian clothes. Finally an MP recognized him and said, but he is the admiral. Then they brought him to the prime minister.

The prime minister asks the admiral: is it conceivable to mount an expedition to retake the Falklands if they land? The admiral makes a couple of calculations and tells her, of course it can be done. I can send this aircraft carrier, that other one, destroyers, frigates; I have this, I have that, I have everything. If you give the order, I will begin assembling the fleet. Who can give the order? Does it require a vote in Parliament—do they go and see? No, the prime minister can give it. It is not war; it is only assembling the fleet. That evening the prime minister orders the admiral to begin assembling the fleet, and they say, all right, perhaps the order to leave had better be discussed in the Council of Ministers. But first, it is March 31, the Argentine landing is expected on April 2, there is still a day and a half, one day, two nights. Something absolutely must be done to see whether they can be stopped.

What can be done? We have already sent the nuclear submarine, so, well. How can they be stopped? There is only one way in the post–Second World War world. What would you have done? I telephone Big Brother, I telephone Ronald Reagan. Thatcher sends—she does not call, she sends—a telegram to Reagan over the secure line, urgently. I will read you Thatcher’s telegram to Reagan on the night of March 31. “Dear Ron, You will know about the disturbing intelligence reports which we have received indicating that the Argentine Navy could be preparing to invade the Falkland Islands within the next 48 hours.” Among other things, she writes, “I am asking you to intervene with President Galtieri and to seek his immediate assurance that there will be no Argentine landing on the Falkland Islands and no hostilities against our troops.” Fondest regards, Margaret.

## 6. Reagan's call and the American choice

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00525–U-00621 -->

So a new player enters the field: the United States of America, the White House. The White House enters the field. Reagan telephones Galtieri. Galtieri cannot be found. We have heard this before too. Finally Reagan insists; after a while it is difficult to say no when the President of the United States is telephoning you. After a while Galtieri answers.

We have a source in real time. It is the diary of one of Reagan’s aides, a man named Jim Rentschler. He is the White House official assigned to brief Reagan on this matter, on what the Falklands are and what is happening, because he has to call this Galtieri. While Reagan is on the telephone with Galtieri, the White House official Jim Rentschler jots down some notes for his diary. He is a clever fellow who has already understood that the diary will be important, so he takes care over it; it is a good diary.

So, while Reagan is there on the telephone, Rentschler makes a note in his diary. He begins with a joke about an advertisement for the Bell Company, the American telephone company. There is this advertisement that says, “Reach out and touch someone,” and Rentschler writes, “The Bell System never had to deal with General Leopoldo Galtieri.” Then he explains: Reagan’s chief aide is the Argentine strongman with whom the president is now speaking on the long-distance line to Buenos Aires to convince him not to invade the Falkland Islands. Never heard of them, right? Neither had I until last night. This is the man who prepared the file for Ronald Reagan. Neither had I until last night, when this request arrived from the English prime minister to prevent the Argentines from landing on these Falkland Islands.

Then there is an assessment of Galtieri. Keep in mind that Galtieri is the man who a year earlier was welcomed with open arms in Washington, a magnificent general. Now Reagan’s adviser is listening to the call and making notes. This fellow Galtieri, who does not wait for the interpreter, is “speaking first in broken mafioso-type English.” You understand, right? He could be Italian, Argentine; his English is so-so, and what does that bring to mind for them? They have watched so many television dramas, right? With the mafioso who speaks with a Latin accent. This Galtieri, speaking in this broken English—“he sounds like a thug.” And Rentschler notes: the president is speaking with great eloquence, I admire him greatly, but Galtieri is a man “whose machismo,” he fears, “will propel him forward in whatever direction he is headed.” Machismo: by now they have decided, and they cannot lose face; they will go ahead.

Reagan, poor Reagan, does what he can. Reagan is someone who, as one of his aides is later discovered to have said in those days, declares: I do not understand why they are quarreling so much over that little heap of frozen land down there. An ice-cold bunch of land, down there. But Reagan has one quality. When a position has to be taken, he throws himself in and takes it. You know, because I told you, that the Argentines have made a precise calculation. We are great friends of the Americans. The Americans will not intervene and will ensure that no one else intervenes.

That evening Reagan discusses it with his advisers. Someone tells him, yes, in fact, let the Argentines do it; they are our friends. I do not know whether you remember that American politician, also a woman, named Jeane Kirkpatrick, Mrs. Kirkpatrick, United States ambassador to the UN and so forth. Mrs. Kirkpatrick was famous because, when it came to dictatorships, she said: dictatorships are all a bad thing, but there are regimes that can be recovered and ones that cannot. The regimes that cannot be recovered are left-wing regimes; nothing can be done with them. Right-wing dictatorships can be recovered and consequently we can even get along with them. Mrs. Kirkpatrick would prefer to do nothing: America should stay out of it and let them get on with it. A colorful detail: the next day Mrs. Kirkpatrick will be the guest of honor at a dinner at the Argentine embassy.

But the other adviser, Haig—Alexander Haig, Reagan’s secretary of state—takes the opposite view. We cannot let them go ahead. And Reagan accepts this and yields. He writes to dear Margaret. A telegram from Reagan to dear Margaret. Dear Margaret, I have just spoken at length with General Galtieri about the situation in the Falklands. I conveyed my personal concern about the possibility of an Argentine invasion; I told him that beginning military operations would compromise—I offered my good offices, and so forth and so forth and so forth. The general listened to me but made no commitment. On the contrary, he left me with the clear impression that they have now embarked on armed conflict. And Reagan concludes his telegram to Thatcher: “While we have a policy of neutrality on the sovereignty issue, we will not be neutral on the issue of Argentine use of military force.” All the best, Ron. Thatcher receives this telegram, from which it emerges that the Argentines will no longer stop and that the Americans will not cover for them. If the Argentines really use force, the Americans will be with us.

## 7. Invasion, Cabinet, and national humiliation

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00622–U-00712 -->

At this point Thatcher convenes the government on the morning of April 2. The Argentines have already landed in the Falklands; they do not know it yet. The governor of the Falklands sent a message; it was lost in the air and never arrived. Later a message from a Falklands radio amateur will arrive, intercepted by an English vessel and retransmitted to London, and that is how London learns that the Argentines have occupied the Falkland Islands. The English government meets in the dark, but knowing that by now the crisis is unavoidable. What should be done? Thatcher informs her ministers that she has already given the order to assemble a fleet. And the fleet is now being assembled. They will have to decide whether to make it leave.

They will discuss it at length. In the meantime they discuss many other things. Here is an example of a detail the English government discusses that Friday, April 2. The fleet will be led by the aircraft carrier *Invincible*. But serving aboard the aircraft carrier *Invincible* as an officer is Prince Andrew, the Queen’s son. What is to be done? If the aircraft carrier sails for war, does Prince Andrew remain at his post or is it better to keep him at home? A discussion takes place between Downing Street and Buckingham Palace; the Queen expresses her opinion. As long as Prince Andrew is serving on the aircraft carrier *Invincible*, he will take part in the expedition. His grandfather, King George VI, fought in the Battle of Jutland, and consequently it is out of the question for a member of the royal family to be treated differently from any other officer.

With this problem settled, broadly speaking Thatcher sees no other major problems. That evening the government decides that the fleet will go. In two days, as soon as it is ready, the fleet will sail. This is despite the fact that the Foreign Office is making a great many objections. In Thatcher’s memoirs you can see her exasperation with the Foreign Office, which, she says, gave her advice that “summed up the flexibility of principle characteristic of that Department.” Notice the Thatcher style. “I was presented with the dangers of a backlash against the British expatriates in Argentina, problems about getting support in the UN Security Council, the lack of reliance we could place on the European Community or the United States, the risk of the Soviets becoming involved, the disadvantage of being looked at as a colonial power.” All valid points, but when you are at war you cannot allow difficulties to dominate your thinking; you must go ahead with an iron will to overcome them. And in any event, what was the alternative? “That a common or garden dictator should rule over the Queen’s subjects and prevail by fraud and violence? Not while I was Prime Minister.” This, mind you, is written afterward, but in any case Thatcher’s idea is certainly that.

On April 2, when the Argentines have already landed, Reagan tries again and telephones Galtieri. Galtieri answers: England has refused to return the Falklands for 149 years. Now time has run out. In England, let us remember, Thatcher is a prime minister in crisis, unpopular, considered weak, who will lose the next election. When the newspapers come out on April 3 with the news that the Argentines have occupied the Falklands, the feeling in England is one of national humiliation. Another disaster has happened to us; we have made fools of ourselves again. The newspapers say it is England’s worst humiliation since Suez.

What is Suez? 1956. The Egyptians nationalize the Suez Canal, which the English need. The English send paratroopers to occupy the canal. The Americans tell the English, forget it, go home. And the English go home with their tails between their legs. In English memory this is the moment when England discovers that it is no longer a world power, indeed that it risks making itself ridiculous when it tries to intervene in the world. On April 3, 1982, the English newspapers say we are facing another Suez. And that day Thatcher has to face Parliament. It is Saturday, the first time in many decades that the English Parliament has been summoned on a Saturday. The House of Commons is packed.

You know what the House of Commons is like? It is small; there is not a seat for every MP. It is designed that way, since they are never all there anyway. And when they all come, it is because something is important, and it is good for them all to be there crowded together, standing, sitting on the steps, listening to what happens. The other characteristic of the English Parliament that we must remember, because we are used to ours, is that the English Parliament is one in which many MPs vote as they themselves think and not as the party tells them. It is perfectly normal in the English Parliament for cross-party majorities to form on important issues. And so in the English Parliament it is essential to convince the MPs, which is why knowing how to make speeches is extremely important in that Parliament.

## 8. Parliament tests the Iron Lady

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00713–U-00864 -->

Thatcher makes one of the great speeches of her career, telling Parliament: “I am sure that the whole House will join me in condemning totally this unprovoked aggression by the Government of Argentina against British territory. It has not a shred of justification and not a scrap of legality.” Thatcher takes control of Parliament. There is only one moment when she risks losing it, because she wants to make a nasty point against Labour; she wants to remind Labour, the opposition, of some weakness they showed when they were in government. And so she begins to say, may I remind the honorable members of the opposition that they too, when they were in government—and a Labour MP interrupts: we did not lose the Falklands. At that moment the mood of the House wavers a little, but Thatcher takes control of it again, and in the end Parliament votes in favor of the fleet’s departure. The fleet leaves, it is about to leave; Parliament is determined.

In her memoirs Thatcher says: I was terrified all the same, because the majority did not really exist. That majority was false. Some, like me, were prepared to go all the way to war. Many thought, let us send the fleet, the Argentines will be frightened, negotiations will begin. Very few, Thatcher says, thought of going all the way. And on the other hand there are also people in Parliament who say: yes, yes, this lady is making a fine show now, but will this woman then have the guts to go all the way?

There is one speech that has remained famous in England. I will tell you about it; that is what we are here for. The MP is called Enoch Powell. He is an extremely famous politician in England, very popular, a kind of freelance. He is on the right, a conservative, but he has left the Conservatives and is on his own. He takes a very anti-immigrant position, for example, very nationalist. But on other matters he is on the left instead; he is a very peculiar figure. Enoch Powell speaks in the House of Commons on April 3, and Thatcher remembers that as he speaks he stares directly at her and speaks, she says, in a sepulchral tone. And in a sepulchral tone Enoch Powell says: “The Prime Minister, shortly after she came into office, received a soubriquet as the ‘Iron Lady’.” Thatcher had received this nickname immediately; it was the Soviets who had nicknamed her that, seeing how tough she was. Powell explains that it arose in the context of relations with the Soviet Union and its allies, and that Thatcher seemed pleased and proud of it. Then he concludes: “In the next week or two this House, the nation and the right hon. Lady herself will learn of what metal she is made.”

So Thatcher leaves Parliament with this wish, this expectation: we shall see what metal you are made of. It should be said that Thatcher knows she is in tune with the country. The country, more than Parliament and more than the Foreign Office, is determined to go all the way. Are there opinion polls? The polls are very clear. Eighty percent of English people answer that the Falklands must be retaken. Then the second question is: but is it better to retake them through peaceful negotiation or by force? The majority of English people answer, by force. So Thatcher is in tune with her people; the fleet will leave.

At this point someone begins saying, but this is a war. Let us declare war. There is a meeting—we have those marvelous documents I was telling you about, right? Minutes of the committee of Conservative MPs. The transcript is online; there are even the original handwritten minutes with little drawings and underlining, and the MPs one after another say everything under the sun. There is also Lord Onslow, whose contribution is: sink their entire fleet. But most of the MPs—well, some MPs are worried. One MP says that people are worried about what has happened and we must not appear weak. At the moment the Foreign Office stinks; it stinks of appeasement. Those of you who were here yesterday, remember appeasement? What is it? It is the name given in English politics to Chamberlain’s attempt in 1938–39 to keep Hitler quiet by conceding many things to him. Appeasement, peace at any price. In the English political vocabulary it has become a dirty word. At that moment in 1982 some say, after all, the Foreign Office keeps saying let us negotiate; this is dangerous, it stinks of appeasement. Let us go ahead.

But then most MPs say: we are at war. The foreign secretary, Carrington, answers: no, there is no obligation to declare war. We are not at war. Other MPs insist: but declare war, Lord Carrington. It is difficult to declare war, there are legal difficulties, we must consult the lawyers, and this is in the minutes. In reality, this conceals the fact that the government has decided that declaring war would be a needless complication. These truly are new times, because the men of 1914 and even those of 1939 took care to send the ambassador with the declaration of war, perhaps full of lies, but they sent it. Now, by contrast, times have changed. Now the times are perhaps more cynical or more pragmatic, perhaps a little more cunning as well, all things considered. Who knows? All of this together. But there is no reason to declare war, as Mrs. Thatcher officially reiterates two days later, on April 5.

An interview with Mrs. Thatcher. The journalist asks whether she is really about to make war on Argentina. Thatcher answers: “Our objective is to recover the Falkland islands. We have to do what is necessary.” And then: “I wouldn’t talk in terms of war. A declaration of war is something different.” Let us not go into the technical details. We must recover the Falkland Islands for Great Britain and for the people who live there. Do not insist on technical aspects; we know what we must do. There are many different ways to achieve it; let us hope we have not chosen the worst one. “When you stop a dictator there are always risks but there are great risks in not stopping a dictator. My generation learned that a long time ago.”

And here, naturally, we must reflect on this aspect. We all knew Thatcher, but now she is dead and she was very old. Thatcher lived through appeasement and Hitler and the Second World War. For her, in 1982, these were still things she had lived through and remembered well. And so the influence of what happened in 1939 comes into play in 1982. What should England do when faced with a dictator? Try to stop him? And come away in shame as at Munich? That is, not try to stop him? It must stop him.

The Foreign Office, as you know, did not agree. And in fact on April 5 Lord Carrington resigns, declaring: it was my ministry that was supposed to manage this crisis. We did not succeed; we are at war. On my honor, I feel I must resign. Now, Lord Carrington was a dear friend of Mrs. Thatcher, a faithful collaborator, but politics is what it is. I will now tell you Mrs. Thatcher’s reaction, when she is interviewed, to the fact that her foreign secretary Peter Carrington has resigned. Mrs. Thatcher says: it was a serious blow to me; I am very sorry to lose him; he was a valuable man in my government, but what can be done? Thatcher explains: “He felt that the policy had failed and therefore it was a matter of honour that he should go.” I am very sorry. And then Thatcher says: “It is rather a wonderful thing in politics to have people who feel strongly about honour and who resign.” And so, exit Lord Carrington.

## 9. Defeat transforms Argentina and Thatcher

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00865–U-00965 -->

It is almost over. I am not telling you the story of the war; I am telling you only how Margaret decided to send the fleet. Then for a month the Americans will try to shuttle back and forth between the Argentines and the English, saying, stop. Everyone remains fixed in their positions, and war is reached. But in Argentina, well before the English landing and the war are reached, morale collapses. The Argentine press continues to say, we are ready, we are waiting for them, but panic is spreading, especially at the upper levels of Argentine society.

We have a secret report from the American intelligence services. It contains the testimony of a high-level Argentine politician who was a member of the military junta, who supports the military junta, and who tells his American confidant: by now it is do or die. That is, if in the end we keep the Falklands, good; otherwise Galtieri falls. We cannot start negotiating now; it would be worse. Galtieri would fall immediately. For him, if anything, it is better to lose the war. And the American observer notes: in any case, my Argentine interlocutor is sure that Argentina will lose. There is no politician in the country who does not think the invasion was a colossal mistake. There is an awareness that the president and his advisers are incompetent.

And then there is this thing that I, being Italian, found fascinating. The Argentine politician tells the American that he is now forty-one years old and now knows that in the course of his life he will never see a serious Argentina. The nice thing is that the American does not understand what he means and explains: he means a stable, democratic, and prosperous country. But as an Italian I understand perfectly well what the Argentine is thinking when he says, why is my country not serious? Will I ever see a serious country?  No, this Argentine says, perhaps my children will see a country, a serious Argentina. I have understood that I will no longer see it.

In fact the war ends, as you know. In a few weeks the English win, win overwhelmingly. The war costs one thousand lives: 649 Argentines, 258 English, three Falklands civilians. The war was never declared, and so there is not even any need to make a peace treaty. It was a thing; it happened, but it is not declared, it leaves no trace. It leaves no trace; it leaves some fine consequences. On June 14 the Argentine flag is lowered over Port Stanley and the last Argentine troops surrender. Four days later, on June 18, Galtieri resigns. They send another general, they try to keep the dictatorship standing, they cannot do it. The situation is such that the military junta is forced to call free elections. Remember? Alfonsín becomes president of Argentina. The military dictatorship ends.

Galtieri will be in prison from 1986 to 1991, then he is pardoned, then they put him on trial again. In 2002 he has a heart attack, and then he dies under house arrest, as happens with elderly politicians who are put on trial. When Galtieri dies in 2003, *The Economist* writes that venomous obituary I already quoted at the beginning. Among other things it says that “He might have been forgiven by at least some of his fellow Argentines for his ruthlessness, but not for his stupidity.”

So the Falklands War brings down the Argentine military junta and creates Mrs. Thatcher. Because the turning point in Margaret Thatcher’s political career is the Falklands War. In its aftermath Thatcher is the strongest politician the United Kingdom has seen for a long time. She wins the 1983 election by a landslide and does everything she does, for good or ill depending on how each of us thinks.

That MP Enoch Powell, who had spoken in Parliament saying that in the next few weeks the country would see what metal the prime minister was made of—you know what the English are like, how their Parliament works—in a later sitting after the end of the war, MP Powell takes the floor and declares: “It shows that the substance under test consisted of ferrous matter of the highest quality, that it is of exceptional tensile strength, is highly resistant to wear and tear and to stress, and may be used with advantage for all national purposes.”

And Mrs. Thatcher becomes, precisely, Mrs. Thatcher, and a few weeks later in an official speech says this: “What really thrilled me, having spent so much of my lifetime in Parliament, and talking about things like inflation, Social Security benefits, housing problems, environmental problems and so on, is that when it really came to the test, what’s thrilled people wasn’t those things, what thrilled people was once again being able to serve a great cause, the cause of liberty.” Well, perhaps she would have been more sincere if she had said that what excited people was finding themselves at war again. But she also had a way of putting it that, in short, as rhetoric, can serve the cause of liberty.

As with so many things about Margaret Thatcher, this declaration, and her management of the Falklands crisis in general, may thrill some people, or it may send shivers down someone else’s spine, or perhaps both things together—which is, in the end, the effect it had on me. And so, just as going back over these three evenings to see how wars break out has, how shall I say, taught me many things, it has also thrilled and fascinated me, and it has also given me a few shivers as I saw how wars break out and how politicians manage crises. And I hope the same thing happened to you. Thank you, and until next year.
