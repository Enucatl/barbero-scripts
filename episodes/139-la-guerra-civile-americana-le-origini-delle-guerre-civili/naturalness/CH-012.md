## 12. John Brown, Republicans, and Lincoln’s Campaign

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01130–U-01243 -->

In 1859, there is one last dramatic episode, and once again the central figure is John Brown. Brown has left Kansas, and now he intends to get serious: he wants to launch a slave revolt in the South. So he puts together a band of men and plans to seize the military arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, then hand out the weapons to Black people.

In the summer of 1859, Brown rents a farm near Harpers Ferry and gradually brings his men there. Once he has about twenty armed men, he seizes the virtually undefended arsenal on October 16. He begins sending free Black people from plantation to plantation, trying to persuade the slaves to rise up. No one moves.

The militia arrives. President Buchanan sends Marines from Washington under a colonel who will soon become famous: Robert E. Lee, the very man who, shortly afterward, will command the Southern army. The Marines exchange fire with Brown’s men. Most of Brown’s followers are killed; Brown is captured, tried, and sentenced to death. He is hanged on December 2, 1859.

He leaves behind a few lines declaring: “I John Brown am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land: will never be purged away; but with Blood. I had as I now think: vainly flattered myself that without very much bloodshed; it might be done.”

John Brown’s death creates a myth—a myth that, in fact, still exists today. On the day he is hanged, bells ring in his honor across the North, cannon fire salutes him, and the song is born.

The following year, 1860, brings an election. A new party enters the race to challenge the Democratic Party’s dominance. It recognizes that the West is no longer certain to side with the South. By now, the West shares too many interests with the North, and that old alliance can be broken. The new party sets out to unite Northern industrialists with Western settlers and put the slaveholding South in the minority. It presents itself as progressive and egalitarian, and it takes the name Republican Party.

As you can see, there’s a paradox here. The Republican and Democratic parties still exist today, but their political positions are, broadly speaking, the reverse of what they were then.

The Republicans enter the election with excellent prospects because the South and West really are drifting apart. The Democratic Party splits and runs two rival candidates. There is also an independent candidate. So the Republicans, you might say, hold all the cards.

To make sure they win, they need to carry the West, so they choose a Westerner as their candidate: Lincoln. He is a lawyer and a seasoned politician with experience in Illinois and Washington. Yet even though he is a professional politician, the party can still fashion him into a frontier figure—the settler’s son raised in a log cabin, the man who understands you and represents you.

Lincoln is a moderate candidate. The Republican platform is extremely moderate on slavery because the party knows perfectly well that radical abolitionism, while gaining ground, is still not a mass movement. Abolitionist ideas have not yet taken hold among most people.

Take Kansas—Bleeding Kansas—where supporters and opponents of slavery have been killing one another. In the end, the antislavery side wins. The Kansas constitution bans slavery but limits voting to white male citizens. Barring free Black people from living in Kansas is discussed, but in the end it is not included in the constitution. [N-029] Even so, for many whites opposing slavery really means: we don’t want competition from Black people.

Lincoln is also chosen because the Republicans hope he won’t frighten the South too much. He has never been an abolitionist. He knows, so to speak, that the problem will have to be confronted sooner or later, but he readily admits that he doesn’t know what to do about it. Let me quote one of his speeches: “If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do, as to the existing institution. My first impulse would be to free all the slaves, and send them to Liberia,---to their own native land. But a moment’s reflection would convince me, that whatever of high hope, as I think there is, there may be in this, in the long run, its sudden execution is impossible. [...] What next? Free them, and make them politically and socially, our equals? My own feelings will not admit of this; and if mine would, we well know that those of the great mass of white people will not.” That is Lincoln the politician.

So Lincoln’s platform says that he has no intention of interfering with slavery and that each state is sovereign and may decide what to do about it. At the same time, the Republican platform makes one thing clear: Southerners must stop talking about states’ rights. Secession? No. The federal government stands above the individual states. No state has the right to nullify federal law, and talk of secession is treason.

Economically, Lincoln’s platform gives Northern industrialists exactly what they want: massive investment, railroads heading west, and so on. The solid South wants nothing to do with Lincoln. Southern politicians portray him to their voters as the devil—an abolitionist, even a follower of John Brown. And you can imagine just how popular John Brown is in the South at that moment.
