## 5. Expansion, Slavery, and the Senate Balance

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00365–U-00509 -->

All in all, the South and the West still have the same interests: farmers on both sides. What finally breaks apart this great alliance between them is the dramatic expansion of the United States. By the 1840s and 1850s, the country is swallowing up this enormous stretch of land conquered from Mexico and pushing all the way to the Pacific. At the end of the 1840s, you have the California Gold Rush, for example, right? And now the great epic is no longer just the West, but the Far West—the distant West. You have the wars against the Plains Indians and the wagon trains of settlers pushing farther and farther west. You know all the American mythology, right? “Go West, young man,” because the West is the future of this new nation.

But there’s a problem. How exactly does the United States expand westward? It works like this: the West is nothing, it belongs to nobody. It belongs to the Indians, if anyone, but people simply pretend the Indians aren’t there. It belongs to Mexico, but the Mexicans are nasty and evil, so we defeat them and take whatever we want. Once a territory—which can be as large as a European country—has a few thousand white settlers, they can ask for it to be recognized as a U.S. territory. Not a state yet, but a territory, an intermediate stage. Then, after a few years, when the white population has grown further, it can officially become a state. [N-009] That’s how the United States keeps adding states. As you know, the Stars and Stripes originally had thirteen stars, one for each of the thirteen colonies that rebelled against England. Today the flag has fifty stars for fifty states. New states are added one after another.

So what’s the problem? The United States is divided between states that allow slavery and states that forbid it. The free-slave divide was not marked by a single line across the continent: the Mason-Dixon Line had eastern significance, while in the western territories the crucial boundary was the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′. [N-010] I say the North abolished slavery because, until the Revolution, slavery existed throughout the United States—even in the North, even in New England. But it had never really taken root there. In an economy built on trade, shipping, and industry, enslaved labor isn’t especially useful. On the great Southern plantations, it is. We’ll come back later to the real reasons the North generally abolished slavery. It’s not as simple as we might think. But for now, let’s leave that aside.

For now, all you need to understand is that the problem comes up whenever the country has to decide whether a new territory—and later a new state—will allow slavery. The Southern ruling class pushes to admit slavery in the new territories and states. Their thinking is: then we can move out there too, buy land, expand, build new plantations, and bring our slaves with us. If those states ban slavery, only Northern farmers will be able to settle and work there.

For the Southern states, extending slavery into the new states is vital. After Texas declares independence in 1836, Anglo-Texan rebels protect slavery, and Texas enters the United States as a slave state in 1845. Mexico has abolished slavery. [N-011] Southern elites want that war because they need the new states to be slave states. By now, everyone can see that two opposing blocs have formed: the industrial and commercial states on one side, and the agricultural slave states on the other.

That struggle plays out in Washington, where the Senate is a crucial part of the political system. As you probably know, the United States Senate is a marvelous institution—still marvelous today—because its makeup has nothing at all to do with a state’s population. Every state sends two senators to Washington, whether it’s a tiny state with half a million people or California. Two senators each.

So if you have a bloc of slave states, you need as many slave states as possible. Every new state that allows slavery gives our bloc two senators. Every new state that forbids it gives the other side two senators. This produces some surreal results.

Let me tell you about the first time they have to deal with it. It’s 1820, and a new Western state is being created: Missouri. For the first time, the two sides clash. Do we want slavery there? No, we don’t. In the end, the Southerners win, and Missouri enters as a slave state. But to compensate the North, they create a new Northern state by cutting off a small piece of Massachusetts: Maine. Maine had once been a separate colony and later became part of Massachusetts. Now they carve it away again and create a new Northern state, one that of course forbids slavery. That gives the North two more senators in Washington to represent its interests.

The issue comes up again during the Mexican-American War. The Wilmot Proviso of 1846 raises the question of whether slavery will be barred from territory acquired from Mexico. In December 1849, Representative Robert Toombs of Georgia declares: “I do not, then, hesitate to avow before this House and the country, and in the presence of the living God, that if by your legislation you seek to drive us from the territories of California and New Mexico, purchased by the common blood and treasure of the whole people, and to abolish slavery in this District, thereby attempting to fix a national degradation upon half the States of this Confederacy, I am for disunion; and if my physical courage be equal to the maintenance of my convictions of right and duty, I will devote all I am and all I have on earth to its consummation.” The Compromise of 1850 admits California as a free state and organizes Utah and New Mexico without a legal ban on slavery.
