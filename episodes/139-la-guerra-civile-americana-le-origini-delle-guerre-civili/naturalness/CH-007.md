## 7. Early Southern Doubts and Colonization Schemes

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00622–U-00702 -->

Meanwhile, at the beginning of the nineteenth century, people in the South are seriously debating whether to abolish slavery. Educated Southern elites feel the moral weight of it, and they also see slavery as an outdated economic system that isn’t really profitable. So there is a genuine debate in the South over whether abolition might be the better choice.

In the early years of the nineteenth century, they eventually decide to abolish—not slavery, because that would be an enormous step—but the slave trade. In other words: we’ll keep the slaves we have, but it’s inhuman to go out and get more. Congress passes the Act to Prohibit the Importation of Slaves in 1807, during Jefferson’s presidency, and it takes effect on January 1, 1808. [N-014] Jefferson is a Southerner and a slave owner, but he is also absolutely convinced that slavery will eventually have to be overcome. In the meantime, he thinks it’s better to keep it from expanding too much.

You see? Some people in the South are also doing the math and starting to worry. How many Black people are there? In 1860, there are four and a half million Black people in the United States, four million of them enslaved. They make up 14 percent of the country’s population—the same percentage as today—but in the South, they make up 40 percent. Almost all of them are there.

So around the time of the Revolution, the American War of Independence, it’s perfectly normal to hear Southerners say that slavery is outdated, immoral, and something the country has to move beyond. The first president, George Washington, is a great Virginia planter who owns two hundred slaves, whom he will free in his will. Washington writes that he had “great repugnance to encreasing my Slaves by purchase,” while elsewhere expressing gradual-emancipation sympathies. [N-015]

Extremely popular Southern politicians have no trouble saying such things in those years—and remember, we’re talking about the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Later, the tune will change, as we’ll see. In a private 1773 letter, Patrick Henry writes: “What adds to the wonder is, that this abominable practice has been introduced in the most enlightened ages.” In a Senate speech in 1826, John Randolph of Roanoke says: “It is a cancer on your face, and must not be tampered with by quacks, who never saw the disease or the patient, and prescribe across the Atlantic.” [N-016]

And as late as 1832—a fateful year that I realize I’ve already mentioned several times—the Virginia assembly, the legislature of the most important state in the South, debates a proposal to abolish slavery. It fails, but only by a few votes.

Above all, it fails because even if everyone can agree in principle that slavery will eventually have to end, what do you actually do? As we’ve said, slaves represent an enormous amount of capital. You can’t just—how can I put this? Private property is sacred, folks. This is America. You can’t take an enormous piece of private property away from people without compensating the owners. And where will the money come from?

Most of all, once you free Black people, what do you tell them to do? That’s where the debate stalls, because almost no one imagines that Black people can continue living in the United States as free citizens alongside white people. They have to be sent somewhere.

In the first decades of the nineteenth century, whenever Southerners debate abolition, organizations also spring up to send free Black people back to Africa. That’s the proposed solution: humanitarian societies will raise the money and arrange it. The American Colonization Society establishes Liberia in 1822 through agreements and coercive expansion, and begins financing the transfer of freed slaves to Africa. All of this happens under President Monroe, a Southern president and slave owner who strongly supports the project of sending Black people back to Africa. That’s why Liberia’s capital, after the colony becomes an independent country, is named Monrovia—and is still called that today—in honor of President Monroe, a slave owner who supports sending slaves to Africa as they are freed.

Over the next few decades, they send something like fifteen thousand freed Black people to Liberia. There, those settlers go on to dominate Indigenous populations through unequal political authority, coerced labor, and systems of dependency—but that’s another story. That’s the kind of society they have in mind. [N-017]

Now, the point is that everything I’ve been telling you applies to the South in the early years of the nineteenth century.
