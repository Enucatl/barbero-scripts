## 3. Three Regions and Westward Expansion

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00199–U-00276 -->

So what explains the South’s political dominance? Well, although I’ve been talking about North and South, the United States isn’t really divided into two parts. It’s divided into three, because there’s also the West—or, as Americans call it, the West.

First, there’s the North, with Puritan New England at its heart—Connecticut and Massachusetts—and the great metropolis of New York. The North is a world of commerce and ports, of business and industry. Its population is growing at a tremendous rate, and commercial and industrial capitalism dominate its economy.

The South is agricultural. It grows cotton and then sends it North because it doesn’t even have good ports of its own. Cotton is what the South lives on. An elite of planters controls the region, and its economy is built on slavery.

Then there’s the West, because much of the American continent still remains to be colonized. In the eyes of white people, the land belongs to no one. We know, of course, that countless Indian nations live there—to use the older term—or Indigenous, Native nations. But to white Americans, it’s as if no one is there. This is a world to be conquered.

The West they’re conquering at this point isn’t yet the Far West of cowboys. It’s what we now call the Midwest, a land of settlers and farmers, where new arrivals believe there’s enough land for everyone. Settlers obtain or claim Western land through several federal and state systems, usually by purchasing it or asserting the right to buy it before others. The federal Homestead Act, which will offer free homesteads, doesn’t arrive until 1862. [N-007]

Naturally, this is also an egalitarian society—the most egalitarian in America. It’s individualistic, and it’s made up of farmers. And that explains why the West and the South remain allies for so long. Fundamentally, they share the same interests. They want to protect agriculture, not industry, and they both want the country to expand.

Territorial expansion means the United States must find room for the flood of immigrants arriving in the country. It must keep moving west, and the Southern planters are just as eager to expand in that direction. The West is vast, but beyond it lies the whole Southwest.

Take the war with Mexico in 1848, which we remember almost nothing about. Who knows that the United States goes to war with Mexico in 1848? At most, we remember the Alamo or Davy Crockett from some Hollywood movie. But the war is an epoch-making event. By defeating Mexico, the United States confirms the Texas boundary and takes possession of immense territories, including New Mexico and California. Texas itself had already been annexed and admitted to the United States in 1845. [N-008]

The Southern planters and Western settlers want that war. They’re allies, represented in politics by a party that still exists today: the Democratic Party. It may seem strange to us that the party of the Southern planters is the Democratic Party. But people my age remember that even in the 1960s, the South still votes Democratic. Then, with Reagan, it begins voting Republican, and now it’s the stronghold of the Republicans and of Trump. But for a very long time, Southerners simply assume that the Democratic Party is their party.

In the nineteenth century, the Democrats are the party of the South. They’re also the party of the Western settlers and small landowners. By the middle of the century, though, that coalition is coming under threat. Against this alliance of farmers and planters, the industrialists of the North are gaining more and more power, and they want very different policies from the ones that benefit agriculture.
