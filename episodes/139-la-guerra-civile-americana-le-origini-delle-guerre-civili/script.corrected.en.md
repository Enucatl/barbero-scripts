## 1. Lincoln’s Assassination and the Civil War Question

Thank you. So, imagine that you are in Washington on 14 April 1865. Five days have passed since General Robert E. Lee, the commander of the Confederate army, surrendered at Appomattox, bringing the American Civil War to an end. President Lincoln is at the theatre, watching a comedy. While the performance is under way, an actor enters the theatre, a very famous stage actor, so famous that nobody checks him, nobody stops him, because he is at home there. He is very young, only twenty-seven years old, but for several years he has been one of America’s most famous actors, in an age, you understand, when theatre occupied the same place that television and cinema occupy today, and probably something more besides. For years, newspapers have been praising this actor; they call him the handsomest man in America. His name is John Wilkes Booth, and he is a sympathizer of the South, which has just lost the Civil War. And he has never concealed his hatred for President Lincoln, whom he sees precisely as the cause of all the South’s troubles. He was once even arrested for the speeches he made around the country against the government. John Wilkes Booth, the handsomest man in America, enters the presidential box without anybody checking him—different times, as you can see—takes out his pistol, and shoots the President in the back of the head. Lincoln’s guest, Major Henry Rathbone, tries to stop Booth; Booth stabs Rathbone and escapes, convinced that he is like Brutus, who killed Julius Caesar to defend liberty. [N-001] He will die two weeks later in a shootout with the cavalry that has followed him in his flight. Lincoln is the first President of the United States to die in an assassination attempt. And as you know, he will not be the last. After him, another three Presidents of the United States will be killed while in office; the last is John Kennedy, naturally. Lincoln’s death is a turning point in the conclusion of a civil war whose traditional estimate is about 620,000 military deaths, though newer demographic estimates are higher; comparisons with twentieth-century wars depend on which conflicts and casualty definitions are counted. [N-002] A war that had been triggered precisely by Lincoln’s election as President of the United States, in the elections of November 1860. Lincoln, the Republican candidate—but we will soon see that Democrats and Republicans, although they already existed then, did not cover the same electoral spectrum that they cover today—had been elected with 40 per cent of the popular vote, therefore a relative majority, also because at that time it was not customary, as it is now, to have only two candidates; there were more. In the 1860 election there are four candidates. Lincoln has a relative majority; he wins throughout the North, and in most of the slave states of the South he does not get even a single vote. A divided country, then. Today in the United States, the division of the electorate between those who vote for Trump and those who vote for Biden often evokes civil-war scenarios. Anyone who has American acquaintances knows that they really believe the country is almost in danger of going through such an experience again, it is so divided today. In reality, when one studies the origins of the American Civil War, one realizes, first of all, that for a country really to plunge into civil war, it is not enough, it is not enough for there to be a division in public opinion, for there to be even a substantial percentage of the population opposed to the system; it is not enough. Civil war broke out in nineteenth-century America because two different models of development, of economy, of society confronted one another in the country, each rooted in one part of the United States. And each of these two opposing models was so deeply rooted that in reality everybody believed in it—not just, precisely, the poor wretches who felt excluded, but politicians, intellectuals, the rich, ideologues. So what was it that, in the nineteenth century, divided the United States so profoundly as to make it plunge into civil war? And what were the United States in the middle of the nineteenth century? ## 2. America in 1860: Growth and the North-South Divide

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00086–U-00198 -->

In 1860 the United States had 31,000,000 inhabitants. Today we might laugh at that, today they have 300,000,000, but for the middle of the nineteenth century it was a very substantial population. 31,000,000 inhabitants meant that the United States had more inhabitants than Great Britain, though France’s population was nearly six million larger, and I am talking about the two greatest world powers, the two countries at the forefront in Europe that already had vast colonial empires in the rest of the world, so the United States was potentially already one of the great world powers. [N-003] But the most impressive thing is this: in 1860 they had 31,000,000 inhabitants, but ten years earlier they had only 23,000,000. Which means that in one decade the population had grown by a third. The country was growing enormously, filling up with immigrants, and experiencing colossal industrial growth. The central decades of the nineteenth century, as you know, were decades of overwhelming industrial growth in the countries at the forefront—in Great Britain, Germany, France, the United States. In that decade, 1850–60, the value of manufacturing output in the United States rose by about 85 percent. When one talks about figures, it is good to stop for a moment and reflect: you know how we struggle with 0.5 percent a year? One percent annual growth? Well, here we are talking about an increase of about 85 percent over the course of ten years. For the moment, it was still a country in which farmers outnumbered city dwellers, but the overtaking was already about to come. In 1860 there were about 49,000 kilometres of functioning railways in the United States, plus those under construction: about 49,000 kilometres. In all of Italy, on the same date, 1,800 kilometres. [N-004] What is more, what is more, it was a society unlike any other in the world, because by 1860 most states had broadly enfranchised white men, although voting rules remained state-based and excluded women, most Black people, and others. Yet there was a women’s movement demanding equal rights and the vote for women too. White literacy was about 90 percent among free white adults under the census measure. Wages were comparatively high, but wealth inequality remained substantial; whereas today the United States, as we know, is among Western countries the one where inequality between the super-rich and the poor is greatest, even though we in Italy are rapidly catching up with them. The literacy rate was extremely high. In Italy, on the same date, 20 percent. United States, 90. Everyone read the newspaper. [N-005]

Now, this overall picture was true of the whole country, but only up to a point: the talk about democracy, egalitarianism, literacy applied throughout the country, even though perhaps in the North these factors were even more pronounced than in the South. But the enormous industrial development, immigration, the mass of people arriving, development and constant transformation characterized only the North. The North had three quarters of the inhabitants, but almost all the factories, almost all the workers. The South was rich, but it was an entirely agricultural country, where wealth consisted of plantations and slaves, you understand? 4,000,000 slaves were an immense capital asset. The South owned 4,000,000 slaves, so on paper it possessed colossal wealth, and it was fabulously rich thanks to the profits from cotton, which had by then become a monoculture in the South. There was still some tobacco in Virginia, but whereas the South a century earlier had been a country where tobacco, sugar, rice were produced, in 1860 the South was the country of cotton, and it was the country of cotton because the Industrial Revolution was under way in England. The mills of Manchester consumed a mountain of cotton every year, and this cotton came from the Southern states. Thanks to the capital tied up in 4,000,000 slaves and the enormous profits from cotton, the South was fabulously rich, but it was a stagnant economy that was not growing, was not transforming itself. The North, on the other hand, was undergoing an extremely profound transformation.

And yet until the middle of the century, the South led politically, directed politically. The elite of the great landowners, the Southern planters, had dominated the politics of the United States. Lincoln was the sixteenth president. Of the fifteen presidents before Lincoln, nine were born in the Southern states, and all nine had at some point held people in slavery. You understand? Until Lincoln’s election, a little more than half of the presidents of the United States had personally held people in slavery. And even those who came from the North had always been elected on the basis of electoral agreements with the Southern planters. There had never been a president who opposed the interests of the South. [N-006]

## 3. Three Regions and Westward Expansion

And this political hegemony of the South, in reality, how shall I put it, is explained by the fact that although so far I have spoken of North and South, in reality the United States was not divided into two; it was divided into three, because there was also the West, or as they called it, the West. The North, the North with Puritan New England at its heart, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and with the great metropolis of New York. The North is commercial, port-based, business-minded, industrial, in tumultuous demographic growth, dominated by commercial and industrial capitalism. The South is agricultural, it produces cotton, then sends it to the North because it does not even have good ports. The South in fact lives off that, it is in the hands of an elite of planters and lives on an economy based on slavery. And then there is the West, because the American continent is still in large part to be colonized. In the eyes of whites, it belongs to nobody. We know that innumerable Indian nations live there, to use the term once used, indigenous, native nations. In the eyes of whites, it is as though nobody were there. It is a world to conquer. The West they are conquering in those years is not yet the Far West of cowboys; it is what today is called the Middle West, a country of settlers, of farmers who arrive there and there is land for everyone. Settlers acquired or claimed western land under several federal and state systems, commonly involving purchase or pre-emption; the federal Homestead Act providing free homesteads came only in 1862. [N-007] Obviously, this too is an egalitarian society, the most egalitarian in America; it is an individualistic society and it is a peasant society. The fact that the West is a world of farmers explains why for a long time the West and the South were allied, because fundamentally they had the same interests: they were interested in defending agriculture and not industrial interests, and then they were interested in territorial expansion. Territorial expansion means that America and the United States must find room for the flood of immigrants who are arriving; they must expand westward, and the Southern planters too have a great desire to expand westward. The West, the West is large, but then there is the whole Southwest. The war against Mexico in 1848, of which we remember nothing. Who knows that the United States went to war against Mexico in 1848? At most we remember Fort Alamo, Davy Crockett from some Hollywood film. The war of 1848 is an epoch-making moment in which the United States, by defeating Mexico, confirms the Texas boundary and takes possession of immense territories: New Mexico, California. Texas had already been annexed and admitted to the United States in 1845. [N-008] And that war was wanted by the Southern planters and the Western settlers, allied and represented in politics by a party that still exists today, the Democratic Party. It may seem strange to us that the party of the Southern Planters was the Democratic Party. In reality, those who are my age remember that even in the 1960s the South voted Democratic. Then with Reagan it began voting Republican and now it is the stronghold of the Republicans and of Trump, but for a long time in the South they took it for granted that their party was the Democratic Party. In the nineteenth century the party of the South was the Democratic Party, and it was also the party of the settlers, the small landowners of the West. Except that this coalition, in the middle of the nineteenth century, begins to be threatened, because in the face of the coalition of farmers and planters, the industrialists of the North count for more and more, and they want policies different from those that suit agriculture.

## 4. Tariffs, Nullification, and State Sovereignty

The first moment when people realize that it is impossible to have an economic policy that suits everyone, and that therefore they will inevitably have to move towards a clash—a political clash, of course; at first nobody is thinking of anything else—but political confrontation becomes inevitable when the industrialists of the North demand, as happens today too, you know, tariffs against China? Well, tariffs suit American industry. We want goods coming from England to cost too much; our inhabitants must buy our stuff, our industrial production, so tariffs. The Southern planters do not want tariffs; they live off exports, they live by exporting cotton. Woe betide them if the English get angry and start imposing tariffs on American cotton. Maybe that seems like little to you; it is an economic conflict, there are such conflicts all the time in advanced countries, and yet in that economic conflict one begins to glimpse the basis for a dramatic split in the United States. Because what happens is that every time the industrialists' lobby in Washington tries to obtain a policy favourable to them, customs tariffs, in the South somebody begins to say that they do not care if the government in Washington decides these things, because the individual states are not at all obliged to do what Washington says. The United States are a union of free states, each of which joined voluntarily and is not obliged to respect federal laws. Do you see? I mean, we live in the Europe of Brexit; you understand how great the problem is of establishing how far you can go, how far you can push things once you have decided to enter a union. In the South, the argument is: we do not want laws contrary to our interests; if you make them, we will not apply them. In 1832, there are still thirty years to go before Lincoln's election; it is a long story, as you can see, this story of the origins of the Civil War. In 1832 Washington passes a law imposing higher customs tariffs, and one of the Southern states, the most extremist state in the South, one of the very few where black slaves are actually more numerous than white inhabitants—it is the same state that will later be the most extremist and the first to declare secession thirty years later, South Carolina. In 1832, faced with a federal law imposing new customs tariffs, South Carolina declares that, as far as South Carolina is concerned, that law is null. The State of South Carolina is free to nullify, as they put it, the laws of Washington. An extremely violent political debate opens up, which has gone down in history precisely as the debate over nullification. And there, for the first time, people begin to discuss what the United States really are. One of the great American politicians of the time enters the scene, a figure whom we in Italy today know very little about, naturally; he will die before the Civil War. He is one of the great politicians representing Southern interests; his name is Calhoun, John Calhoun. He is a very profound intellectual, a defender of slavery as the only system by which the South can live. And in the debate over nullification, John Calhoun, speaking for the whole South, declares that even though we have joined together, each individual state retains its full sovereignty. The states are sovereign, and the federal government in Washington is merely their agent, and there is no obligation to obey the government in Washington, because that is something one might do in nation-states, where the national interest is one, and the United States are not the same thing. In a memoir published decades later, Oliver Dyer recalled Calhoun as saying: “I never use the word Nation in speaking of the United States; I always use the word Union, or Confederacy. We are not a nation, but a Union, a confederacy of equal and sovereign States. England is a nation, Austria is a nation, Russia is a nation, but the United States are not a nation.” [N-034] Faced with this position, the ideologues of the North instead argue that the United States are a nation and that the laws of a nation must be the same for everyone, do you see? Today it is easy, I think, to see how Calhoun's argument, in its lucidity, ran counter to the spirit of the age, because in the nineteenth century—and we Italians know something about this—the spirit of the age was the nation, the union of the nation, the sacred interests of the nation. To say we are not a nation was an extraordinarily unsettling argument, but also, but also one that would lose out in the long run. And in fact today there are those who say, look, the American Civil War was the moment when this question was resolved, when the United States were transformed by force into a nation. ## 6. The West Aligns with the North

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00510–U-00621 -->

Except that, in the course of this debate, the great political novelty emerges. Whereas until now the Southern planters and the Western settlers had been political allies, well, on this matter of having slaves in the new Western territories, the settlers are not really in agreement at all. Because in fact, precisely, there is a hunger for land, everyone wants these new territories, and the Western settlers are small farmers; they do not have slaves, they do not need them. They begin to think: why should we listen to the Southern planters and introduce slavery into these new territories? And then there is the fact that the West, in the tumultuous development of the American economy, in those years, the 1840s and 1850s, is becoming increasingly economically tied to the North. Think of the railways: railways in the United States all run from east to west, they are like seams linking the East to the West, and therefore the North of industries and ports to the agricultural Middle West. By now, thanks to the railways, the grain and meat of the West no longer go to New Orleans, the only Southern port that could be reached by floating down the Mississippi on barges. Now the products of the West go east, to Boston, to New York, by rail. Then there is, how shall I put it, the fact that the industrial North is now producing machines, agricultural machinery, and agricultural machinery is of no use in the South because the work on the cotton plantations is done by slaves by hand, and no one feels like investing in mechanization; whereas the independent small farmers of the West certainly do buy the new automatic threshing machines. And in the Middle West new cities arise which become metropolises out of nothing: Chicago. Think of Chicago, which in a few decades becomes a metropolis with its slaughterhouses, with its canned-meat factories. Al Capone’s empire, sixty years later, will still be based on that: control of the slaughterhouses, of industry, of processing. And all this acts as a flywheel, precisely, between the agricultural West and the industries and ports of the North. And the South of cotton and slaves increasingly feels economically like an area isolated from the rest of the country.

And so you see, if someone says, as one increasingly hears discussed and said today, but is it really slavery, the American Civil War? Was slavery the problem? Yes, it is true, of course it was. But we have to understand what that means. Slavery was the reason the United States split apart, and the clearest minds had foreseen it long before. As early as 1832, Tocqueville, you know? The great French traveller who wrote extraordinarily lucid books about the United States, who travelled across it from one side to the other. Tocqueville, thirty years before the Civil War, already wrote: “The most formidable of all the evils that threaten the future of the United States arises from the presence of Blacks on their soil.” And so slavery is the reason, but be careful, because we must not turn this into, how shall I put it, a moralistic or humanitarian narrative, with the good people on one side who want to abolish slavery and the bad people on the other who want to keep it. The question is that this is about the hegemony of one sector or the other. Why had the North abolished slavery? White workers’ hostility to enslaved labor was one factor, but northern abolition reflected varied state laws, antislavery principle, Black and Quaker activism, revolutionary politics, labor interests, and racial exclusion. [N-013] Let us be clear: among the educated elites of the North, as among European elites in the eighteenth century, naturally there was talk of slavery being something unworthy and needing to be abolished; but these were, I repeat, elite discussions that had almost no hold on popular opinion in the North and West. John Adams, for example, wrote—we are at the beginning of the nineteenth century, Massachusetts has abolished slavery—“argument might have some Weight, in the Abolition of Slavery in the Massachusetts, but the real Cause was the multiplication of labouring White People, who, would no longer suffer the Rich to employ these Sable Rivals, so much to their Injury. This Principle has kept Negro Slavery out of France England and other Parts of Europe. The common People would not suffer, the Labour by which alone they could obtain a subsistance to be done by Slaves. If the Gentlemen had been permitted by Law to hold Slaves, the common white People would have put the Negroes to Death and their Masters too perhaps.” This is how the second president of the United States assessed the motives that had led the Northern states to abolish slavery.

Besides, the fact that the motives were not humanitarian is demonstrated by the fact that many Northern states, such as New York, and also almost all the new Western states, had laws denying the vote to free blacks, forbidding them to attend school, barring blacks from serving on juries, prohibiting mixed marriages, and in some cases even prohibiting blacks from residing there. Illinois, Lincoln’s state, had a law prohibiting entry into the state by any free black person who was not already a resident. In other words, we cannot throw out those who are already there, but we do not want any more of them.

[N-018] But officially it has been abolished, and so the price of slaves rises. At the outbreak of the Civil War, a slave easily costs 1,000 dollars at a time when a farm laborer earns 8 dollars a month. The system of slavery, we had been mistaken, was not so archaic after all, it was not so outdated at all, not in the slightest. It is a system that guarantees gigantic profits. And then there is another reason why in the South the talk of abolishing slavery ceases to be heard, and this time it is ideological. And here too one sees how important ideology is, how capable it is of blinding people when, when it is taken too seriously and when it creates divisions between two opposing camps, because earlier Black, Quaker, and gradual-emancipation antislavery traditions long predated the 1830s; a new immediatist abolitionist movement expanded in that decade and demanded immediate, uncompensated abolition. [N-019] That is, the birth of a movement that takes hold in the North and criticizes slavery from a moral point of view, and with growing energy demands the abolition of slavery immediately, without endless discussions, without compensating anyone, because slavery is shameful and those who own slaves have made a pact with the devil and will burn in hell and deserve no respect, and in a Christian country slavery must be abolished. The abolitionist movement is a great movement of opinion that spreads in the North, which never really penetrates the masses very much, but which among the educated elites, starting in the 1830s and ’40s, gains a great deal of ground. Result: in the South they become increasingly frightened. Hearing the tones of abolitionism, they stiffen in the South. For a Southern politician to go on saying, as was normal before, yes, fundamentally slavery will have to be overcome sooner or later, becomes impossible. They simply do not re-elect you. James McDowell, who had supported gradual emancipation in Virginia in 1832, later became governor and stopped raising the issue. John H. Pleasants blamed “the fanatics of the North [who] have taken the most effective means not to leave a single friend of emancipation in the Southern States.” [N-020] Speaking against slavery is political death in the South, I repeat, no one votes for you anymore. And so do you understand what is happening? What is happening is that the Southern states now feel themselves to be an isolated sector of the country, with interests opposed to the rest of the country, and they identify their own interests with slavery. Slavery must be defended at all costs, otherwise our system will end up in the minority and we are finished. And so Southern intellectuals stop speaking ill of slavery and begin to defend it.

## 9. Proslavery Ideology and Sectional Polarization

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00805–U-00959 -->

The debate over slavery in the 1830s, 40s, and 50s takes on this new character. Politicians, ideologues, thinkers, theologians begin to appear; the Protestant preachers of the South are in the front line. Slavery is something sacred, it is in the Bible. Theorists begin to appear who defend slavery with fascinating arguments. For example, do you think the workers in the North are any better off? Why, in the North workers are exploited in an ignoble, shameful way; they break their backs for pennies. We treat our slaves well and they are happy. A quotation from one of the speeches of that time. “Our slaves are black, of another and inferior race. [...] They are happy, content, unaspiring, and utterly incapable, from intellectual weakness, ever to give us any trouble by their aspirations.” Other proslavery writers argued that enslavement brought Africans Christianity and civilization and denied that Black people could govern themselves. [N-021] Everyone in the United States agrees, mind you, that races exist and that races are divided into superior races and inferior races, more advanced races and more backward races, races that may perhaps one day reach our level, but for now have not yet got there. These are the arguments heard in the South: you want to free them? Then you will see anarchy, they will start doing everything, stealing, killing; we will have to exterminate them. Is that what you want? They are so happy as slaves. The remarkable thing is that these arguments gain a great deal of traction in the South even among that majority of the white population who do not own slaves. Because those who own slaves are a large minority of Southern whites, but the majority do not own slaves. And yet the supporters of the system actually manage to present it in such a way that even those who are not personally interested, because they do not own slaves, still feel that slavery is an advantage for them. There are those who explain very seriously—and incidentally note the intellectual effort deployed in this cause to defend slavery. They say: in every society there must be people who do the hardest, heaviest jobs, and in every society they are people who are poorly paid and live worse than others. And where we are, no white person does hard, heavy work and lives badly. Where we are, whites are all free and all equal. That is democracy. We have democracy, where whites are all equal, and this is because, precisely, we have another race that does the dirty work. And so they achieve the masterpiece of convincing poor whites that slavery is the guarantee of the American dream, of freedom, of equality. And for the masters this means that the South is safe from that other nightmare that is spreading across the world in the middle of the nineteenth century, namely class struggle. In Europe, for years now people have been talking about exploited, dissatisfied workers, strikes, threats of revolution, the spectre of communism, which Marx and Engels will say in 1848, precisely, is haunting Europe. In the South the problem does not exist: whites are all happy and satisfied, they support the system. In a Boston lecture in January 1856, more than five years before Fort Sumter, Robert Toombs described conditions in the South: “Stability, progress, order, peace, content, prosperity, reign throughout our borders. Not a single soldier is to be found in our widely-extended domain to overawe or protect society. The desire for organic change nowhere manifests itself.” [N-022] The South feels strong, and optimism gives rise to an extremist minority that wants ever more. Have we defeated Mexico? Now it is time to conquer Cuba, to expand into South America, new territories to open up to plantations and slavery. There is even a movement to reopen the slave trade with Africa, which Jefferson had abolished fifty years earlier. At the end of the 1850s, Maxcy Gregg was an advocate of reopening the transatlantic slave trade during the late-1850s agitation in South Carolina. He too will later be a Southern general, killed at the Battle of Fredericksburg. [N-023] So the South feels strong and is prepared to clash in order to defend its interests. It feels strong above all because cotton seems an absolute guarantee. European industry needs Southern cotton. In 1858, Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina, a former governor of the state, declared: “No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king.” [N-024] This is the slogan that Southern newspapers and politicians keep repeating. In turn, public opinion in the North begins to be alarmed. What do these slaveholders want? What is this all about? Do they really want to rule the whole United States? Do these slave masters want to come and rule in our homes too, by any chance? Concern about the conspiracy of the slaveholders, about slave power, spreads through Northern public opinion. SLAVE POWER. We all know, don’t we, how forcefully newspapers impose certain labels on public discourse that we then all repeat without thinking about them anymore, don’t we? Sovereigntism, populism—there it is, slave power. Once you have created it, it exists: people in the North begin to be afraid, the Southern planters are an arrogant power group plotting to dominate the United States. And all this, precisely, shapes public opinion and accustoms Northerners and Southerners to considering themselves two different nations. For Southerners, the North means disorder, foreign immigration, smoky cities, working-class misery, hypocritical Puritanism, the brute rule of money. For Northerners, the South is backwardness, laziness, immorality, a ball and chain on the progress of the United States. Even the Protestant churches split. The churches—the Baptists, the Methodists, who are strongest in mass public opinion. Well, that is, there are Northern Baptists and Southern Baptists. Those in the North demand the abolition of slavery on the basis of the Bible, and those in the South declare that slavery is perfectly just on the basis of the Bible. And in the South, as they gradually realize that the conflict of interests can no longer be patched up, they begin to think about Plan B. That is, if these people become too influential in Washington and we find ourselves in the minority, we leave, we secede. Those arguments John Calhoun had made years earlier become relevant again, namely: we are not a nation, we are a free union of States. This issue of states’ rights, you understand, is politically crucial. In the South the argument is: South Carolina or Virginia are sovereign States that agreed to enter this union and can leave it whenever they wish.

## 10. The 1850 Compromise and Northern Radicalization

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01018 -->

In 1850, then, they are discussing the new state of California and whether or not to allow slavery in California. For the first time—there are ten years left until Lincoln’s election—the Nashville Convention brings delegates from several Southern states into open discussion of conditional disunion. [N-025] If Washington’s policy does not go in the direction we want, what can we do? And there, for the first time, quite a few people can be heard saying, precisely, we leave. But then in reality, you understand. There is a long way to go between the moment when you begin to say it and the moment when you seriously think about it. In the Senate they debate and seek a compromise. Old John Calhoun, who is still alive but will die very soon, contemplates peaceful separation if no settlement can be reached. The Senate is alarmed; even this is too much. They seek a watered-down compromise. The watered-down compromise is this: there will be no slavery in California. The South has to swallow this bitter pill; in exchange it obtains something that seems fundamental. The federal authorities undertake to hunt down fugitive slaves who escape from the South and reach the North. It is a widespread phenomenon but not a mass one, yet it is a problem that exists, and until then the slave who managed to reach a free state disappeared. Now the federal government undertakes to hunt him down. The Fugitive Slave Act empowers federal marshals, commissioners, deputies, and compels local assistance to seize alleged fugitives and return them to enslavement. [N-026] This makes a terrible impression on Northern public opinion. Seeing, in one’s own home, that the slaveholders are able to mobilize the federal authorities to hunt down their slaves—well then, there really is a slave power, then these people really do want to come and rule in our own homes? The abolitionist movement spreads. More and more people reflect on the immorality of slavery and decide to commit themselves seriously to helping, to helping the slaves who escape. Underground organizations arise to help them; it is called the underground railroad, and in 1852 a book comes out that immediately becomes famous, entitled *Uncle Tom’s Cabin, or Life Among the Humble*, which portrays, precisely, in a very sentimental way, and the specialists say in a rather imaginary way, yet precisely, the life of slaves on Southern plantations; it rapidly becomes a bestseller in the North. And so, even though Northern public opinion continues to have no particular sympathy for Black people and in general nobody wants Black people as next-door neighbors, still, still in the North too the idea begins to spread, precisely, that perhaps slavery really is a wrong thing and these Southerners really are immoral if they want to go on keeping slaves.

## 11. Bleeding Kansas and the Dred Scott Decision

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-01019–U-01129 -->

And in the middle of the decade, 1855, 56, a new tragedy breaks out, the tragedy of Kansas. Kansas is a new territory in the West, north of the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′, whose restriction on slavery the Kansas-Nebraska Act had repealed, and so in the North they take it for granted that there will be no slavery in Kansas. [N-027] The government, influenced by Southern politicians, instead decides that whether or not there will be slavery in Kansas will be decided by its inhabitants. Both the North and the South begin urging people to emigrate to Kansas in order to secure a majority. Settlers arrive from the South to support slavery; settlers arrive from the North to support abolition. We are in the West, everyone goes around with a gun. Violence begins. On 21 May 1856, a band of Southerners attacks a small town inhabited by Northerners, Lawrence, loots it, destroys the printing works of the local newspapers, which were, precisely, abolitionist. Four days later, on 25 May, the Northerners respond with what has gone down in history as the Pottawatomie massacre. A band of abolitionists turns up at night, precisely, in this area by the Pottawatomie River, at the house of a Southern settler; they take the father and two sons, bring them into the woods, kill them with sword blows; then they go to the house of a second settler, take him outside, kill him with knife blows; then they go to the cabin of a third settler, where they find the owner and three guests, interrogate them to find out who they are, then take one of them, bring him into the woods, and kill him. The leader of the band, regrettably, is a figure we have all heard of, his name is John Brown. He is the celebrated abolitionist leader, the protagonist of a song which, in my day, we all knew from childhood, and the others in the band are his sons, his friends—John Brown, Captain John Brown, as he calls himself, a man from the North, and a deeply convinced religious abolitionist, convinced that slavery is absolute evil, that it must be destroyed, and that slaveholders must be annihilated; he made his sons swear to fight to the death to abolish slavery, and he is convinced that he can make the slaves rise up. Now he is operating in Kansas. Before long we will find him somewhere else, and by then we will truly be at the end of our, our overview. A case like that of Kansas obviously inflames public opinion on both sides. As you can well imagine, everyone sees only the violence of the others. And consequently, throughout the country, people speak of bleeding Kansas, Bleeding Kansas. But for those in the North it means that the slaveholders are killing people, and for those in the South it means that the damned abolitionists are killing our people. And in 1857, as you can see we are getting closer, a legal case breaks out, the Dred Scott case. Dred Scott is an old Black slave, who for a long time was the slave of an officer, a military doctor, and in the wake of his master he stayed for a long time in various states of the country, of the Union, including states where slavery was forbidden. After his master's death, Dred Scott sues the widow, his mistress, maintaining that he must be considered free, because he can prove that for several years he lived in states where slavery was forbidden. The Dred Scott case is obviously an enormous hot potato, so it is bounced from one court to another until it reaches the Supreme Court of the United States. As you know, the Supreme Court, still today, is a judicial body of immense prestige and enormous political weight. The Supreme Court examines the Dred Scott case and then, by a majority, hands down an explosive ruling. That is, it declares, first, that Dred Scott is a slave and, not being a citizen, has no right to turn to federal justice or to sue anyone. Second, and this is even more important—although it may not seem so to us, it is even more important—it is true that in the old days there were states where slavery had been forbidden by an act of Congress, but according to the Supreme Court, Congress cannot prohibit slavery in the federal territories, invalidating the Missouri Compromise restriction; it does not deny a state’s authority to prohibit slavery. [N-028] You understand what this means? It means establishing, precisely, definitively that the government in Washington has no right to forbid slavery in the territories. And this ruling too, obviously, is explosive and provokes immense debate, and then in 1859 we reach the final episode. I am making this very long, as you can see, but you also understand that to sum up a story like this in an hour—well, we are moving, so to speak, toward wrapping things up. Thank you.

## 12. John Brown, Republicans, and Lincoln’s Campaign

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01130–U-01243 -->

In 1859 there is one last dramatic episode, and once again the protagonist is John Brown. John Brown has left Kansas and now intends to get serious, to unleash a slave revolt in the South. So he gathers a band and plans to seize a military arsenal in Virginia, Harpers Ferry, and distribute weapons to Black people. So in the summer of 1859 John Brown rents a farm near Harpers Ferry and then, little by little, has his men come there. When he has about twenty armed men, on 16 October 1859 he seizes the arsenal, which is virtually undefended, and begins sending messengers around, free Negroes—I say Negroes because after reading the sources of that period so much it becomes almost impossible not to use the words they used, even though we have become accustomed, so to speak, to being careful about the words we use—and these free Black men go around the plantations trying to persuade the slaves to rise up; nobody moves. The militia arrives; President Buchanan sends the Marines from Washington, under the command of a colonel who will become famous: Colonel Robert E. Lee, the very man who shortly afterwards will become commander of the Southern army. The Marines have a firefight; most of John Brown’s men are killed, John Brown is captured, tried, and sentenced to death. He is hanged on 2 December 1859. And he leaves, leaves a few lines in which he declares: “I John Brown am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land: will never be purged away; but with Blood. I had as I now think: vainly flattered myself that without very much bloodshed; it might be done.” John Brown’s death creates a myth, that myth which, indeed, still exists today. In the North, on the day John Brown is hanged, bells ring in his honor, cannon salutes are fired in his honor, the song is born. And the following year, 1860, there are elections. And at the elections a new party appears, which wants to challenge the hegemony of the Democratic Party. A new party that takes note of the fact that it is no longer certain that the West is allied with the South; by now the West has too many interests in common with the North, and so this alliance can be broken. The new party proposes, precisely, to bring together the interests of Northern industrialists and Western settlers and to put the slaveholding South in the minority. And the party presents itself as progressive, egalitarian, and takes the name Republican Party. As you can see, it is a paradox: the Republican Party and the Democratic Party still exist today, and today they have, roughly speaking, political positions, so to speak, reversed from those they had at that time. The Republican Party goes into the election with excellent prospects, because the South and the West really no longer agree, and so the Democratic Party splits, goes into the election with two opposing candidates; in addition there is an independent, and so the Republican Party, let us say, holds all the cards. To be sure of winning, they must convince the West, and so they choose as candidate a man from the West: Lincoln. A lawyer, a seasoned politician, who has already had political experience in his Illinois, but also in Washington, and yet, although he is a professional politician, he is someone on whom one can also stitch the image of the frontier man, the son of a settler raised in a log cabin, the man who understands you and represents you. Lincoln is a moderate candidate. The Republican platform for his candidacy is extremely moderate with regard to the problem of slavery, because the party knows perfectly well that extreme abolitionism, although it is gaining ground, is still not a mass phenomenon and that abolitionist ideas have not yet penetrated the masses. Just to give you an example: Kansas, Bleeding Kansas, where people killed one another, those who wanted slavery and those who wanted to abolish it. Then those who do not want slavery in Kansas win. The antislavery Wyandotte Constitution prohibits slavery and restricts suffrage to white male citizens, but it does not bar free Black people from residing in Kansas; the proposed exclusion clause is defeated. [N-029] Because not wanting slavery really means, we do not want the competition of Black people. And Lincoln, Lincoln is also chosen because they hope he will not frighten the South too much, because he has never been an abolitionist. He is someone who, so to speak, is aware that the problem will sooner or later have to be addressed, but does not hesitate to declare that he does not know how to do it. I quote from a speech by Lincoln: “If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do, as to the existing institution. My first impulse would be to free all the slaves, and send them to Liberia,---to their own native land. But a moment’s reflection would convince me, that whatever of high hope, as I think there is, there may be in this, in the long run, its sudden execution is impossible. [...] What next? Free them, and make them politically and socially, our equals? My own feelings will not admit of this; and if mine would, we well know that those of the great mass of white people will not.” Thus the politician, Lincoln. Therefore, in his platform Lincoln declares that he does not intend to meddle in slavery and that every state is sovereign and can do what it wants with regard to slavery. But at the same time the Republican platform makes clear that the Southerners must stop talking about states’ rights. Secession? No: the federal government is superior to the individual states. No state has the right to nullify federal laws; talking about secession is treason. From an economic point of view, Lincoln’s platform is what Northern industrialists want: large investments, railways westward, and so on. The solid South does not want Lincoln. And Southern politicians present Lincoln to their voters as the devil, the abolitionist, even as a follower of John Brown, and you can imagine how popular John Brown was in the South at that moment.
