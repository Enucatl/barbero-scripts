Because the climate has changed? One cause is economic, and I must say that it shows us the power of the economy, that is, the power with which economic factors can shape the collective mentality, public opinion. Because the fact is that at the beginning of the nineteenth century the Southern economy is not so rich, yes, sugar cane, tobacco, cotton, but profits are modest and many can say it is not an economic system that has a future. Instead, the industrial revolution changes everything. With the industrial revolution, I told you before, but now it takes on another meaning, you understand the point, with the industrial revolution cotton production by means of slaves proves to be a way of making a great deal of money. And so there are fewer and fewer people saying no, slavery is an archaic system that must be overcome, no, no, figures are figures, slavery produces enormous profits. One result, among other things, is that the value, the price of slaves rises vertiginously; moreover, the slave trade having been banned, even though secretly the trade goes on all the same, illegal importation continues after the federal ban, with documented links to New York and other northern commercial networks. [N-018] But officially it has been abolished, and so the price of slaves rises. At the outbreak of the Civil War, a slave easily costs 1,000 dollars at a time when a farm laborer earns 8 dollars a month. The system of slavery, we have been mistaken, is not so archaic after all, it is not so outdated at all, not in the slightest. It is a system that guarantees gigantic profits. And then there is another reason why in the South the talk of abolishing slavery ceases to be heard, and this time it is ideological. And here too one sees how important ideology is, how capable it is of blinding people when, when it is taken too seriously and when it creates divisions between two opposing camps, because earlier Black, Quaker, and gradual-emancipation antislavery traditions long predate the 1830s; a new immediatist abolitionist movement expands in that decade and demands immediate, uncompensated abolition. [N-019] That is, the birth of a movement that takes hold in the North and criticizes slavery from a moral point of view, and with growing energy demands the abolition of slavery immediately, without endless discussions, without compensating anyone, because slavery is shameful and those who own slaves have made a pact with the devil and will burn in hell and deserve no respect, and in a Christian country slavery must be abolished. The abolitionist movement is a great movement of opinion that spreads in the North, which never really penetrates the masses very much, but which among the educated elites, starting in the 1830s and ’40s, gains a great deal of ground. Result: in the South they become increasingly frightened. Hearing the tones of abolitionism, they stiffen in the South. For a Southern politician to go on saying, as was normal before, yes, fundamentally slavery will have to be overcome sooner or later, becomes impossible. They simply do not re-elect you. James McDowell, who supports gradual emancipation in Virginia in 1832, later becomes governor and stops raising the issue. John H. Pleasants blames “the fanatics of the North [who] have taken the most effective means not to leave a single friend of emancipation in the Southern States.” [N-020] Speaking against slavery is political death in the South, I repeat, no one votes for you anymore. And so do you understand what is happening? What is happening is that the Southern states now feel themselves to be an isolated sector of the country, with interests opposed to the rest of the country, and they identify their own interests with slavery. Slavery must be defended at all costs, otherwise our system will end up in the minority and we are finished. And so Southern intellectuals stop speaking ill of slavery and begin to defend it.
