## 6. The West Aligns with the North

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00510–U-00621 -->

Except that, in the course of this debate, the great political novelty emerges. Whereas until now the Southern planters and the Western settlers have been political allies, well, on this matter of having slaves in the new Western territories, the settlers are not really in agreement at all. Because in fact, precisely, there is a hunger for land, everyone wants these new territories, and the Western settlers are small farmers; they do not have slaves, they do not need them. They begin to think: why should we listen to the Southern planters and introduce slavery into these new territories? And then there is the fact that the West, in the tumultuous development of the American economy, in those years, the 1840s and 1850s, is becoming increasingly economically tied to the North. Think of the railways: railways in the United States all run from east to west, they are like seams linking the East to the West, and therefore the North of industries and ports to the agricultural Middle West. By now, thanks to the railways, the grain and meat of the West no longer go to New Orleans, the only Southern port that can be reached by floating down the Mississippi on barges. Now the products of the West go east, to Boston, to New York, by rail. Then there is, how shall I put it, the fact that the industrial North is now producing machines, agricultural machinery, and agricultural machinery is of no use in the South because the work on the cotton plantations is done by slaves by hand, and no one feels like investing in mechanization; whereas the independent small farmers of the West certainly do buy the new automatic threshing machines. And in the Middle West new cities arise which become metropolises out of nothing: Chicago. Think of Chicago, which in a few decades becomes a metropolis with its slaughterhouses, with its canned-meat factories. Al Capone’s empire, sixty years later, will still be based on that: control of the slaughterhouses, of industry, of processing. And all this acts as a flywheel, precisely, between the agricultural West and the industries and ports of the North. And the South of cotton and slaves increasingly feels economically like an area isolated from the rest of the country.

And so you see, if someone says, as one increasingly hears discussed and said today, but is it really slavery, the American Civil War? Is slavery the problem? Yes, it is true, of course it is. But we have to understand what that means. Slavery is the reason the United States splits apart, and the clearest minds foresee it long before. As early as 1832, Tocqueville, you know? The great French traveller who writes extraordinarily lucid books about the United States, who travels across it from one side to the other. Tocqueville, thirty years before the Civil War, already writes: “The most formidable of all the evils that threaten the future of the United States arises from the presence of Blacks on their soil.” And so slavery is the reason, but be careful, because we must not turn this into, how shall I put it, a moralistic or humanitarian narrative, with the good people on one side who want to abolish slavery and the bad people on the other who want to keep it. The question is that this is about the hegemony of one sector or the other. Why has the North abolished slavery? White workers’ hostility to enslaved labor is one factor, but northern abolition reflects varied state laws, antislavery principle, Black and Quaker activism, revolutionary politics, labor interests, and racial exclusion. [N-013] Let us be clear: among the educated elites of the North, as among European elites in the eighteenth century, naturally there is talk of slavery being something unworthy and needing to be abolished; but these are, I repeat, elite discussions that have almost no hold on popular opinion in the North and West. John Adams, for example, writes—we are at the beginning of the nineteenth century, Massachusetts has abolished slavery—“argument might have some Weight, in the Abolition of Slavery in the Massachusetts, but the real Cause was the multiplication of labouring White People, who, would no longer suffer the Rich to employ these Sable Rivals, so much to their Injury. This Principle has kept Negro Slavery out of France England and other Parts of Europe. The common People would not suffer, the Labour by which alone they could obtain a subsistance to be done by Slaves. If the Gentlemen had been permitted by Law to hold Slaves, the common white People would have put the Negroes to Death and their Masters too perhaps.” This is how the second president of the United States assesses the motives that have led the Northern states to abolish slavery.

Besides, the fact that the motives are not humanitarian is demonstrated by the fact that many Northern states, such as New York, and also almost all the new Western states, have laws denying the vote to free blacks, forbidding them to attend school, barring blacks from serving on juries, prohibiting mixed marriages, and in some cases even prohibiting blacks from residing there. Illinois, Lincoln’s state, has a law prohibiting entry into the state by any free black person who is not already a resident. In other words, we cannot throw out those who are already there, but we do not want any more of them.
