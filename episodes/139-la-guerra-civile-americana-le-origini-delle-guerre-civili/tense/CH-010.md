## 10. The 1850 Compromise and Northern Radicalization

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01018 -->

In 1850, then, they are discussing the new state of California and whether or not to allow slavery in California. For the first time—there are ten years left until Lincoln’s election—the Nashville Convention brings delegates from several Southern states into open discussion of conditional disunion. [N-025] If Washington’s policy does not go in the direction we want, what can we do? And there, for the first time, quite a few people can be heard saying, precisely, we leave. But then in reality, you understand. There is a long way to go between the moment when you begin to say it and the moment when you seriously think about it. In the Senate they debate and seek a compromise. Old John Calhoun, who is still alive but will die very soon, contemplates peaceful separation if no settlement can be reached. The Senate is alarmed; even this is too much. They seek a watered-down compromise. The watered-down compromise is this: there will be no slavery in California. The South has to swallow this bitter pill; in exchange it obtains something that seems fundamental. The federal authorities undertake to hunt down fugitive slaves who escape from the South and reach the North. It is a widespread phenomenon but not a mass one, yet it is a problem that exists, and until then the slave who managed to reach a free state disappeared. Now the federal government undertakes to hunt him down. The Fugitive Slave Act empowers federal marshals, commissioners, deputies, and compels local assistance to seize alleged fugitives and return them to enslavement. [N-026] This makes a terrible impression on Northern public opinion. Seeing, in one’s own home, that the slaveholders are able to mobilize the federal authorities to hunt down their slaves—well then, there really is a slave power, then these people really do want to come and rule in our own homes? The abolitionist movement spreads. More and more people reflect on the immorality of slavery and decide to commit themselves seriously to helping, to helping the slaves who escape. Underground organizations arise to help them; it is called the underground railroad, and in 1852 a book comes out that immediately becomes famous, entitled *Uncle Tom’s Cabin, or Life Among the Humble*, which portrays, precisely, in a very sentimental way, and the specialists say in a rather imaginary way, yet precisely, the life of slaves on Southern plantations; it rapidly becomes a bestseller in the North. And so, even though Northern public opinion continues to have no particular sympathy for Black people and in general nobody wants Black people as next-door neighbors, still, still in the North too the idea begins to spread, precisely, that perhaps slavery really is a wrong thing and these Southerners really are immoral if they want to go on keeping slaves.
