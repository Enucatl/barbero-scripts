What really blows up this great alliance between the South and the West, instead, is precisely the grand expansion of the United States, which by then, in the 1840s and 1850s, swallows this enormous mouthful of territories conquered from Mexico and continues on, reaches the Pacific Ocean; at the end of the 1840s there is the gold rush in California, for example, right? There you go. And the epic begins no longer of the West, but of the Far West, the more distant West, of the wars with the Plains Indians, of the wagon trains of settlers pushing farther and farther west. You know all these American mythologies, right? Go West, young man, because in the West there is, precisely, the future of a new nation. Now there is a problem. What is the procedure by which the United States expands westward? It works like this: the West is nothing, it belongs to nobody; it belongs to the Indians, if anything, but precisely, people pretend the Indians are not there. It belongs to Mexico, but the Mexicans are nasty and evil, we defeat them, we take what we want. Congress organizes territories and admits states case by case; population growth matters politically, but no automatic settler threshold determines statehood. [N-009] It is by this mechanism that the United States becomes ever more numerous; you know that at the beginning the stars on the Stars and Stripes were 13. Thirteen were the colonies that rebelled against England. Today there are 50 stars on the flag, 50 states. New ones are created one after another. What is the problem? The problem is that the United States is divided between states where slavery is allowed and states where slavery is forbidden. The free-slave divide was not marked by a single line across the continent: the Mason-Dixon Line had eastern significance, while in the western territories the crucial boundary was the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′. [N-010] I say they abolished it in the North because until the Revolution slavery existed throughout the United States, in the North too, in New England too, but in the North it had never really taken root, because in an economy based on trade, shipping, industry, slave labor is not in fact useful, whereas it is useful on the great plantations of the South. We shall see later what the real reasons are why the North has generally abolished slavery; it is not as simple as we might think, but for now let us leave it aside. For now it is enough that you understand clearly that the problem arises when it is necessary to decide whether a new territory, and then a new state, will have slaves or will not have them. And the Southern ruling class pushes for the new territories and new states to admit slavery, because the idea is: this way we too will be able to push as far as there to buy, expand, create new plantations, take our slaves there. If, instead, slavery is forbidden in those states, then only Northern farmers will be able to go and live and work there. For the Southern states it is vital that slavery be introduced in the new states, so much so that, how shall I put it, after Texas declares independence in 1836, Anglo-Texan rebels protect slavery, and Texas enters the United States as a slave state in 1845. Mexico has abolished slavery. [N-011] That war is wanted by the Southern elites, who at that point, the Southern elites, need the new states to be slave states, also because by now it is clear to everyone that there are opposing blocs: the interests of the industrial and commercial states and the interests of the agricultural states with slaves. These interests play themselves out in Washington. And in Washington a crucial part of the political mechanism is the Senate, which, as perhaps or certainly you know, the United States Senate is a marvelous institution, still marvelous today, because the United States Senate is created with no relation whatsoever to the population of the states. Every state sends two senators to Washington, whether they are tiny states with half a million inhabitants or California: each of these states sends two senators to Washington. And so, if there is a bloc of slave states, the slave states must be as numerous as possible. Every new state with slaves means two senators for our bloc; every new state that forbids slavery means two senators who will vote on the other side. The matter produces surreal effects. I will tell you when they discuss it for the first time: 1820, a new state is created in the West, Missouri. There for the first time there is the clash: do we want slaves? No, we do not want them. In the end the Southerners win and Missouri is born as a state that will have slaves. But to give compensation to the North, a new Northern state is created, by cutting off a little piece of Massachusetts: Maine, which in the past had been an autonomous colony, then had become part of Massachusetts, but now they cut it out of Massachusetts, they invent a new state in the North, which obviously forbids slavery, and so they too will have two more senators in Washington representing the interests of the Northern states. During the Mexican-American War, the Wilmot Proviso of 1846 raises whether slavery will be excluded from territory acquired from Mexico. In December 1849 Representative Robert Toombs of Georgia declares: “I do not, then, hesitate to avow before this House and the country, and in the presence of the living God, that if by your legislation you seek to drive us from the territories of California and New Mexico, purchased by the common blood and treasure of the whole people, and to abolish slavery in this District, thereby attempting to fix a national degradation upon half the States of this Confederacy, I am for disunion; and if my physical courage be equal to the maintenance of my convictions of right and duty, I will devote all I am and all I have on earth to its consummation.” The Compromise of 1850 admits California free and organizes Utah and New Mexico without a statutory prohibition of slavery.
