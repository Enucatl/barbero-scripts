## 11. Bleeding Kansas and the Dred Scott Decision

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-01019–U-01129 -->

And in the middle of the decade, 1855, 56, a new tragedy breaks out, the tragedy of Kansas. Kansas is a new territory in the West, north of the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′, whose restriction on slavery the Kansas-Nebraska Act has repealed, and so in the North they take it for granted that there will be no slavery in Kansas. [N-027] The government, influenced by Southern politicians, instead decides that whether or not there will be slavery in Kansas will be decided by its inhabitants. Both the North and the South begin urging people to emigrate to Kansas in order to secure a majority. Settlers arrive from the South to support slavery; settlers arrive from the North to support abolition. We are in the West, everyone goes around with a gun. Violence begins. On 21 May 1856, a band of Southerners attacks a small town inhabited by Northerners, Lawrence, loots it, destroys the printing works of the local newspapers, which are, precisely, abolitionist. Four days later, on 25 May, the Northerners respond with what has gone down in history as the Pottawatomie massacre. A band of abolitionists turns up at night, precisely, in this area by the Pottawatomie River, at the house of a Southern settler; they take the father and two sons, bring them into the woods, kill them with sword blows; then they go to the house of a second settler, take him outside, kill him with knife blows; then they go to the cabin of a third settler, where they find the owner and three guests, interrogate them to find out who they are, then take one of them, bring him into the woods, and kill him. The leader of the band, regrettably, is a figure we have all heard of, his name is John Brown. He is the celebrated abolitionist leader, the protagonist of a song which, in my day, we all knew from childhood, and the others in the band are his sons, his friends—John Brown, Captain John Brown, as he calls himself, a man from the North, and a deeply convinced religious abolitionist, convinced that slavery is absolute evil, that it must be destroyed, and that slaveholders must be annihilated; he has made his sons swear to fight to the death to abolish slavery, and he is convinced that he can make the slaves rise up. Now he is operating in Kansas. Before long we will find him somewhere else, and by then we will truly be at the end of our, our overview. A case like that of Kansas obviously inflames public opinion on both sides. As you can well imagine, everyone sees only the violence of the others. And consequently, throughout the country, people speak of bleeding Kansas, Bleeding Kansas. But for those in the North it means that the slaveholders are killing people, and for those in the South it means that the damned abolitionists are killing our people. And in 1857, as you can see we are getting closer, a legal case breaks out, the Dred Scott case. Dred Scott is an old Black slave, who for a long time has been the slave of an officer, a military doctor, and in the wake of his master he has stayed for a long time in various states of the country, of the Union, including states where slavery is forbidden. After his master's death, Dred Scott sues the widow, his mistress, maintaining that he must be considered free, because he can prove that for several years he has lived in states where slavery is forbidden. The Dred Scott case is obviously an enormous hot potato, so it is bounced from one court to another until it reaches the Supreme Court of the United States. As you know, the Supreme Court, still today, is a judicial body of immense prestige and enormous political weight. The Supreme Court examines the Dred Scott case and then, by a majority, hands down an explosive ruling. That is, it declares, first, that Dred Scott is a slave and, not being a citizen, has no right to turn to federal justice or to sue anyone. Second, and this is even more important—although it may not seem so to us, it is even more important—it is true that in the old days there were states where slavery had been forbidden by an act of Congress, but according to the Supreme Court, Congress cannot prohibit slavery in the federal territories, invalidating the Missouri Compromise restriction; it does not deny a state’s authority to prohibit slavery. [N-028] You understand what this means? It means establishing, precisely, definitively that the government in Washington has no right to forbid slavery in the territories. And this ruling too, obviously, is explosive and provokes immense debate, and then in 1859 we reach the final episode. I am making this very long, as you can see, but you also understand that to sum up a story like this in an hour—well, we are moving, so to speak, toward wrapping things up. Thank you.
