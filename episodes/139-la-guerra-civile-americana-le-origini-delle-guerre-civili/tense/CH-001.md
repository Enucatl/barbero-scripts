## 1. Lincoln’s Assassination and the Civil War Question

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00085 -->

Thank you. So, imagine that you are in Washington on 14 April 1865. Five days have passed since General Robert E. Lee, the commander of the Confederate army, surrendered at Appomattox, bringing the American Civil War to an end. President Lincoln is at the theatre, watching a comedy. While the performance is under way, an actor enters the theatre, a very famous stage actor, so famous that nobody checks him, nobody stops him, because he is at home there. He is very young, only twenty-seven years old, but for several years he has been one of America’s most famous actors, in an age, you understand, when theatre occupied the same place that television and cinema occupy today, and probably something more besides. For years, newspapers have been praising this actor; they call him the handsomest man in America. His name is John Wilkes Booth, and he is a sympathizer of the South, which has just lost the Civil War. And he has never concealed his hatred for President Lincoln, whom he sees precisely as the cause of all the South’s troubles. He has once even been arrested for the speeches he has made around the country against the government. John Wilkes Booth, the handsomest man in America, enters the presidential box without anybody checking him—different times, as you can see—takes out his pistol, and shoots the President in the back of the head. Lincoln’s guest, Major Henry Rathbone, tries to stop Booth; Booth stabs Rathbone and escapes, convinced that he is like Brutus, who killed Julius Caesar to defend liberty. [N-001] He will die two weeks later in a shootout with the cavalry that has followed him in his flight. Lincoln is the first President of the United States to die in an assassination attempt. And as you know, he will not be the last. After him, another three Presidents of the United States will be killed while in office; the last is John Kennedy, naturally. Lincoln’s death is a turning point in the conclusion of a civil war whose traditional estimate is about 620,000 military deaths, though newer demographic estimates are higher; comparisons with twentieth-century wars depend on which conflicts and casualty definitions are counted. [N-002] A war that had been triggered precisely by Lincoln’s election as President of the United States, in the elections of November 1860. Lincoln, the Republican candidate—but we will soon see that Democrats and Republicans, although they already existed then, did not cover the same electoral spectrum that they cover today—had been elected with 40 per cent of the popular vote, therefore a relative majority, also because at that time it was not customary, as it is now, to have only two candidates; there were more. In the 1860 election there are four candidates. Lincoln has a relative majority; he wins throughout the North, and in most of the slave states of the South he does not get even a single vote. A divided country, then. Today in the United States, the division of the electorate between those who vote for Trump and those who vote for Biden often evokes civil-war scenarios. Anyone who has American acquaintances knows that they really believe the country is almost in danger of going through such an experience again, it is so divided today. In reality, when one studies the origins of the American Civil War, one realizes, first of all, that for a country really to plunge into civil war, it is not enough, it is not enough for there to be a division in public opinion, for there to be even a substantial percentage of the population opposed to the system; it is not enough. Civil war breaks out in nineteenth-century America because two different models of development, of economy, of society confront one another in the country, each rooted in one part of the United States. And each of these two opposing models is so deeply rooted that in reality everybody believes in it—not just, precisely, the poor wretches who feel excluded, but politicians, intellectuals, the rich, ideologues. So what is it that, in the nineteenth century, divides the United States so profoundly as to make it plunge into civil war? And what are the United States in the middle of the nineteenth century?
