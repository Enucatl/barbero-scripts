## 4. Tariffs, Nullification, and State Sovereignty

The first moment when people realize that it is impossible to have an economic policy that suits everyone, and that therefore they will inevitably have to move towards a clash—a political clash, of course; at first nobody is thinking of anything else—but political confrontation becomes inevitable when the industrialists of the North demand, as happens today too, you know, tariffs against China? Well, tariffs suit American industry. We want goods coming from England to cost too much; our inhabitants must buy our stuff, our industrial production, so tariffs. The Southern planters do not want tariffs; they live off exports, they live by exporting cotton. Woe betide them if the English get angry and start imposing tariffs on American cotton. Maybe that seems like little to you; it is an economic conflict, there are such conflicts all the time in advanced countries, and yet in that economic conflict one begins to glimpse the basis for a dramatic split in the United States. Because what happens is that every time the industrialists' lobby in Washington tries to obtain a policy favourable to them, customs tariffs, in the South somebody begins to say that they do not care if the government in Washington decides these things, because the individual states are not at all obliged to do what Washington says. The United States are a union of free states, each of which joined voluntarily and is not obliged to respect federal laws. Do you see? I mean, we live in the Europe of Brexit; you understand how great the problem is of establishing how far you can go, how far you can push things once you have decided to enter a union. In the South, the argument is: we do not want laws contrary to our interests; if you make them, we will not apply them. In 1832, there are still thirty years to go before Lincoln's election; it is a long story, as you can see, this story of the origins of the Civil War. In 1832 Washington passes a law imposing higher customs tariffs, and one of the Southern states, the most extremist state in the South, one of the very few where black slaves are actually more numerous than white inhabitants—it is the same state that will later be the most extremist and the first to declare secession thirty years later, South Carolina. In 1832, faced with a federal law imposing new customs tariffs, South Carolina declares that, as far as South Carolina is concerned, that law is null. The State of South Carolina is free to nullify, as they put it, the laws of Washington. An extremely violent political debate opens up, which has gone down in history precisely as the debate over nullification. And there, for the first time, people begin to discuss what the United States really are. One of the great American politicians of the time enters the scene, a figure whom we in Italy today know very little about, naturally; he will die before the Civil War. He is one of the great politicians representing Southern interests; his name is Calhoun, John Calhoun. He is a very profound intellectual, a defender of slavery as the only system by which the South can live. And in the debate over nullification, John Calhoun, speaking for the whole South, declares that even though we have joined together, each individual state retains its full sovereignty. The states are sovereign, and the federal government in Washington is merely their agent, and there is no obligation to obey the government in Washington, because that is something one might do in nation-states, where the national interest is one, and the United States are not the same thing. In a memoir published decades later, Oliver Dyer recalls Calhoun as saying: “I never use the word Nation in speaking of the United States; I always use the word Union, or Confederacy. We are not a nation, but a Union, a confederacy of equal and sovereign States. England is a nation, Austria is a nation, Russia is a nation, but the United States are not a nation.” [N-034] Faced with this position, the ideologues of the North instead argue that the United States are a nation and that the laws of a nation must be the same for everyone, do you see? Today it is easy, I think, to see how Calhoun's argument, in its lucidity, runs counter to the spirit of the age, because in the nineteenth century—and we Italians know something about this—the spirit of the age is the nation, the union of the nation, the sacred interests of the nation. To say we are not a nation is an extraordinarily unsettling argument, but also, but also one that will lose out in the long run. And in fact today there are those who say, look, the American Civil War is the moment when this question is resolved, when the United States are transformed by force into a nation.
