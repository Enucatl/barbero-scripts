## 9. Proslavery Ideology and Sectional Polarization

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00805–U-00959 -->

The debate over slavery in the 1830s, 40s, and 50s takes on this new character. Politicians, ideologues, thinkers, theologians begin to appear; the Protestant preachers of the South are in the front line. Slavery is something sacred, it is in the Bible. Theorists begin to appear who defend slavery with fascinating arguments. For example, do you think the workers in the North are any better off? Why, in the North workers are exploited in an ignoble, shameful way; they break their backs for pennies. We treat our slaves well and they are happy. A quotation from one of the speeches of that time. “Our slaves are black, of another and inferior race. [...] They are happy, content, unaspiring, and utterly incapable, from intellectual weakness, ever to give us any trouble by their aspirations.” Other proslavery writers argue that enslavement brings Africans Christianity and civilization and deny that Black people can govern themselves. [N-021] Everyone in the United States agrees, mind you, that races exist and that races are divided into superior races and inferior races, more advanced races and more backward races, races that may perhaps one day reach our level, but for now have not yet got there. These are the arguments heard in the South: you want to free them? Then you will see anarchy, they will start doing everything, stealing, killing; we will have to exterminate them. Is that what you want? They are so happy as slaves. The remarkable thing is that these arguments gain a great deal of traction in the South even among that majority of the white population who do not own slaves. Because those who own slaves are a large minority of Southern whites, but the majority do not own slaves. And yet the supporters of the system actually manage to present it in such a way that even those who are not personally interested, because they do not own slaves, still feel that slavery is an advantage for them. There are those who explain very seriously—and incidentally note the intellectual effort deployed in this cause to defend slavery. They say: in every society there must be people who do the hardest, heaviest jobs, and in every society they are people who are poorly paid and live worse than others. And where we are, no white person does hard, heavy work and lives badly. Where we are, whites are all free and all equal. That is democracy. We have democracy, where whites are all equal, and this is because, precisely, we have another race that does the dirty work. And so they achieve the masterpiece of convincing poor whites that slavery is the guarantee of the American dream, of freedom, of equality. And for the masters this means that the South is safe from that other nightmare that is spreading across the world in the middle of the nineteenth century, namely class struggle. In Europe, for years now people have been talking about exploited, dissatisfied workers, strikes, threats of revolution, the spectre of communism, which Marx and Engels said in 1848, precisely, is haunting Europe. In the South the problem does not exist: whites are all happy and satisfied, they support the system. In a Boston lecture in January 1856, more than five years before Fort Sumter, Robert Toombs describes conditions in the South: “Stability, progress, order, peace, content, prosperity, reign throughout our borders. Not a single soldier is to be found in our widely-extended domain to overawe or protect society. The desire for organic change nowhere manifests itself.” [N-022] The South feels strong, and optimism gives rise to an extremist minority that wants ever more. Have we defeated Mexico? Now it is time to conquer Cuba, to expand into South America, new territories to open up to plantations and slavery. There is even a movement to reopen the slave trade with Africa, which Jefferson had abolished fifty years earlier. At the end of the 1850s, Maxcy Gregg is an advocate of reopening the transatlantic slave trade during the late-1850s agitation in South Carolina. He too will later be a Southern general, killed at the Battle of Fredericksburg. [N-023] So the South feels strong and is prepared to clash in order to defend its interests. It feels strong above all because cotton seems an absolute guarantee. European industry needs Southern cotton. In 1858, Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina, a former governor of the state, declares: “No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king.” [N-024] This is the slogan that Southern newspapers and politicians keep repeating. In turn, public opinion in the North begins to be alarmed. What do these slaveholders want? What is this all about? Do they really want to rule the whole United States? Do these slave masters want to come and rule in our homes too, by any chance? Concern about the conspiracy of the slaveholders, about slave power, spreads through Northern public opinion. SLAVE POWER. We all know, don’t we, how forcefully newspapers impose certain labels on public discourse that we then all repeat without thinking about them anymore, don’t we? Sovereigntism, populism—there it is, slave power. Once you have created it, it exists: people in the North begin to be afraid, the Southern planters are an arrogant power group plotting to dominate the United States. And all this, precisely, shapes public opinion and accustoms Northerners and Southerners to considering themselves two different nations. For Southerners, the North means disorder, foreign immigration, smoky cities, working-class misery, hypocritical Puritanism, the brute rule of money. For Northerners, the South is backwardness, laziness, immorality, a ball and chain on the progress of the United States. Even the Protestant churches split. The churches—the Baptists, the Methodists, who are strongest in mass public opinion. Well, that is, there are Northern Baptists and Southern Baptists. Those in the North demand the abolition of slavery on the basis of the Bible, and those in the South declare that slavery is perfectly just on the basis of the Bible. And in the South, as they gradually realize that the conflict of interests can no longer be patched up, they begin to think about Plan B. That is, if these people become too influential in Washington and we find ourselves in the minority, we leave, we secede. Those arguments John Calhoun had made years earlier become relevant again, namely: we are not a nation, we are a free union of States. This issue of states’ rights, you understand, is politically crucial. In the South the argument is: South Carolina or Virginia are sovereign States that have agreed to enter this union and can leave it whenever they wish.
