## 12. John Brown, Republicans, and Lincoln’s Campaign

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01130–U-01243 -->

In 1859 there is one last dramatic episode, and once again the protagonist is John Brown. John Brown has left Kansas and now intends to get serious, to unleash a slave revolt in the South. So he gathers a band and plans to seize a military arsenal in Virginia, Harpers Ferry, and distribute weapons to Black people. So in the summer of 1859 John Brown rents a farm near Harpers Ferry and then, little by little, has his men come there. When he has about twenty armed men, on 16 October 1859 he seizes the arsenal, which is virtually undefended, and begins sending messengers around, free Negroes—I say Negroes because after reading the sources of that period so much it becomes almost impossible not to use the words they use, even though we have become accustomed, so to speak, to being careful about the words we use—and these free Black men go around the plantations trying to persuade the slaves to rise up; nobody moves. The militia arrives; President Buchanan sends the Marines from Washington, under the command of a colonel who will become famous: Colonel Robert E. Lee, the very man who shortly afterwards will become commander of the Southern army. The Marines have a firefight; most of John Brown’s men are killed, John Brown is captured, tried, and sentenced to death. He is hanged on 2 December 1859. And he leaves, leaves a few lines in which he declares: “I John Brown am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land: will never be purged away; but with Blood. I had as I now think: vainly flattered myself that without very much bloodshed; it might be done.” John Brown’s death creates a myth, that myth which, indeed, still exists today. In the North, on the day John Brown is hanged, bells ring in his honor, cannon salutes are fired in his honor, the song is born. And the following year, 1860, there are elections. And at the elections a new party appears, which wants to challenge the hegemony of the Democratic Party. A new party that takes note of the fact that it is no longer certain that the West is allied with the South; by now the West has too many interests in common with the North, and so this alliance can be broken. The new party proposes, precisely, to bring together the interests of Northern industrialists and Western settlers and to put the slaveholding South in the minority. And the party presents itself as progressive, egalitarian, and takes the name Republican Party. As you can see, it is a paradox: the Republican Party and the Democratic Party still exist today, and today they have, roughly speaking, political positions, so to speak, reversed from those they have at that time. The Republican Party goes into the election with excellent prospects, because the South and the West really no longer agree, and so the Democratic Party splits, goes into the election with two opposing candidates; in addition there is an independent, and so the Republican Party, let us say, holds all the cards. To be sure of winning, they must convince the West, and so they choose as candidate a man from the West: Lincoln. A lawyer, a seasoned politician, who has already had political experience in his Illinois, but also in Washington, and yet, although he is a professional politician, he is someone on whom one can also stitch the image of the frontier man, the son of a settler raised in a log cabin, the man who understands you and represents you. Lincoln is a moderate candidate. The Republican platform for his candidacy is extremely moderate with regard to the problem of slavery, because the party knows perfectly well that extreme abolitionism, although it is gaining ground, is still not a mass phenomenon and that abolitionist ideas have not yet penetrated the masses. Just to give you an example: Kansas, Bleeding Kansas, where people kill one another, those who want slavery and those who want to abolish it. Then those who do not want slavery in Kansas win. The antislavery Wyandotte Constitution prohibits slavery and restricts suffrage to white male citizens, but it does not bar free Black people from residing in Kansas; the proposed exclusion clause is defeated. [N-029] Because not wanting slavery really means, we do not want the competition of Black people. And Lincoln, Lincoln is also chosen because they hope he will not frighten the South too much, because he has never been an abolitionist. He is someone who, so to speak, is aware that the problem will sooner or later have to be addressed, but does not hesitate to declare that he does not know how to do it. I quote from a speech by Lincoln: “If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do, as to the existing institution. My first impulse would be to free all the slaves, and send them to Liberia,---to their own native land. But a moment’s reflection would convince me, that whatever of high hope, as I think there is, there may be in this, in the long run, its sudden execution is impossible. [...] What next? Free them, and make them politically and socially, our equals? My own feelings will not admit of this; and if mine would, we well know that those of the great mass of white people will not.” Thus the politician, Lincoln. Therefore, in his platform Lincoln declares that he does not intend to meddle in slavery and that every state is sovereign and can do what it wants with regard to slavery. But at the same time the Republican platform makes clear that the Southerners must stop talking about states’ rights. Secession? No: the federal government is superior to the individual states. No state has the right to nullify federal laws; talking about secession is treason. From an economic point of view, Lincoln’s platform is what Northern industrialists want: large investments, railways westward, and so on. The solid South does not want Lincoln. And Southern politicians present Lincoln to their voters as the devil, the abolitionist, even as a follower of John Brown, and you can imagine how popular John Brown was in the South at that moment.
