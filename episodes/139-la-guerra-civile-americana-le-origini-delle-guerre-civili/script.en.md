# How Civil Wars Begin: The American Civil War

## 1. Lincoln’s Assassination and the Civil War Question

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00085 -->

Thank you. Now, imagine you’re in Washington on April 14, 1865. Five days have passed since General Robert E. Lee, commander of the Confederate army, surrendered at Appomattox, bringing the American Civil War to an end. President Lincoln is at the theater watching a comedy.

During the performance, an actor walks into the theater—a hugely famous stage actor. He’s so famous that nobody checks him or tries to stop him. This is his world. He’s very young, only twenty-seven, but he’s already been one of the most famous actors in America for several years. And you have to remember, this is an age when theater fills the role that television and movies fill today, and probably then some. The newspapers have been praising this actor for years. They call him the handsomest man in America.

His name is John Wilkes Booth. He sympathizes with the South, which has just lost the Civil War, and he’s never hidden his hatred for President Lincoln, the man he blames for all the South’s troubles. He’s even been arrested once for speeches he gave around the country against the government.

So John Wilkes Booth, the handsomest man in America, walks into the presidential box without anybody checking him—different times, as you can see—pulls out a pistol, and shoots the president in the back of the head. Lincoln’s guest, Major Henry Rathbone, tries to stop him. Booth stabs Rathbone and escapes, convinced that he’s another Brutus, killing Julius Caesar in defense of liberty. [N-001] Two weeks later, he dies in a shootout with the cavalry that has pursued him.

Lincoln is the first president of the United States to die in an assassination. And as you know, he won’t be the last. Three more presidents will be killed in office after him, the latest being John Kennedy, of course.

Lincoln’s death marks a turning point at the close of a civil war that cost America 600,000 dead—far more than all of America’s twentieth-century wars combined. [N-002] And this war had been set off by Lincoln’s election as president of the United States in November 1860.

Lincoln is the Republican candidate. But we’ll soon see that although Democrats and Republicans already exist, they don’t occupy the same parts of the political spectrum that they do today. He wins with 40 percent of the popular vote—a plurality. That’s possible in part because, unlike today, elections don’t normally have just two candidates. There are four in 1860. Lincoln wins across the North, while in most of the South’s slave states he doesn’t receive a single vote. So this is a divided country.

Today, the split in the American electorate between Trump voters and Biden voters often brings up talk of civil war. Anyone with American friends knows that many of them truly believe the country is so divided that it may be in danger of going through that experience again.

But when you study the origins of the American Civil War, the first thing you realize is that a divided public isn’t enough to plunge a country into civil war. Even having a substantial share of the population opposed to the system isn’t enough. Civil war breaks out in nineteenth-century America because two different models of development, two different economies and societies, are confronting each other, and each one is rooted in a different part of the United States. Both models are so deeply established that everyone believes in them—not just some poor souls who feel shut out, but politicians, intellectuals, the wealthy, and ideologues.

So what divides the United States so deeply in the nineteenth century that the country plunges into civil war? And what are the United States in the middle of the nineteenth century? Let’s look at a few figures.

## 2. America in 1860: Growth and the North-South Divide

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00086–U-00198 -->

In 1860, the United States has 31 million people. That may sound small to us today, when the population is 300 million, but in the mid-nineteenth century it’s a very large number. The United States has more people than Great Britain, while France has nearly six million more. And those are the world’s two greatest powers, the two leading countries in Europe, both of them already ruling vast colonial empires around the globe. So the United States already has the potential to become one of the world’s great powers. [N-003]

But here’s the most impressive part. In 1860, the population is 31 million. Just ten years earlier, it was only 23 million. So in a single decade, it has grown by a third. The country is expanding at an extraordinary pace. It’s filling up with immigrants and going through enormous industrial growth. As you know, the middle decades of the nineteenth century bring explosive industrial growth to the leading countries: Great Britain, Germany, France, and the United States.

From 1850 to 1860, the value of American manufacturing rises by about 85 percent. When we hear numbers like that, it’s worth stopping for a moment. You know how hard we struggle for half a percent of growth a year? One percent? Here we’re talking about roughly 85 percent in ten years.

For now, more Americans still live on farms than in cities, but that balance is already about to shift. In 1860, the United States has about 49,000 kilometers of working railroad, with still more under construction. About 49,000 kilometers. Britain, France, and the German states combined have only about 35,000 kilometers. [N-004]

And there’s more. This is a society unlike any other in the world, because it’s a democracy with universal suffrage, something no European country has. Universal suffrage as Americans understand it, of course: male. At the same time, a women’s movement is already demanding equal rights and the vote. About 90 percent of free white adults can read and write under the measure used by the census. Wages are higher than in Europe, and inequality is lower. Today, as we know, the United States has the widest gap between the super-rich and the poor among Western countries, even if we in Italy are catching up fast. Literacy is extraordinarily high. In Italy at the same date, it’s 20 percent. In the United States, 90. Everybody reads the newspaper. [N-005]

Now, this broad picture applies to the whole country—but only to a point. Democracy, egalitarianism, and literacy exist across the country, though they may be even more pronounced in the North than in the South. But huge industrial growth, immigration, crowds of new arrivals, constant development and change—those belong to the North alone. The North has three quarters of the population and almost all the factories and industrial workers.

The South is rich, but its economy is entirely agricultural. Its wealth lies in plantations and slaves. Four million slaves represent an immense amount of capital, you see. So on paper, the South possesses colossal wealth. It’s fabulously rich from cotton, which has become almost its only crop. Virginia still grows some tobacco, but a century earlier the South produced tobacco, sugar, and rice. By 1860, it’s cotton country.

And it’s cotton country because the Industrial Revolution is underway in England. Every year, the mills of Manchester consume a mountain of cotton, and that cotton comes from the Southern states. With all the capital tied up in four million slaves and all the profits from cotton, the South is fabulously rich. But its economy is stagnant. It isn’t growing or changing. The North, by contrast, is being transformed from top to bottom.

And yet, until the middle of the century, the South is in charge politically. The elite of large landowners—the Southern planters—has dominated American politics. Lincoln is the sixteenth president. Of the fifteen presidents before him, nine were born in Southern states, and all nine had owned slaves at some point. Think about that. Until Lincoln’s election, just over half of all American presidents had personally owned slaves. Even the presidents from the North had always been elected through political deals with Southern planters. There had never been a president who stood against the interests of the South. [N-006]

## 3. Three Regions and Westward Expansion

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00199–U-00276 -->

So what explains the South’s political dominance? Well, although I’ve been talking about North and South, the United States isn’t really divided into two parts. It’s divided into three, because there’s also the West.

First, there’s the North, with Puritan New England at its heart—Connecticut and Massachusetts—and the great metropolis of New York. The North is a world of commerce and ports, of business and industry. Its population is growing at a tremendous rate, and commercial and industrial capitalism dominate its economy.

The South is agricultural. It grows cotton and then sends it North because it doesn’t even have good ports of its own. Cotton is what the South lives on. An elite of planters controls the region, and its economy is built on slavery.

Then there’s the West, because much of the American continent still remains to be colonized. In the eyes of white people, the land belongs to no one. We know, of course, that countless Indian nations live there—to use the older term—or Indigenous, Native nations. But to white Americans, it’s as if no one is there. This is a world to be conquered.

The West they’re conquering at this point isn’t yet the Far West of cowboys. It’s the West, a land of settlers and farmers, where new arrivals believe there’s enough land for everyone. Settlers obtain or claim Western land cheaply, and at one point even for free. [N-007]

Naturally, this is also an egalitarian society—the most egalitarian in America. It’s individualistic, and it’s made up of farmers. And that explains why the West and the South remain allies for so long. Fundamentally, they share the same interests. They want to protect agriculture, not industry, and they both want the country to expand.

Territorial expansion means the United States must find room for the flood of immigrants arriving in the country. It must keep moving west, and the Southern planters are just as eager to expand in that direction. The West is vast, but beyond it lies the whole Southwest.

Take the war with Mexico in 1848, which we remember almost nothing about. Who knows that the United States goes to war with Mexico in 1848? At most, we remember the Alamo or Davy Crockett from some Hollywood movie. But the war is an epoch-making event. By defeating Mexico, the United States confirms the Texas boundary and takes possession of immense territories, including New Mexico and California. Texas itself had already been annexed and admitted to the United States in 1845. [N-008]

The Southern planters and Western settlers want that war. They’re allies, represented in politics by a party that still exists today: the Democratic Party. It may seem strange to us that the party of the Southern planters is the Democratic Party. But people my age remember that even in the 1960s, the South still votes Democratic. Then, with Reagan, it begins voting Republican, and now it’s the stronghold of the Republicans and of Trump. But for a very long time, Southerners simply assume that the Democratic Party is their party.

In the nineteenth century, the Democrats are the party of the South. They’re also the party of the Western settlers and small landowners. By the middle of the century, though, that coalition is coming under threat. Against this alliance of farmers and planters, the industrialists of the North are gaining more and more power, and they want very different policies from the ones that benefit agriculture.

## 4. Tariffs, Nullification, and State Sovereignty

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00277–U-00364 -->

The first sign that no single economic policy can satisfy everyone comes when the industrialists of the North demand tariffs. And once that happens, political conflict becomes inevitable. Political conflict, mind you—at first, no one is imagining anything more.

You know the arguments today about tariffs on Chinese goods? Tariffs are good for American industry. The thinking is simple: make goods from England too expensive, so Americans have to buy our products instead. That means tariffs.

The Southern planters want nothing to do with them. They live on exports. They make their money by selling cotton abroad, and the last thing they need is for the British to get angry and impose tariffs on American cotton. This may not sound like much. It’s an economic dispute, and advanced countries have those all the time. Yet this dispute begins to reveal the foundations of a dramatic split in the United States.

Every time the industrial lobby in Washington pushes for a policy that benefits the North—tariffs, for example—people in the South begin saying they don’t care what the government in Washington decides. The individual states, they argue, don’t have to do what Washington tells them. The United States are a union of free states. Each joined voluntarily, and none is required to obey federal law.

You see the problem? We live in the Europe of Brexit. We understand how difficult it is to decide how far a union can go once you’ve chosen to join it. The Southern argument is straightforward: we don’t want laws that go against our interests. If you pass them, we won’t enforce them.

This is already happening in 1832, nearly thirty years before Lincoln’s election. The road to the Civil War is a long one, as you can see. Washington passes a law raising tariffs, and one Southern state responds. It’s the most extreme state in the South, one of the very few where Black slaves outnumber white residents. Thirty years later, this same state will be the first to secede: South Carolina.

South Carolina declares that, within its borders, the new federal tariff law is null. The state claims the right to “nullify,” as they put it, laws passed in Washington. This sets off a ferocious political debate that goes down in history as the nullification crisis. And for the first time, Americans begin arguing over what the United States really are.

One of the great American political figures of the age now enters the story. Naturally, he’s someone we know very little about in Italy today, and he will die before the Civil War. His name is John Calhoun, and he’s one of the leading defenders of Southern interests. He’s a formidable intellectual who defends slavery as the only system under which the South can survive.

During the nullification debate, Calhoun speaks for the South. He argues that even though the states have joined together, each one retains its full sovereignty. The states themselves are sovereign. The federal government in Washington is merely their agent, and they have no obligation to obey it. That kind of obedience may make sense in a nation-state, where there’s one national interest, but the United States, he says, are something different.

In a memoir published decades later, Oliver Dyer recalls Calhoun as saying: “I never use the word Nation in speaking of the United States; I always use the word Union, or Confederacy. We are not a nation, but a Union, a confederacy of equal and sovereign States. England is a nation, Austria is a nation, Russia is a nation, but the United States are not a nation.” [N-034]

Northern thinkers take the opposite position. They argue that the United States are a nation, and a nation’s laws must apply equally to everyone. You see? From where we stand today, I think it’s easy to see how Calhoun’s argument, for all its clarity, runs against the spirit of the age. In the nineteenth century—and we Italians know something about this—the age belongs to the nation, national unity, and the sacred interests of the nation.

To say, “We are not a nation,” is an extraordinarily unsettling argument. It’s also an argument that will lose in the long run. In fact, some people today say that this is the question the American Civil War settles: it’s the moment when the United States are turned into a nation by force.

For now, the dispute remains a war of words and politics over tariffs.

## 5. Expansion, Slavery, and the Senate Balance

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00365–U-00509 -->

All in all, the South and the West still have the same interests: farmers on both sides. What finally breaks apart this great alliance between them is the dramatic expansion of the United States. By the 1840s and 1850s, the country is swallowing up this enormous stretch of land conquered from Mexico and pushing all the way to the Pacific. At the end of the 1840s, you have the California Gold Rush, for example, right? And now the great epic is no longer just the West, but the Far West—the distant West. You have the wars against the Plains Indians and the wagon trains of settlers pushing farther and farther west. You know all the American mythology, right? “Go West, young man,” because the West is the future of this new nation.

But there’s a problem. How exactly does the United States expand westward? It works like this: the West is nothing, it belongs to nobody. It belongs to the Indians, if anyone, but people simply pretend the Indians aren’t there. It belongs to Mexico, but the Mexicans are nasty and evil, so we defeat them and take whatever we want. Once a territory—which can be as large as a European country—has a few thousand white settlers, they can ask for it to be recognized as a U.S. territory. Not a state yet, but a territory, an intermediate stage. Then, after a few years, when the white population has grown further, it can officially become a state. [N-009] That’s how the United States keeps adding states. As you know, the Stars and Stripes originally had thirteen stars, one for each of the thirteen colonies that rebelled against England. Today the flag has fifty stars for fifty states. New states are added one after another.

So what’s the problem? The United States is divided between states that allow slavery and states that forbid it. The free-slave divide was not marked by a single line across the continent: the Mason-Dixon Line had eastern significance, while in the western territories the crucial boundary was the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′. [N-010] I say the North abolished slavery because, until the Revolution, slavery existed throughout the United States—even in the North, even in New England. But it had never really taken root there. In an economy built on trade, shipping, and industry, enslaved labor isn’t especially useful. On the great Southern plantations, it is. We’ll come back later to the real reasons the North generally abolished slavery. It’s not as simple as we might think. But for now, let’s leave that aside.

For now, all you need to understand is that the problem comes up whenever the country has to decide whether a new territory—and later a new state—will allow slavery. The Southern ruling class pushes to admit slavery in the new territories and states. Their thinking is: then we can move out there too, buy land, expand, build new plantations, and bring our slaves with us. If those states ban slavery, only Northern farmers will be able to settle and work there.

For the Southern states, extending slavery into the new states is vital. After Texas declares independence in 1836, Anglo-Texan rebels protect slavery, and Texas enters the United States as a slave state in 1845. Mexico has abolished slavery. [N-011] Southern elites want that war because they need the new states to be slave states. By now, everyone can see that two opposing blocs have formed: the industrial and commercial states on one side, and the agricultural slave states on the other.

That struggle plays out in Washington, where the Senate is a crucial part of the political system. As you probably know, the United States Senate is a marvelous institution—still marvelous today—because its makeup has nothing at all to do with a state’s population. Every state sends two senators to Washington, whether it’s a tiny state with half a million people or California. Two senators each.

So if you have a bloc of slave states, you need as many slave states as possible. Every new state that allows slavery gives our bloc two senators. Every new state that forbids it gives the other side two senators. This produces some surreal results.

Let me tell you about the first time they have to deal with it. It’s 1820, and a new Western state is being created: Missouri. For the first time, the two sides clash. Do we want slavery there? No, we don’t. In the end, the Southerners win, and Missouri enters as a slave state. But to compensate the North, they create a new Northern state by cutting off a small piece of Massachusetts: Maine. Maine had once been a separate colony and later became part of Massachusetts. Now they carve it away again and create a new Northern state, one that of course forbids slavery. That gives the North two more senators in Washington to represent its interests.

The issue comes up again during the Mexican-American War. The Wilmot Proviso of 1846 raises the question of whether slavery will be barred from territory acquired from Mexico. In December 1849, Representative Robert Toombs of Georgia declares: “I do not, then, hesitate to avow before this House and the country, and in the presence of the living God, that if by your legislation you seek to drive us from the territories of California and New Mexico, purchased by the common blood and treasure of the whole people, and to abolish slavery in this District, thereby attempting to fix a national degradation upon half the States of this Confederacy, I am for disunion; and if my physical courage be equal to the maintenance of my convictions of right and duty, I will devote all I am and all I have on earth to its consummation.” The Compromise of 1850 admits California as a free state and organizes Utah and New Mexico without a legal ban on slavery. [N-012]

## 6. The West Aligns with the North

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00510–U-00621 -->

But as this debate unfolds, a major political shift emerges. Until now, Southern planters and Western settlers have been political allies. But when it comes to allowing slavery in the new Western territories, the settlers aren’t so sure. Everyone is hungry for land. Everyone wants these new territories. And the Western settlers are small farmers. They don’t own slaves, and they don’t need them. So they start asking: why should we listen to the Southern planters and bring slavery into these new territories?

There’s something else going on too. With the American economy growing at a frantic pace in the 1840s and 1850s, the West is becoming more and more closely tied to the North. Think about the railroads. In the United States, the rail lines run east to west, stitching the two regions together. They connect the industrial, port-filled North to the agricultural West. Thanks to the railroads, Western grain and meat no longer have to travel down the Mississippi by barge to New Orleans, the one Southern port they could reach that way. Now Western goods travel east by rail, to Boston and New York.

The industrial North is also producing machinery, including farm equipment. That equipment isn’t much use in the South, where slaves do the work on cotton plantations by hand and no one wants to invest in mechanization. But the independent small farmers of the West? They’re absolutely buying the new mechanical threshers.

And new cities spring up in the West and become major cities almost from nothing. Chicago. Just think of Chicago, becoming a metropolis within a few decades, with its slaughterhouses and canned-meat factories. Sixty years later, Al Capone’s empire will still be built on that: control of the slaughterhouses, the industry, and the processing plants. All of this drives the relationship between the agricultural West and the industries and ports of the North. Meanwhile, the South of cotton and slavery feels increasingly cut off from the rest of the country economically.

So when people ask—as we hear more and more often today—was the American Civil War really about slavery? Was slavery really the issue? Yes, it was. Of course it was. But we have to understand what that means. Slavery is the reason the United States breaks apart, and the sharpest observers see it coming long beforehand.

As early as 1832, Tocqueville—you know, the great French traveler who crosses the United States and writes extraordinarily perceptive books about it—writes, thirty years before the Civil War: “The most formidable of all the evils that threaten the future of the United States arises from the presence of Blacks on their soil.”

So yes, slavery is the reason. But be careful. We shouldn’t turn this into a moralistic or humanitarian story, with good people on one side who want to abolish slavery and bad people on the other who want to preserve it. The real struggle is over which part of the country will dominate.

Why has the North abolished slavery? White workers’ hostility to enslaved labor is one factor, but northern abolition reflects varied state laws, antislavery principle, Black and Quaker activism, revolutionary politics, labor interests, and racial exclusion. [N-013] To be clear, educated elites in the North talk about slavery as something shameful that needs to be abolished, just as European elites do in the eighteenth century. But these are elite debates. They have almost no influence on popular opinion in the North and West.

John Adams, for example, writes—we’re at the beginning of the nineteenth century, and Massachusetts has abolished slavery—“argument might have some Weight, in the Abolition of Slavery in the Massachusetts, but the real Cause was the multiplication of labouring White People, who, would no longer suffer the Rich to employ these Sable Rivals, so much to their Injury. This Principle has kept Negro Slavery out of France England and other Parts of Europe. The common People would not suffer, the Labour by which alone they could obtain a subsistance to be done by Slaves. If the Gentlemen had been permitted by Law to hold Slaves, the common white People would have put the Negroes to Death and their Masters too perhaps.” That’s how the second president of the United States explains why the Northern states abolished slavery.

And there’s more evidence that the motives aren’t humanitarian. Many Northern states, including New York, and nearly all the new Western states have laws that deny free Black people the vote, keep them out of school and off juries, ban interracial marriage, and, in some cases, even bar Black people from living there. Illinois, Lincoln’s state, has a law forbidding any free Black person who isn’t already a resident from entering the state. In other words: we can’t throw out the people who are already here, but we don’t want any more.

## 7. Early Southern Doubts and Colonization Schemes

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00622–U-00702 -->

Meanwhile, at the beginning of the nineteenth century, people in the South are seriously debating whether to abolish slavery. Educated Southern elites feel the moral weight of it, and they also see slavery as an outdated economic system that isn’t really profitable. So there is a genuine debate in the South over whether abolition might be the better choice.

In the early years of the nineteenth century, they eventually decide to abolish—not slavery, because that would be an enormous step—but the slave trade. In other words: we’ll keep the slaves we have, but it’s inhuman to go out and get more. Congress passes the Act to Prohibit the Importation of Slaves in 1807, during Jefferson’s presidency, and it takes effect on January 1, 1808. [N-014] Jefferson is a Southerner and a slave owner, but he is also absolutely convinced that slavery will eventually have to be overcome. In the meantime, he thinks it’s better to keep it from expanding too much.

You see? Some people in the South are also doing the math and starting to worry. How many Black people are there? In 1860, there are four and a half million Black people in the United States, four million of them enslaved. They make up 14 percent of the country’s population—the same percentage as today—but in the South, they make up 40 percent. Almost all of them are there.

So around the time of the Revolution, the American War of Independence, it’s perfectly normal to hear Southerners say that slavery is outdated, immoral, and something the country has to move beyond. The first president, George Washington, is a great Virginia planter who owns two hundred slaves, whom he will free in his will. Washington writes that he had “great repugnance to encreasing my Slaves by purchase,” while elsewhere expressing gradual-emancipation sympathies. [N-015]

Extremely popular Southern politicians have no trouble saying such things in those years—and remember, we’re talking about the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Later, the tune will change, as we’ll see. In a private 1773 letter, Patrick Henry writes: “What adds to the wonder is, that this abominable practice has been introduced in the most enlightened ages.” In a Senate speech in 1826, John Randolph of Roanoke says: “It is a cancer on your face, and must not be tampered with by quacks, who never saw the disease or the patient, and prescribe across the Atlantic.” [N-016]

And as late as 1832—a fateful year that I realize I’ve already mentioned several times—the Virginia assembly, the legislature of the most important state in the South, debates a proposal to abolish slavery. It fails, but only by a few votes.

Above all, it fails because even if everyone can agree in principle that slavery will eventually have to end, what do you actually do? As we’ve said, slaves represent an enormous amount of capital. You can’t just—how can I put this? Private property is sacred, folks. This is America. You can’t take an enormous piece of private property away from people without compensating the owners. And where will the money come from?

Most of all, once you free Black people, what do you tell them to do? That’s where the debate stalls, because almost no one imagines that Black people can continue living in the United States as free citizens alongside white people. They have to be sent somewhere.

In the first decades of the nineteenth century, whenever Southerners debate abolition, organizations also spring up to send free Black people back to Africa. That’s the proposed solution: humanitarian societies will raise the money and arrange it. The American Colonization Society establishes Liberia in 1822 through agreements and coercive expansion, and begins financing the transfer of freed slaves to Africa. All of this happens under President Monroe, a Southern president and slave owner who strongly supports the project of sending Black people back to Africa. That’s why Liberia’s capital, after the colony becomes an independent country, is named Monrovia—and is still called that today—in honor of President Monroe, a slave owner who supports sending slaves to Africa as they are freed.

Over the next few decades, they send something like fifteen thousand freed Black people to Liberia. There, those settlers go on to dominate Indigenous populations through unequal political authority, coerced labor, and systems of dependency—but that’s another story. That’s the kind of society they have in mind. [N-017]

Now, the point is that everything I’ve been telling you applies to the South in the early years of the nineteenth century.

## 8. Cotton Profits and the Rise of Abolitionism

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00703–U-00804 -->

But by the time Abraham Lincoln is elected, that’s no longer true. In 1860, no Southern politician would dare say that slavery is immoral and must be abolished. Why not? What has changed?

One reason is economic, and it shows us just how powerfully economics can shape public opinion and the way an entire society thinks. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, the Southern economy isn’t all that rich. Yes, it produces sugar, tobacco, and cotton, but profits are modest. Plenty of people can still say, “This economic system has no future.”

Then the Industrial Revolution changes everything. I mentioned it earlier, but now you can see what it really means. With industrialization, producing cotton with enslaved labor turns out to be enormously profitable. So fewer and fewer people are willing to say that slavery is an archaic system that has to be left behind. No, the numbers speak for themselves: slavery produces huge profits.

One result is that the price of slaves soars. The slave trade has been banned, though illegal imports continue after the federal ban, with documented ties to New York and other Northern commercial networks. [N-018] Officially, though, the trade is over, so prices rise. By the outbreak of the Civil War, a slave can easily cost $1,000 at a time when a farm laborer earns $8 a month.

So perhaps, people tell themselves, we were wrong about slavery. It isn’t archaic or outdated at all. Not in the slightest. It’s a system that guarantees gigantic profits.

There’s another reason the South stops talking about abolishing slavery, and this one is ideological. Here again we see how powerful ideology can be—how it can blind people when they take it too seriously and when it divides society into two opposing camps. Black, Quaker, and gradual-emancipation antislavery traditions all exist long before the 1830s. What expands in that decade is a new immediatist abolitionist movement demanding immediate abolition with no compensation for enslavers. [N-019]

This movement takes hold in the North and attacks slavery on moral grounds. With growing urgency, it demands an end to slavery now—no endless debate, no compensation. Slavery is shameful. Slaveholders have made a pact with the devil, will burn in hell, and deserve no respect. In a Christian country, slavery must be abolished.

Abolitionism becomes a major force in Northern public opinion. It never makes much headway among the masses, but from the 1830s and ’40s onward, it gains a great deal of ground among educated elites. And the South grows increasingly frightened. Faced with the abolitionists’ language, Southerners dig in.

For a Southern politician, it becomes impossible to keep saying what had once been perfectly normal: yes, sooner or later, slavery will have to end. Say that now and you simply won’t be reelected. James McDowell, who supports gradual emancipation in Virginia in 1832, later becomes governor and stops raising the issue. John H. Pleasants blames “the fanatics of the North [who] have taken the most effective means not to leave a single friend of emancipation in the Southern States.” [N-020]

Speaking against slavery is political suicide in the South. I’ll say it again: nobody will vote for you.

So do you see what’s happening? The Southern states now see themselves as an isolated section of the country, with interests opposed to those of everyone else, and they identify those interests with slavery. Slavery has to be defended at all costs. Otherwise, their system will be reduced to a minority, and they’re finished. Southern intellectuals stop criticizing slavery and start defending it.

## 9. Proslavery Ideology and Sectional Polarization

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00805–U-00959 -->

The debate over slavery takes on a new character in the 1830s, ’40s, and ’50s. Politicians, ideologues, thinkers, and theologians all enter the fray, with Southern Protestant preachers leading the charge. Slavery, they argue, is sacred. It’s in the Bible.

Theorists begin defending slavery with some remarkable arguments. Do you really think Northern workers are any better off? In the North, they say, workers are shamefully exploited. They break their backs for pennies. We treat our slaves well, and they’re happy.

Here’s a line from one speech of the period: “Our slaves are black, of another and inferior race. [...] They are happy, content, unaspiring, and utterly incapable, from intellectual weakness, ever to give us any trouble by their aspirations.” Other proslavery writers argue that enslavement brings Africans Christianity and civilization, and they deny that Black people can govern themselves. [N-021]

Remember, virtually everyone in the United States accepts the idea that races exist and can be ranked: superior and inferior, more advanced and less advanced. Perhaps, they think, the supposedly inferior races might one day reach our level, but they haven’t yet.

So this is what people in the South say: You want to free them? Then watch what happens. There will be anarchy. They’ll steal, they’ll kill, and we’ll have to exterminate them. Is that what you want? They’re so happy as slaves.

What’s striking is how persuasive these arguments become even among the majority of Southern whites who don’t own slaves. Slaveholders make up a large minority of the white population, but still a minority. Yet the system’s defenders manage to convince even people with no direct stake in slavery that it benefits them too.

Some lay out the case very seriously—and notice the intellectual effort being poured into the defense of slavery. Every society, they say, needs people to do the hardest, heaviest work. Everywhere, those people are paid badly and live worse than everyone else. But here, no white person has to do that grueling work or live that way. Here, all white people are free and equal. That is democracy. We have equality among whites precisely because another race does the dirty work.

And so they pull off the extraordinary feat of convincing poor whites that slavery guarantees the American dream: freedom and equality. For slaveholders, it also means the South is safe from another nightmare spreading across the world in the mid-nineteenth century—class struggle. Europe has spent years talking about exploited, discontented workers, strikes, and the threat of revolution. In 1848, Marx and Engels say that the specter of communism is haunting Europe. The South supposedly has no such problem. White people are happy and content, and they support the system.

In a Boston lecture in January 1856, more than five years before Fort Sumter, Robert Toombs describes conditions in the South: “Stability, progress, order, peace, content, prosperity, reign throughout our borders. Not a single soldier is to be found in our widely-extended domain to overawe or protect society. The desire for organic change nowhere manifests itself.” [N-022]

The South feels strong, and that optimism produces an extremist minority that always wants more. We defeated Mexico, didn’t we? Now let’s conquer Cuba. Let’s expand into South America and open new territory to plantations and slavery. A movement even emerges to reopen the slave trade with Africa, which Jefferson had abolished fifty years earlier. During the agitation in South Carolina in the late 1850s, Maxcy Gregg advocates reopening the transatlantic slave trade. He later becomes a Confederate general and is killed at the Battle of Fredericksburg. [N-023]

So the South feels strong and is ready to fight for its interests. Above all, it feels secure because cotton seems like an absolute guarantee. European industry needs Southern cotton. In 1858, Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina, a former governor of the state, declares: “No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king.” [N-024]

That’s the slogan Southern newspapers and politicians keep repeating. And Northern public opinion, in turn, grows alarmed. What do these slaveholders want? What is this all about? Do they really intend to control the entire United States? Do these enslavers plan to come rule us in our own homes?

Fear of a slaveholders’ conspiracy—of the “Slave Power”—spreads across the North. SLAVE POWER. We all know how powerfully newspapers can impose a label on public debate until everyone repeats it without thinking. Sovereigntism, populism—and here it is, Slave Power. Once you create the label, the thing itself seems to exist. Northerners begin to fear that Southern planters are an arrogant power bloc conspiring to dominate the United States.

All of this shapes public opinion and gets Northerners and Southerners used to thinking of themselves as two separate nations. For Southerners, the North means disorder, foreign immigration, smoke-filled cities, working-class misery, hypocritical Puritanism, and the brutal rule of money. For Northerners, the South means backwardness, laziness, immorality—a dead weight holding back American progress.

Even the Protestant churches split, including the Baptists and Methodists, the denominations with the strongest influence over mass opinion. There are Northern Baptists and Southern Baptists. Those in the North use the Bible to demand the abolition of slavery; those in the South use the Bible to declare slavery perfectly just.

And as Southerners gradually realize that this conflict of interests can no longer be patched up, they begin to consider Plan B. If these people become too powerful in Washington and put us in the minority, we’ll leave. We’ll secede.

The arguments John Calhoun made years earlier become relevant again: we aren’t a nation; we’re a voluntary union of states. This question of states’ rights, you see, is politically crucial. The Southern argument is that South Carolina and Virginia are sovereign states. They agreed to join the Union, and they can leave whenever they choose.

## 10. The 1850 Compromise and Northern Radicalization

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00960–U-01018 -->

In 1850, the question is whether the new state of California will allow slavery. Lincoln’s election is still ten years away, but for the first time, delegates from several Southern states gather at the Nashville Convention and openly discuss leaving the Union under certain conditions. [N-025] If Washington’s policies don’t go our way, they ask, what can we do? And for the first time, quite a few voices answer: we’ll leave.

Now, you understand, saying it is one thing. Seriously considering it is another. There’s still a long way to go.

The Senate debates the crisis and looks for a compromise. John Calhoun, now an old man with only a short time left to live, considers peaceful separation if no settlement can be reached. The Senate is alarmed. Even that goes too far. So they look for a compromise everyone can somehow live with.

Here’s the deal: California will be a free state. The South has to swallow that bitter pill, but in return it gets something that seems essential. Federal authorities will hunt down slaves who escape from the South into the North. Escape is widespread, though hardly a mass phenomenon. Until now, a slave who reaches a free state can simply disappear. Now the federal government promises to go after them. The Fugitive Slave Act authorizes federal marshals, commissioners, and deputies to seize alleged fugitives, forces local people to help, and sends those captured back into slavery. [N-026]

This horrifies Northern public opinion. In their own communities, Northerners watch slaveholders mobilize federal authorities to hunt down people who have escaped. So the Slave Power is real after all. These people really do want to come into our homes and rule us.

The abolitionist movement grows. More and more people confront the immorality of slavery and make a serious commitment to helping those who escape. Secret networks emerge to help them—the Underground Railroad.

Then, in 1852, a book called *Uncle Tom’s Cabin, or Life Among the Humble* appears and immediately becomes famous. Its portrait of life on Southern plantations is highly sentimental and, according to specialists, rather fanciful. Still, the book quickly becomes a Northern bestseller.

Northern public opinion remains deeply prejudiced against Black people. Most white Northerners certainly don’t want Black neighbors. Even so, the idea begins to spread in the North that slavery itself may truly be wrong—and that Southerners who insist on keeping people enslaved really are acting immorally.

## 11. Bleeding Kansas and the Dred Scott Decision

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-01019–U-01129 -->

Then, in the middle of the decade—in 1855 and 1856—a new tragedy erupts: the tragedy of Kansas. Kansas is a new territory in the West, north of the Missouri Compromise line at 36°30′. The Kansas-Nebraska Act has repealed the restriction on slavery there, but people in the North take it for granted that Kansas will be free. [N-027] The government, heavily influenced by Southern politicians, decides instead to let the people of Kansas choose whether to allow slavery.

So both North and South start urging people to move to Kansas and create a majority. Settlers come up from the South to support slavery; settlers come from the North to oppose it. This is the West. Everyone carries a gun. And the violence begins.

On May 21, 1856, a group of Southerners attacks Lawrence, a small town settled by Northerners. They loot the town and destroy the presses of its abolitionist newspapers. Four days later, on May 25, the Northerners answer with what becomes known as the Pottawatomie massacre. A group of abolitionists arrives at a Southern settler’s home at night, near the Pottawatomie River. They drag the man and two of his sons into the woods and hack them to death with swords. Then they go to a second settler’s house, pull him outside, and stab him to death. Then they reach the cabin of a third settler, where they find the owner and three guests. They question the men to find out who they are, take one of them into the woods, and kill him.

Regrettably, the group’s leader is someone we’ve all heard of: John Brown. He is the celebrated abolitionist leader from a song that, in my day, every child knew. The other men are his sons and friends. John Brown—Captain John Brown, as he calls himself—is a Northerner and a fiercely committed religious abolitionist. He believes slavery is absolute evil, that it must be destroyed, and that slaveholders must be wiped out. He has made his sons swear to fight to the death to abolish slavery, and he believes he can lead the slaves in revolt.

For now, he is operating in Kansas. Before long, we’ll find him somewhere else, and by then we really will be nearing the end of our overview.

A case like Kansas obviously inflames public opinion on both sides. As you can imagine, each side sees only the other side’s violence. So people across the country begin talking about “Bleeding Kansas.” But in the North, that means slaveholders are killing people. In the South, it means those damned abolitionists are killing our people.

Then, in 1857—we’re getting closer—a legal battle erupts: the Dred Scott case. Dred Scott is an elderly Black man who has been enslaved for years by an Army doctor. He travels with his enslaver and spends a long time in several states, including some where slavery is illegal. After his enslaver dies, Scott sues the widow who now enslaves him. He argues that he should be considered free because he can prove that he lived for years in states where slavery was illegal.

The Dred Scott case is obviously an enormous hot potato, so it gets passed from one court to another until it reaches the United States Supreme Court. As you know, the Supreme Court is still a judicial body of immense prestige and enormous political power today. It considers the Dred Scott case and, by a majority vote, delivers an explosive ruling.

First, the Court declares that Dred Scott is enslaved and, because he is not a citizen, has no right to bring a case in federal court or sue anyone. Second—and this is even more important, though it may not sound that way to us—the Court rules that Congress cannot prohibit slavery in the federal territories. That invalidates the Missouri Compromise restriction, though it does not deny a state’s power to prohibit slavery. [N-028]

Do you see what that means? It establishes once and for all that the government in Washington has no right to ban slavery in the territories. This ruling is explosive too, and it sets off an enormous debate. Then we come to 1859 and the final episode. I know this is running long, but you can also see what it takes to summarize a story like this in an hour. Still, we are, so to speak, starting to wrap things up. Thank you.

## 12. John Brown, Republicans, and Lincoln’s Campaign

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-01130–U-01243 -->

In 1859, there is one last dramatic episode, and once again the central figure is John Brown. Brown has left Kansas, and now he intends to get serious: he wants to launch a slave revolt in the South. So he puts together a band of men and plans to seize the military arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, then hand out the weapons to Black people.

In the summer of 1859, Brown rents a farm near Harpers Ferry and gradually brings his men there. Once he has about twenty armed men, he seizes the virtually undefended arsenal on October 16. He begins sending free Black people from plantation to plantation, trying to persuade the slaves to rise up. No one moves.

The militia arrives. President Buchanan sends Marines from Washington under a colonel who will soon become famous: Robert E. Lee, the very man who, shortly afterward, will command the Southern army. The Marines exchange fire with Brown’s men. Most of Brown’s followers are killed; Brown is captured, tried, and sentenced to death. He is hanged on December 2, 1859.

He leaves behind a few lines declaring: “I John Brown am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land: will never be purged away; but with Blood. I had as I now think: vainly flattered myself that without very much bloodshed; it might be done.”

John Brown’s death creates a myth—a myth that, in fact, still exists today. On the day he is hanged, bells ring in his honor across the North, cannon fire salutes him, and the song is born.

The following year, 1860, brings an election. A new party enters the race to challenge the Democratic Party’s dominance. It recognizes that the West is no longer certain to side with the South. By now, the West shares too many interests with the North, and that old alliance can be broken. The new party sets out to unite Northern industrialists with Western settlers and put the slaveholding South in the minority. It presents itself as progressive and egalitarian, and it takes the name Republican Party.

As you can see, there’s a paradox here. The Republican and Democratic parties still exist today, but their political positions are, broadly speaking, the reverse of what they were then.

The Republicans enter the election with excellent prospects because the South and West really are drifting apart. The Democratic Party splits and runs two rival candidates. There is also an independent candidate. So the Republicans, you might say, hold all the cards.

To make sure they win, they need to carry the West, so they choose a Westerner as their candidate: Lincoln. He is a lawyer and a seasoned politician with experience in Illinois and Washington. Yet even though he is a professional politician, the party can still fashion him into a frontier figure—the settler’s son raised in a log cabin, the man who understands you and represents you.

Lincoln is a moderate candidate. The Republican platform is extremely moderate on slavery because the party knows perfectly well that radical abolitionism, while gaining ground, is still not a mass movement. Abolitionist ideas have not yet taken hold among most people.

Take Kansas—Bleeding Kansas—where supporters and opponents of slavery have been killing one another. In the end, the antislavery side wins. The Kansas constitution bans slavery but limits voting to white male citizens. Barring free Black people from living in Kansas is discussed, but in the end it is not included in the constitution. [N-029] Even so, for many whites opposing slavery really means: we don’t want competition from Black people.

Lincoln is also chosen because the Republicans hope he won’t frighten the South too much. He has never been an abolitionist. He knows, so to speak, that the problem will have to be confronted sooner or later, but he readily admits that he doesn’t know what to do about it. Let me quote one of his speeches: “If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do, as to the existing institution. My first impulse would be to free all the slaves, and send them to Liberia,---to their own native land. But a moment’s reflection would convince me, that whatever of high hope, as I think there is, there may be in this, in the long run, its sudden execution is impossible. [...] What next? Free them, and make them politically and socially, our equals? My own feelings will not admit of this; and if mine would, we well know that those of the great mass of white people will not.” That is Lincoln the politician.

So Lincoln’s platform says that he has no intention of interfering with slavery and that each state is sovereign and may decide what to do about it. At the same time, the Republican platform makes one thing clear: Southerners must stop talking about states’ rights. Secession? No. The federal government stands above the individual states. No state has the right to nullify federal law, and talk of secession is treason.

Economically, Lincoln’s platform gives Northern industrialists exactly what they want: massive investment, railroads heading west, and so on. The solid South wants nothing to do with Lincoln. Southern politicians portray him to their voters as the devil—an abolitionist, even a follower of John Brown. And you can imagine just how popular John Brown is in the South at that moment.

## 13. Lincoln’s Election and Southern Secession

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-01244–U-01312 -->

The result is that Lincoln wins the election in November—but he wins by carrying the North and the West. In nine Southern states, as I’ve been telling you, he doesn’t receive a single vote. Lincoln receives no popular votes in nine Southern states that hold a presidential vote because Republican ballots are often unavailable and the party has little or no organization there. Public voting and local pressure can also discourage people from openly supporting him. [N-030]

But he has been elected. He will be the next president. The Republicans have won in November. On December 20, South Carolina declares its independence. Before Lincoln’s inauguration in March, six more Southern states leave the Union. They all join together and form the Confederacy.

We’re at the end now, but let me quote a few more passages from President Lincoln’s inaugural address of March 4, 1861. “I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so. [...] I hold, that in contemplation of universal law, and of the Constitution, the Union of these States is perpetual. [...] no State, upon its own mere motion, can lawfully get out of the Union. [...] I therefore consider that, in view of the Constitution and the laws, the Union is unbroken; and, to the extent of my ability, I shall take care [...] that the laws of the Union be faithfully executed in all the States. [...] Plainly, the central idea of secession, is the essence of anarchy. A majority, held in restraint by constitutional checks, and limitations [...] is the only true sovereign of a free people. Whoever rejects it, does, of necessity, fly to anarchy or to despotism.”

“Physically speaking”—this is still Lincoln’s inaugural address—“Physically speaking, we cannot separate. We cannot remove our respective sections from each other, nor build an impassable wall between them.” Clearly, this is before Trump. “A husband and wife may be divorced, and go out of the presence, and beyond the reach of each other; but the different parts of our country cannot do this.”

Throughout the speech, Lincoln is really addressing only the people of the South. He is speaking directly to them, trying to persuade them to turn back. “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The government will not assail you. You can have no conflict, without being yourselves the aggressors. You have no oath registered in Heaven to destroy the government, while I shall have the most solemn one to preserve, protect and defend it.”

And then he ends this way: “I am loth to close. We are not enemies, but friends. We must not be enemies.”

Following Lincoln’s inauguration, the Southern states declare that all federal property within their territory is confiscated.

## 14. Fort Sumter and the War’s Legacy

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-01313–U-01348 -->

That includes military installations and fortifications. Almost everything is seized without a shot being fired. But in Charleston Harbor, South Carolina, one stubborn garrison refuses to abandon the fort placed in its care: Fort Sumter. The federal troops hold their ground and refuse to hand it over.

On April 12, South Carolina’s authorities order their forces to open fire on Fort Sumter. The garrison agrees to surrender the next day. Meanwhile, Lincoln has already asked for full authority to raise an army and, at first, to retake the fort. The surrender is agreed to on April 13, and the garrison evacuates on April 14. On April 15, Lincoln issues a proclamation calling for 75,000 militia volunteers to serve for ninety days. [N-031]

And so the American Civil War finally begins, after thirty years of talk about it.

Lincoln is shot on April 14, 1865, four years and one day after the surrender agreement at Fort Sumter. Lee has surrendered by then, though major Confederate forces elsewhere will surrender over the weeks and months that follow. [N-032]

Slavery has been abolished throughout the United States. The Thirteenth Amendment abolishes it without compensating enslavers. Reconstruction, especially the Fourteenth Amendment, establishes citizenship and equal protection in the face of sustained white resistance. [N-033]

It’s an enormous social advance, achieved even though no one had really imagined it and, until shortly before, hardly anyone had seriously wanted it. The white population is wholly unprepared for this outcome, and large majorities in both the North and the South don’t want equality. We still feel the consequences today. Thank you.
