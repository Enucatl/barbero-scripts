## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

<!-- chapter: CH-016; transcript: U-00676–U-00698 -->

The crusade ends so badly in part because of what happens during a major attack on the Saracens. The
Templar knights are supposed to hold the vanguard. By custom in the Holy Land, they go first into
the attack, the place of honor. But the king's brother, Robert, count of Artois, has some rather
overbearing advisers around him. Then Robert and his knights speed up, overtake the Templars, and
break the agreed order.

The Templars see the count of Artois overtaking them and begin spurring their horses too, because
heaven forbid they should fall behind; that cannot happen. Everyone behind them accelerates as well,
and the entire attack, planned as a succession of interventions by various groups of knights,
degenerates into a wild race because no one wants the shame of being the one left behind. It ends
very badly. The Turks surround them, the count of Artois is killed, almost all the Templars are
killed, and the crusade is lost.

So knightly arrogance sometimes causes serious trouble. But not every knight is so arrogant. Their
culture contains a constant tension between the cult of honor—the grand gesture, the need to show
off—and the professionalism of a soldier who knows that war must be conducted properly. The cult of
honor can also help someone fight well: it keeps you going and gives you courage when things get
dangerous.
