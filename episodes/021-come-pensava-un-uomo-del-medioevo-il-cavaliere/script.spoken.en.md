# How a Medieval Man Thought: The Knight

## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00062 -->

Good evening, everyone. This is our third episode in this attempt to get to know the people of the
Middle Ages. And as I say that, I realize I haven't talked about women. That's a real problem. The
overwhelming majority of people who wrote in the Middle Ages were men. There were women too, of
course, and perhaps I should have thought of that sooner. Next year, I promise, if you'd like. But
I've been lazy, because when we think about the Middle Ages, we tend to divide it into categories,
don't we? That's exactly what I've done here: the friar, the knight, the merchant, the peasant.

Women are harder to fit into these boxes. They have a social position, but they also have something
more, at least in this period. But again, perhaps we'll come back to them.

In this episode, the knight. The knight we're talking about is a Frenchman named Jean de Joinville.
He's a thirteenth-century knight, a contemporary of Friar Salimbene, whom we discussed in the
previous episode. Strictly speaking, Joinville is more than a knight: he commands knights and is a
great lord. Most knights are gentlemen who live well and have land, servants, and horses, but serve
someone else—a castellan or great lord who is also a knight, only at a higher level.

Joinville is that kind of great lord. He owns several castles and holds a lordship where he is the
public authority. He keeps order, collects taxes, and administers justice. On top of that, he is a
high royal official. For years Joinville serves the king of France as seneschal of Champagne, the
king's administrator and representative in the region.

But for all that, he is still a knight. Being a knight means being raised a certain way and sharing
certain values. Some are the ideals described in the medieval romances we all picture—and that they
read too—where the knight must protect widows and orphans, remain humble, and so on. In practice,
knights value rather different things. The values Joinville shares with his vassals are valor in
war, the physical courage to risk your skin in battle, comradeship among companions in arms, and
honor.

Jean de Joinville is famous among historians, and I can tell you about him because he did something
rather unusual for a knight: he wrote a book. And he wrote it for a specific reason. As I said, he
served the king of France for a long time, especially Louis IX—Saint Louis—the great
thirteenth-century king who went on two crusades and died during the second. Joinville accompanied
him on the first, an expedition that left in 1248 and returned six years later—those few who
returned, that is. Joinville didn't join the king's second crusade. Like many survivors of the
first, he preferred not to go. The king went and died there in 1270.

Joinville outlives his king by many years. By the beginning of the fourteenth century, he is an old
man living in his castle at Joinville, during the same period when Dino Compagni—the subject of the
previous episode—is caught up in Florentine politics. Joinville realizes he is one of the few people
left who remembers the saintly king. By then Louis IX really has become a saint: he has been
canonized and is officially Saint Louis. Joinville wants to write, someone asks him to write, and so
he produces this extraordinary book. It is supposed to be a life of Louis IX, but it is really the
memoir of Jean de Joinville, a great French lord of his time, telling above all the epic story of
the crusade.

So the man before us is not just a knight but a crusading knight. And I don't think it will surprise
anyone that religion occupies a central place in his worldview, especially when he looks back on the
crusade.

## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00063–U-00118 -->

Anyone who pictures a Middle Ages dominated by religion won't be disappointed here. For nearly
everyone, religion is a part of life experienced with an intensity few people today can imagine. And
I'm not talking only about Louis IX, who is eventually made a saint. I thought about giving you the
full list of Masses and divine offices he hears every day, but we'd spend far too long on it. He
hears several Masses and quite a few offices, sung and unsung, every single day. He is a saint.
Joinville is simply a knight and an ordinary lord, yet even he hears one Mass a day. A Christian's
day—a knight's day—begins with Mass.

Religion is always present, and these people have a natural familiarity with sacred things. Dogs
wandered into medieval churches, so we certainly aren't going to let one frighten us here. Let me
tell you an anecdote that shows how they think. They are returning from the crusade after six years
away, having seen most of their friends die. They sail back from Egypt and stop at Lampedusa along
the way.

Joinville recounts the episode this way: We came to an island called Lampedusa, where we caught a
great many rabbits. We found an ancient hermitage among the rocks and the garden made by the hermits
who had once lived there: it contained olive trees, fig trees, grapevines, and other trees. A stream
from the spring ran through the garden. The king and the rest of us went to the far end of the
garden and found an oratory in the first vault, whitewashed with lime, with a red earthen cross. We
entered the second vault and found the bodies of two dead people whose flesh had entirely decayed;
their ribs still held together, the bones of their hands lay upon their chests, and they lay facing
east, in the manner in which bodies are buried. When we reassembled aboard our ship, one of our
sailors was missing. The shipmaster supposed that he had remained there to become a hermit; and so
Nicholas of Soissons, the king's master sergeant, left three sacks of ship's biscuit on the shore,
so that the man might find them and live on them.

Just as it is normal for miracles to happen. You know, if you believe in miracles, they happen. At
that time practically everyone believed in them, and so they happened. It was easy to be certain
that you had witnessed a miracle. And witnessing a miracle was something important that remained
impressed on you, that you carried with you. Especially during a catastrophic crusade like Saint
Louis's, they needed many miracles to help the survivors return home.

Joinville reports what the rescued man told him and what he did afterward: One of the king's
galleys picked him up and brought him to our ship, where he told us how it had happened. I asked him
why he had made no effort to save himself, either by swimming or in any other way. He answered that
there was no need for him to make any effort, because, as soon as he began to fall, he commended
himself to Our Lady of Vauvert, and she supported him by the shoulders from the moment he fell until
the king's galley picked him up. In honor of this miracle, I had it painted in my chapel at
Joinville and in the windows at Blécourt.

He doesn't dwell on it—anyone can hear a story about a miracle—but when it happens to you, it stays
with you. He is the local lord, so he has the scene painted in his chapel and placed in the church
windows. This is their religious world: faith is a powerful bond that unites everyone, felt with
great fervor and great simplicity.

## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00119–U-00157 -->

Sometimes there is even too much simplicity. Joinville recounts an episode with great approval, I
must say. He says: once, they recently told me at the monastery of Cluny—you know Cluny in Burgundy,
the great Benedictine monastery—at the monastery of Cluny they had organized a great disputation
between clerics and Jews. Disputations were something—perhaps I already mentioned it when talking
about Friar Salimbene—public intellectual disputations were something that clerics, churchmen of the
Middle Ages, liked very much. They liked this opportunity to demonstrate how good they were by
facing opponents and arguing with them. Their brothers who had not become priests held tournaments
and demonstrated every day that they were the best by defeating an opponent. Churchmen liked doing
it too.

In fact, Louis IX told Joinville the story, and Joinville records it as follows: He told me that
there was a great disputation between clerics and Jews at the monastery of Cluny. There was a knight
there to whom the abbot had given bread in the house for God's sake. He asked the abbot to let him
speak first, and permission was granted only reluctantly. Then he rose, leaned on his staff, and
asked them to bring him the greatest scholar and foremost master among the Jews, which they did. He
asked him this question: 'Master,' said the knight, 'I ask whether you believe that the Virgin Mary,
who bore God in her womb and in her arms, gave birth as a virgin and is the mother of God.' The Jew
replied that he believed none of it. The knight answered that he had acted like a great fool by
entering her monastery and her house when he neither believed in her nor loved her. 'And truly,'
said the knight, 'you will pay for it.' Then he raised his crutch, struck the Jew beside the ear,
and knocked him to the ground. The Jews fled and carried away their master badly wounded; and that
was the end of the disputation.

Joinville then gives Louis’s conclusion: 'And so I tell you,' said the king, 'that no one should
dispute with them unless he is a very learned cleric. But when a layman hears the Christian faith
spoken ill of, he should defend the Christian faith only with his sword, which he should drive into
the offender's belly as far as it can go.'

This faith is spontaneous, impetuous, sometimes violent, and intensely felt. So it shouldn't
surprise you that it is also deeply attached to ritual and form. A simple faith means caring about
doing the right things—observing the Friday fast and the Lenten fast—and feeling terrible when you
fail. These practices have lost much of their importance in Christianity today, but Christians then
feel powerfully bound to observe them.

## 4. Meat on Friday: intention versus observance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00158–U-00197 -->

Joinville experiences this himself near the disastrous end of the crusade. The Turks have won,
massacred half the crusader army, and captured everyone else. Joinville describes these scenes with
extraordinary power: collapse, flight, chaos, surrender. And surrender is a gamble. You don't know
whose hands you'll fall into or what the Saracen Turks will do. Some see prisoners who are wounded
or sick, strike them on the head, and throw them into the sea. Others treat their prisoners very
courteously. The king and great lords receive courteous treatment, partly because they can pay large
ransoms. Christian and Muslim kings and lords understand one another; they belong to the same world
and have a great deal in common, as we'll see in a moment.

So things are already more or less over. Joinville is a prisoner and is having lunch with an emir
who has invited him to eat. The emir has invited him to lunch, and they are chatting through an
interpreter, naturally. They are happily discussing their respective family trees and mutual
acquaintances, because the emir is very interested in Emperor Frederick II. It emerges that
Joinville is related to Emperor Frederick II, and there they are, comparing acquaintances and
ancestors as they eat.

Joinville’s account is more precise: While we were eating, he had a citizen of Paris brought before
us. When the citizen arrived, he said to me, 'My lord, what are you doing?' 'Why, what am I doing?'
I said. 'In God's name,' he said, 'you are eating meat on a Friday!' When I heard this, I pushed my
bowl away. The admiral asked my Saracen why I had done that, and he told him; the admiral replied
that God would not hold it against me, since I had not done it knowingly. And you should know that
the legate gave me the same answer once we were out of prison. Yet that did not stop me from fasting
afterward on bread and water every Friday in Lent. The legate became very angry with me about this,
because I was the only great lord who had remained with the king.

## 5. Dangerous words: devils, oaths, and dismissal

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00198–U-00235 -->

The words people utter, for example. This is a curious world, because everyone constantly has God
and the devil on their lips. Everyone, constantly—everyone swears. When they have to say something,
they say by God, in God's name, by God's head, by God's wounds. Everyone has his own way of
swearing. Joinville often notes it: there are great lords who have a particular way of swearing.
There is one who swears by God's cap. Constantly.

And on the other hand, he says, everyone always has the devil on their lips: go to the devil, may
the devil take you. And this, Joinville says, is an ugly thing; it is one of the tragedies of our
fine kingdom of France. The saintly King Louis never mentioned either God or the devil. I never
heard him say these things. We, on the other hand, do. And mentioning God in this way is not
actually the most serious thing; what is serious is mentioning the devil. In fact, Joinville says:
in my household I established a rule. Anyone who lets a mention of the devil slip from his lips
either pays a forfeit or does penance, and among my servants this bad habit has practically
disappeared.

Mentioning God, by contrast, is acceptable. To reinforce a statement, you call God as your witness.
Joinville does it too, but sometimes it gets him into trouble. He tells another story from the
crusade, like most of the stories in this episode. The crusaders are on the move and pitch camp in a
new place each evening. One night, Joinville says, the knight who has selected that night's campsite
comes to him and says, “Sire”—in Old French, lords were called *sire*, not just kings—“Sire, this
evening I have lodged you better than last evening.”

The knight who chose the previous night's campsite hears this, jumps up, grabs him by the hair, and
says, “You are very bold to speak about something I do.” This is an enormous breach of courtesy. Two knights
brawling in front of their lord is the height of boorishness and bad manners. Joinville sees red and
says, “Out of my household now; for, so help me God, you will never be with me again.”

The man leaves in tears, then comes back and begs Joinville to forgive him. He brings friends who
plead with Joinville: come on, over something like this? Joinville says he would gladly forgive the
man, but he has sworn an oath. He said, so help me God, you will never enter my household again. He
can't simply take him back as though nothing happened. If the papal legate releases him from the
oath, Joinville will be delighted to take the knight back.

They take the case to the papal legate. The legate says, “I'm sorry. This is a valid oath, and there
were grounds for it. I can't simply release you from it as though it were nothing. It can't be
done.” And Joinville says this is how he learns to be careful about what he says: after this affair,
he never takes the knight back. This brings us to another striking aspect of this religious outlook,
one that Joinville expresses but that many others certainly share—

## 6. A practical bargain with God

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00236–U-00269 -->

—is another side of this practical outlook. Miracles happen, and people continually call on God as a
witness. More than that, they expect help from God, the Virgin, and the saints. When you're in
trouble, you invoke God and expect him to help. When you're risking your skin in battle, you invoke
God and expect help. This, Joinville says, is why we serve God.

Joinville describes and rejects the belief in these terms: Their belief is that no one can die
except on his appointed day, and for that reason they will not wear armor; and when they curse their
children, they say: 'May you be accursed like the Frank who arms himself for fear of death!' [...]
Since returning from overseas, I have seen in this country some disloyal Christians who held to the
Bedouins' law and said that no one could die except on his appointed day. Their belief is so
faithless that it amounts to saying that God has no power to help us. For those who served God would
be fools if we did not believe that he had the power to lengthen our lives and preserve us from harm
and misfortune; and we must believe in him, for he has the power to do all things. [...] Another
tenet is that they believe no one can die before the day appointed to him. No one should believe
this, because God has the power to lengthen and shorten our lives. The Bedouins believe this tenet,
and for that reason they will not wear armor when they go into battle, because they would think that
they were acting against the commandment of their law.

## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00270–U-00307 -->

Joinville describes his departure: The abbot of Cheminon gave me my pilgrim's scrip and staff; then
I departed from Joinville, without re-entering the castle until my return, on foot, barefoot, and
dressed in wool. In this way I went to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, and to the other holy relics that
are there. While I was going to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, I never once wished to turn my eyes back
toward Joinville, for fear that my heart would soften at the thought of the fine castle I was
leaving and of my two children.

And yet sacrifices are made, but common sense is always kept in mind. You do not go on crusade for
the pleasure of getting yourself killed. On the contrary, you go on crusade hoping in the end to
save your skin and return. These crusaders, these crusading knights, are not fanatical martyrs. And
this becomes absolutely clear precisely when the crusaders are routed and the Turks begin capturing
them.

Joinville records the company’s decision and the lone objection: It was not long before we saw four
of the sultan's galleys approaching, carrying fully a thousand men. I called my knights and my
people and asked what they wished us to do: surrender to the sultan's galleys or surrender to those
on shore. We all agreed that we preferred to surrender to the sultan's galleys, because they would
keep us together, rather than to those on shore, who would scatter us and sell us to the Bedouins.
Then one of my cellarers, who came from Doullens, said: 'My lord, I do not agree with this counsel.'
I asked what counsel he favored, and he told me: 'I favor letting ourselves all be killed; then we
shall all go to paradise.' But we did not believe him.

## 8. A saint keeps his word—and takes a joke

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00308–U-00361 -->

—which is something that rather surprises Joinville, because, after all, keeping your word,
certainly—but with those people? And yet it happened; he saw it himself. After their captivity they
are paying the ransom. There have been long negotiations with the Saracens who captured them, and in
the end they have agreed on a very large sum, 200,000 livres tournois, an enormous sum, to free the
king and the great lords. The money has arrived from France, and it has taken days and days to count
and hand it over.

Joinville records the exchange and Louis’s response: Then Sir Philippe de Nemours told the king
that the Saracens had been undercounted by one balance-load of ten thousand livres. The king became
extremely angry and said that he wished the ten thousand livres to be returned to them, because he
had promised to pay them two hundred thousand livres before leaving the river. I then trod on Sir
Philippe's foot and told the king not to believe him, because what he said was untrue: the Saracens
were the most skillful counters in the world. Sir Philippe said that I spoke the truth, because he
had said it only as a joke. The king said, 'May misfortune befall such a joke. And I command you,
Sir Philippe, by the fealty you owe me as my man, that if the ten thousand livres have not been
paid, you are to have them paid without fail.' Many people had advised the king to withdraw to his
ship waiting at sea, to remove himself from the Saracens' hands. The king would listen to no one;
instead, he said that he would not leave the river, just as he had promised, until he had paid them
two hundred thousand livres.

So, as you can see, it was not very easy to live with this king. I began by telling you about a
crusading knight with a profound religiosity, but having a profound religiosity is one thing and
having to live with a saintly king who pushes things to the extreme is another. Moreover, when
Joinville writes as an old man, it is official by then: the king is a saint; he has been canonized.
But when these things happened, when they were both young, yes, the king already had this reputation
for being a saint, but you understand the difference. Joinville greatly admired him and loved him
very much, but working with him was not at all easy. Opportunities for conflict arose constantly.

Joinville tells the episode this way: In the course of our marches we came to the sands of Acre,
where the king and the army encamped. There a large company from Greater Armenia came to me on
pilgrimage to Jerusalem, paying a heavy tribute to the Saracens who were conducting them. Through an
interpreter who knew their language and ours, they asked me to show them the holy king. I went to
the king, who was sitting in a pavilion, leaning against its pole; he sat on the sand without a
carpet or anything else beneath him. I said to him: 'My lord, outside there is a large company from
Greater Armenia going to Jerusalem, and they ask me to show them the holy king; but I have no
intention of kissing your bones just yet.' He laughed very openly and told me to go and fetch them,
which I did. When they had seen the king, they commended him to God, and the king did the same for
them.

## 9. Crusading accounts and a payment in patience

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00362–U-00419 -->

And perhaps I should pause here, because I am talking about crusading knights, and shortly we will
talk about martial valor, courtesy, and chivalric ideals. So far we have been talking about
religious faith, crusades, martyrdom. Money, it would seem, should have nothing to do with any of
this. Wrong. Money has a great deal to do with it. A crusade costs an enormous amount and is a great
enterprise that must be planned, and the very first thing is to find the money. Once the money is
there, you can begin organizing the army, hiring knights, recruiting people, building ships, and so
on. It is extremely expensive. Money enters into everything.

Joinville’s own figures show the financial scale of his commitment: Because I did not wish to carry
away any money wrongfully, I went and mortgaged a large amount of my land at Metz in Lorraine. You
should know that on the day I left our country for the Holy Land I did not hold land worth a
thousand livres a year, because my lady mother was still alive; and I went as one of ten knights and
one of three bannerets. I remind you of these things because, if God had not helped me—he who never
failed me—I could scarcely have endured for so long as the six years I remained in the Holy Land.
[...] I, who did not have land worth a thousand livres a year, took responsibility when I went
overseas for a company of ten knights including myself, with two other knights bearing banners. When
I arrived in Cyprus, after paying for my ship, I had only two hundred and forty livres tournois
left. Some of my knights sent word that unless I found money, they would leave me. God, who never
failed me, then provided for me in this way: the king, who was at Nicosia, sent for me, retained me
in his service, and put eight hundred livres in my coffers; and then I had more money than I
needed.

When the earlier arrangement expired, Joinville and Louis made a new bargain: While the king was
fortifying Caesarea, I went to his quarters to see him. As soon as he saw me enter his chamber,
where he was speaking with the legate, he rose, drew me aside, and said: 'You know,' said the king,
'that I retained you only until Easter; so I ask you to tell me what I should give you to remain
with me from Easter for one year.' I told him that I did not wish him to give me any more of his
money than he had given me before, but that I wished to make a different bargain with him.
'Because,' I said, 'you become angry whenever someone asks you for something, I want you to agree
that if I ask you for anything during this whole year, you will not become angry; and if you refuse
me, I will not become angry either.' When he heard this, he began to laugh very openly and told me
that he retained me on that condition. He took me by the hand, led me to the legate and his council,
and recounted to them the bargain we had made. They were very pleased, because I was the richest man
in the army.

## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00420–U-00458 -->

Saint Louis constantly demonstrates his holiness; people are astonished. At Sidon in 1253, on the
Levantine coast, Louis helps carry decomposing Christian bodies into burial trenches. [N-008] In a
separate episode in 1250, he refuses to escape alone by galley because doing so would leave hundreds
of people stranded in Cyprus.

When urged to leave others behind and save himself, Louis answers: Then the king said: 'Lords, I
have heard your advice and the advice of my people; now I will tell you mine. If I leave the ship,
there are five hundred people or more aboard who, fearing danger to their lives—for there is not one
among them who does not love his life as much as I love mine—will remain on the island of Cyprus,
and who may perhaps never return to their own countries. Therefore I would rather place my own
person, my wife, and my children in God's hands than inflict such harm on so great a number of
people as are aboard.'

Joinville records both his blunt answer and Louis’s later rebuke: 'Now I ask you,' he said, 'which
would you rather: that you were a leper, or that you had committed a mortal sin?' And I, who never
lied to him, answered that I would rather have committed thirty than be a leper. When the friars had
left, he called me aside alone, made me sit at his feet, and said: 'Why did you say that to me
yesterday?' I told him that I still said the same. He said: 'You spoke like a rash fool, for you
should know that there is no leprosy so hideous as being in mortal sin, because the soul that is in
mortal sin resembles the Devil; therefore no leprosy can be so hideous. It is true that when a man
dies he is cured of the leprosy of the body. But when a man who has committed a mortal sin dies, he
does not know and cannot be certain that in his lifetime he repented in such a way that God pardoned
him; therefore he should greatly fear that this leprosy will last as long as God is in paradise. So
I beg you as earnestly as I can, for love of God and of me, to set your heart on preferring every
bodily misfortune—leprosy and every other illness—to mortal sin entering your soul.'

On washing the feet of the poor, Joinville records this exchange: He asked me whether I washed the
feet of the poor on Maundy Thursday. 'My lord,' I said, 'God forbid! I will never wash the feet of
those common people.' 'Truly,' he said, 'that was badly said, for you must not disdain what God did
for our instruction. So I ask you, first for the love of God and then for love of me, to accustom
yourself to washing them.'

## 11. Noble blood against Robert de Sorbon

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00459–U-00506 -->

—of the life and mentality of these knights: this aristocratic mentality according to which society
is divided, and there are gentlemen, nobles, knights, lords, and they are the best people there are.
Then there are the villains. Everyone else is a villain, everyone who works—not only peasants
digging in the fields but also merchants and financiers, people who may have made money, perhaps a
great deal of money, perhaps more than the knights, as is beginning to happen. It is an ignoble
thing, because villains, everyone knows, are ignoble people. The only respectable people are nobles,
gentlemen, knights. This is rooted extremely deeply, almost automatically, in the minds of people
like Joinville.

There are countless episodes of this kind. Robert de Sorbon rises from a non-noble background to
become a master of theology and royal cleric. In 1253 he founds a theological college, royally
confirmed in 1257, that becomes known as the Sorbonne; he does not found the University of Paris.
[N-009] Joinville is at the king's court when Master Robert sees him arrive, takes him by the hem of
his garment, and pulls him before the king. Joinville is rather surprised: what the devil is
happening—“the devil,” indeed, because sometimes it slips out.

Joinville records the confrontation with Robert de Sorbon: Master Robert de Sorbon came to fetch me
there, took me by the hem of my cloak, and led me to the king; all the other knights followed us. I
asked Master Robert, 'Master Robert, what do you want with me?' He said: 'I want to ask you this: if
the king were sitting in this meadow and you went and sat on his bench higher than he, should you
rightly be blamed for it?' I said yes. He said: 'Then you are indeed to blame when you are more
nobly dressed than the king, for you dress in vair fur and green cloth, which the king does not.' I
said: 'Master Robert, saving your grace, I am not to blame if I dress in green and vair, for my
father and mother left me this mode of dress. But you are to blame, for you are the son of a villein
man and a villein woman, and you have abandoned the dress of your father and mother and are dressed
in richer camelin cloth than the king.' Then I took the hem of his surcoat and the hem of the king's
and said to him: 'Now see whether I am telling the truth.' The king then began to defend Master
Robert verbally with all his might.

Joinville says Louis later reconsidered his intervention: Then he told me that he had called us so
that he could confess to me that he had wrongly defended Master Robert against me. 'But,' he said,
'I saw him so bewildered that he badly needed my help. Still, do not attach importance to anything I
said in Master Robert's defense, for, as the seneschal says, you ought to dress well and neatly, so
that your wives will love you the more and your people will esteem you more. For, as the wise man
says, one should adorn oneself in clothing and arms in such a way that the worthy men of this world
do not say one does too much, nor the young people of this world say one does too little.'

A mentality that will last for centuries, incidentally. It will take not merely the French
Revolution but well into the nineteenth century before this idea—that they are an intrinsically
superior class and everyone else is worth little—begins to fade among the European nobility. And
yet—

## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00507–U-00549 -->

—Joinville knows, as I said before, that some of these newly rich villains are beginning to make so
much money that they become important people, buy castles, and occupy important positions at court.
This is difficult to accept. Nothing is more entertaining than seeing these villains who have risen
in the world put back in their place.

Joinville’s actual anecdote names Artaud de Nogent and runs as follows: Artaud de Nogent was the
burgess in all the world whom the count trusted most, and he was so rich that he built the castle of
Nogent-l'Artaud with his own money. [...] At the foot of the steps a poor knight came before him,
knelt, and said: 'My lord, I beg you for God's sake to give me something of yours with which I may
marry my two daughters, whom you see here.' Artaud, who was walking behind him, said to the poor
knight: 'Sir knight, you are behaving discourteously by asking my lord, for he has given so much
that he has nothing left to give.' The generous count turned toward Artaud and said: 'Sir villein,
what you say—that I have nothing left to give—is not true. I still have you yourself. Here, sir
knight: I give him to you, and I will guarantee him to you.' The knight was not taken aback; instead
he seized Artaud by the cloak and said he would not release him until Artaud had settled with him.
Before Artaud escaped him, he had settled with the knight for five hundred livres.

Joinville describes the judgment imposed on the royal sergeant Le Goulu: The third judgment that I
saw given at Caesarea was this. One of the king's sergeants, named Le Goulu, laid hands on a knight
of my company. I went to complain to the king. The king told me that, in his view, I could well let
the matter pass, since the sergeant had only shoved him. I said I would not let it pass, and that if
the king did not give me justice I would leave his service, since his sergeants were shoving
knights. He had justice done for me, and the judgment, according to the customs of the country, was
this: the sergeant came to my quarters barefoot, wearing only his shirt and breeches, with a bare
sword in his hand. He knelt before the knight, took the sword by its point, offered the pommel to
the knight, and said: 'My lord, I make amends to you for laying hands on you, and I have brought you
this sword so that you may cut off my fist, if it pleases you.' I asked the knight to forgive his
resentment against the sergeant, and he did so.

## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00550–U-00588 -->

—in their eyes, I mean, because perhaps it cannot be justified, but in their eyes how is this idea
justified, that nobles are the salt of the earth, that knights are the best there are and everyone
else counts for nothing? Fundamentally, the issue is that nobles see themselves as—and largely
are—the people who wage war, the people who risk their skin. Others work and make money, but a
knight's profession is risking his life: for his lord, his companions in arms, loyalty, the cause,
the cross when he goes on crusade.

A roughly contemporary didactic poem gives the maxim in a different form: Whoever goes on without
learning to ride and remains at school until the age of twelve is thereafter poorly suited to
knighthood: he lacks the strength for it. The unridden schoolboy is fit for nothing but the
priesthood.

Being at war means remaining there for hours and hours in heat, cold, and frost, wearing armor, with
nothing to drink or eat. Nobles tell themselves these things. In truth, if you look at armies, there
are also infantrymen, domestics, servants, people on foot, who are often killed first when things go
badly. But nobles do not even see them; they see themselves on their horses, wearing their helmets.

Joinville describes Louis’s landing and appearance in battle: When the king heard that the standard
of Saint-Denis was ashore, he strode rapidly through his vessel and would not stop for the legate
who was with him. He leapt into the sea, where the water came up to his armpits, and went with his
shield at his neck, his helmet on his head, and his lance in his hand until he reached his men on
the seashore. When he came ashore and saw the Saracens, he asked what people they were. He was told
they were Saracens; he lowered the lance beneath his armpit, put his shield before him, and would
have charged the Saracens if the worthy men with him had allowed it. [...] Never did I see so fine
an armed man, for he stood out above all his people from the shoulders upward, with a gilded helmet
on his head and a German sword in his hand.

Naturally, there are other things that are also supposed to justify their privileges. They should be
the best mannered, most courteous and refined, the people who know how to behave. In part it is
true: they attach importance to these things. Then the scenes Joinville recounts show us that
reality was often different.

## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-00589–U-00649 -->

Joinville recounts the scene and its aftermath: On the eve of Shrove Tuesday I saw a marvel that I
wish to recount to you, for that same day Sir Hugh of Landricourt, who served under my banner, was
buried. While he lay on his bier in my chapel, six of my knights were leaning on several sacks full
of barley. Because they were talking loudly in my chapel and making noise that disturbed the priest,
I went to tell them to be quiet, and told them it was a disgraceful thing for knights and gentlemen
to talk while Mass was being sung. They began to laugh at me and told me, laughing, that they were
arranging the remarriage of his wife. I reproached them and told them that such words were neither
good nor proper, and that they had quickly forgotten their companion. God took such vengeance on
them that the next day was the great battle of Shrove Tuesday, in which they were killed or mortally
wounded; consequently all six of their wives had to remarry.

Joinville explicitly criticizes Louis’s distance from his family: The queen, who had recently
recovered from giving birth to Lady Blanche at Jaffa, arrived at Sidon by sea. When I heard she had
arrived, I rose from before the king, went to meet her, and brought her to the castle. When I
returned to the king, who was in his chapel, he asked whether the queen and the children were well,
and I said they were. He said to me: “I know well that when you rose from before me, you went to
meet the queen; that is why I had the sermon held for you.” I remind you of these things because I
had already been around him for five years, yet I had never heard him speak of the queen or his
children, either to me or to anyone else; and it was not good conduct, as it seems to me, to be
aloof from one's wife and children.

If we wanted—well, we still have five minutes. There were reasons for this in King Louis, and he is
another figure who would yield interesting results if one applied a little psychoanalysis. The king
lost his father very young and remained for years under his mother's guardianship. Queen Blanche of
Castile was a formidable woman of power who governed the kingdom for years while he was a child.
When he grew up he took control of the kingdom himself, but Blanche of Castile, his mother, was
always present and controlled everything. The king had to marry, because a king must marry and
produce heirs. But Blanche did not get along with her daughter-in-law and did everything she could
to keep her away from her husband.

Joinville describes the arrangement at Pontoise: Queen Blanche's harsh treatment of Queen Margaret
went so far that, as much as she could, Blanche would not allow her son to be in his wife's company
except in the evening, when he went to bed with her. The residence where the king and queen most
liked to stay was Pontoise, because the king's chamber was above and the queen's chamber below. They
arranged matters so that they held their conversations on a spiral staircase leading from one
chamber to the other. They had also arranged that when the ushers saw Queen Blanche coming to the
chamber of the king, her son, they struck the doors with their staffs, and the king came running
back to his chamber so that his mother would find him there. The ushers of Queen Margaret's chamber
did the same when Queen Blanche came there, so that she would find Queen Margaret in her chamber.

Joinville records the reactions to Blanche of Castile’s death: At Sidon news came to the king that
his mother had died. He grieved so deeply that for two days no one was able to speak to him. After
that, he sent one of his chamber servants to fetch me. When I came before him in his chamber, where
he was all alone, he saw me, stretched out his arms, and said: “Ah, Seneschal, I have lost my
mother!” [...] My lady Marie of Vertus, a very good and holy woman, came to tell me that the queen
was grieving deeply, and asked me to go to comfort her. When I arrived, I found her weeping, and I
told her that whoever says one should not believe a woman speaks truly: “For the woman you hated
most is dead, and yet you are grieving like this!” She told me that she was not weeping for Blanche,
but because of the distress the king was suffering in his grief, and for her daughter—who later
became queen of Navarre—who had been left in the custody of men.

## 15. Adults at play and chivalry recapitulated

<!-- chapter: CH-015; transcript: U-00650–U-00675 -->

You may not know that adults in the Middle Ages play a great deal. Some of their games are the same
ones we play: chess, checkers, and backgammon. They play for money—even chess—because it's more fun.
They gamble heavily at dice, although the king doesn't tolerate it. They also play games that adults
rarely play today. When knights and ladies are together and get bored, they may play blind man's
buff or hide-and-seek. No one finds this strange. People enjoy themselves, and adults aren't ashamed
to have fun like children.

Joinville’s version includes both the small ballista and a second prank: I will tell you about the
games the count of Eu used to play on us. I had built a room where my knights and I ate by the light
from the doorway. The doorway faced the count of Eu's quarters; and he, being very ingenious, made a
small ballista that shot into the room. He had someone watch for when we were seated at our meal,
then set his ballista lengthwise toward our table and had it fired, breaking our pots and glasses. I
had stocked up on hens and capons, and I do not know who had given him a young bear, which he let
loose among my hens; it had killed a dozen before anyone could get there, while the woman who looked
after them beat the bear with her distaff.

## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

<!-- chapter: CH-016; transcript: U-00676–U-00698 -->

The crusade ends so badly in part because of what happens during a major attack on the Saracens. The
Templar knights are supposed to hold the vanguard. By custom in the Holy Land, they go first into
the attack, the place of honor. But the king's brother, Robert, count of Artois, has some rather
overbearing advisers around him. Then Robert and his knights speed up, overtake the Templars, and
break the agreed order.

The Templars see the count of Artois overtaking them and begin spurring their horses too, because
heaven forbid they should fall behind; that cannot happen. Everyone behind them accelerates as well,
and the entire attack, planned as a succession of interventions by various groups of knights,
degenerates into a wild race because no one wants the shame of being the one left behind. It ends
very badly. The Turks surround them, the count of Artois is killed, almost all the Templars are
killed, and the crusade is lost.

So knightly arrogance sometimes causes serious trouble. But not every knight is so arrogant. Their
culture contains a constant tension between the cult of honor—the grand gesture, the need to show
off—and the professionalism of a soldier who knows that war must be conducted properly. The cult of
honor can also help someone fight well: it keeps you going and gives you courage when things get
dangerous.

## 17. “We shall speak of this day in the ladies' chambers”

<!-- chapter: CH-017; transcript: U-00699–U-00726 -->

During the battle of Mansurah on February 8, 1250, Joinville and the count of Soissons help hold a
small bridge so attackers cannot strike Louis's forces from front and rear. Other knights and royal
sergeants are nearby, and royal crossbowmen eventually drive the attackers off; this is not the
army's later April retreat. [N-015]

At the bridge, Joinville attributes this remark to the count of Soissons: The good count of
Soissons, there where we stood, joked with me and said: “Seneschal, let this pack of curs howl; for
by God's coif!—that was how he swore—you and I will yet speak of this day in the ladies' chambers.”

This is one of those cases in which chivalric culture—honor and pride, but also courtly love,
boasting, but boasting after winning, not after losing—comes together. We will win, we will return
alive, we will remember this day, and in the ladies' chambers we will make ourselves look splendid
by recounting our adventures. And Joinville lived so long that he did indeed manage to tell his
adventures, and I have tried, in a small way, to tell them to you too. Thank you, thank you.
