# How a Medieval Man Thought: The Knight — assembled faithful translation

## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00062 -->

Good evening. Well, good evening, everyone. This evening we are at our third appointment, so to speak, in this attempt to get to know the people of the Middle Ages. As I say that, I realize that I haven't talked about women. There is a big problem here. The overwhelming majority of the people who wrote in the Middle Ages were men. There were women too, and perhaps I could have thought of that sooner. Next year, I promise you, if you like, here. But I have been lazy, because when we think about the Middle Ages, we always think of it as divided up a little like this, into categories, don't we? Just as I have done here. The friar, the knight, the merchant, the peasant—with women it is more difficult. Women are, how can I put it, they have a social position, but they also have something more. They are harder to put into boxes, at least in that period. But I repeat, perhaps we will talk about them again.

This evening, the knight. The knight we are talking about is a Frenchman. His name is Jean de Joinville. He is a knight of the thirteenth century, a contemporary of that Salimbene, that Friar Salimbene we talked about the day before yesterday. To tell the truth, he is something more than a knight: he is a leader of knights, a great lord. You know, most knights are gentlemen who live well, who have land, servants, horses, but who are in someone's service. They are in the service of a castellan, of a great lord, who is also a knight himself, but, in short, a knight at a somewhat higher level. Joinville is a man like that. He is someone who owns several castles, and therefore he possesses a lordship where he is the lord, where he is the public authority. He keeps order, collects taxes, administers justice, and on top of that he is also a high official of the king. Joinville served the king of France for years as seneschal of Champagne, that is, as the king's administrator and representative in the region of Champagne.

And with all that, he is a knight. Being a knight means having been educated in a certain way, sharing certain values, which perhaps are also the ones described in the romances, the medieval romances we all have in mind and which they also read, where it says that the knight must protect widows and orphans, be humble, and so forth. Then, in reality, the true values of the knights are rather different. The real values that Joinville shares with his vassals are valor in war, physical courage demonstrated in battle when you risk your skin, comradeship among companions in arms, honor.

That said, Jean de Joinville is famous among us historians, and I can talk to you about him this evening, because he did something that was rather unusual for knights. He wrote a book. He wrote a book for a precise reason. As I was telling you, he served the king of France for a long time, and the king he served most of all was Louis IX, Saint Louis, the great thirteenth-century king of France who took part in two crusades and then died on the second. Joinville accompanied him on the first crusade, an expedition that left in 1248 and returned—those few who returned—six years later. Joinville did not go on the king's second crusade. Like many survivors of the first one, he preferred not to go. The king went and died there in the year Seventy.

Joinville outlived his king by many years. When he was already old, at the beginning of the fourteenth century, in the same years when Dino Compagni, whom I told you about yesterday, was living through Florentine politics, in those same years, in his castle at Joinville, he realized that he was one of the few people left who remembered the saintly king. In the meantime, Louis IX had really been made a saint: they had canonized him, and by then he was officially a saint. And so Joinville felt the desire to write, and someone asked him to write, and he wrote—he wrote this extraordinary book that was supposed to be a life of Louis IX and that is in reality the memoirs of Jean de Joinville, of this great French lord of his time, telling above all this epic story of the crusade.

And so, and so he is not only a knight but a crusading knight, the man we have before us this evening. And I don't think I will surprise anyone if I say that in the worldview of this crusading knight, who moreover is specifically remembering the time of the crusade, well, in this worldview religion occupies a central place.

## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00063–U-00118 -->

Anyone who has in mind, let's say, a Middle Ages dominated by religion will not be disappointed here. It is true that for many people, almost everyone, religion was in any case a dimension of life experienced with an intensity that few people today can imagine. I am not talking only about Louis IX, who was in fact made a saint. Earlier I thought I would tell you, I would give you the list of all the masses and divine offices that Louis IX heard every day of his life. Then I thought we would waste too much time on this long list, but the king heard several masses and quite a few divine offices, sung and unsung, every day. He was a saint. Joinville, who is a knight, an ordinary lord, nevertheless hears one mass a day. A Christian's day, a knight's day, begins with mass.

And religion is always present. There is a spontaneous familiarity with religious things that is characteristic of these people. Dogs used to enter churches in the Middle Ages, so we certainly are not going to let ourselves be frightened here. I will tell you an anecdote that gives you an idea of how they reasoned. They are returning from the crusade after being away for six years and seeing most of their friends die. Returning from the crusade, which took place in Egypt, they travel back by sea and at a certain point stop at Lampedusa, as naturally happens to people who leave Egypt and travel by sea.

They stop at Lampedusa and find it deserted; no one lives there. But there are remains—or rather, there is still a hermitage. They immediately understand that it is a hermitage; you can see that it is a hermitage. It is a little isolated house with its vegetable garden, its garden where there are fig trees, vines, olive trees, and then a chapel, an oratory, which is what makes it clear that it is a hermitage. There is a whitewashed oratory with a crucifix, and there is no one there. In the next room there are two skeletons. There are two dead people who were laid out after death and left there, and they are evidently the hermits who lived in that place.

They are all deeply moved. Then they take on water and return to the galleys to leave. As they are about to depart, they realize that a sailor is missing. A sailor is missing. They wait and wait, but he does not return. Finally, the people who know him say, well, in our opinion he has stayed here to become a hermit himself. This opportunity has arisen; until a moment ago, who knows what else he thought he would do with his life, and instead he was so struck by the discovery of this deserted, abandoned hermitage that he decided to become a hermit. And this seems natural to everyone. Everyone says, yes, of course, evidently that is what happened. They leave crates of ship's biscuit for him on the beach so that he can feed himself for a while, and then some other ship will arrive and continue to support the hermit, and they leave. And to them it is a normal event.

Just as it is normal for miracles to happen. You know, if you believe in miracles, they happen. At that time practically everyone believed in them, and so they happened. It was easy to be certain that you had witnessed a miracle. And witnessing a miracle was something important that remained impressed on you, that you carried with you. Especially during a catastrophic crusade like Saint Louis's, they needed many miracles to help the survivors return home.

Joinville personally witnessed a miracle. Again during the return journey, at a certain point, on the ship in front of his, someone climbed up somewhere to do some work on the ship and fell into the water. And Joinville says: from the ship behind, we could see this thing in the water, but it was motionless, so much so that we thought it was a package, a piece of furniture, something that had fallen there, an object that had fallen into the sea. In any case, we put the boat in the water to go and recover it. We discover that it is a man, a sailor, alive and well and perfectly fine. And we ask him, but why weren't you swimming? Why weren't you trying to move around, to call out?

And the sailor says—Joinville heard him with his own ears—he said, but there was no need, because when I felt myself falling into the sea, I invoked the Virgin. The Virgin appeared to me and held me up, and I felt that she was holding me up and keeping me afloat, so I was calm. He witnessed a miracle with his own eyes. He tells it like that, *en passant*; it could have ended there. Instead, in the next sentence he says: and I had it painted in the chapel at Joinville and in the windows of the church at Blécourt, which is the other village that belongs to him.

This means that even if he does not insist on it now—because hearing about a miracle can happen to anyone, after all—when it really happens to you, it is something you feel inside. He is the lord of the village; he has it painted as a fresco in the church, he has it placed in the windows, and so on. This, then, is the world in which they live from a religious point of view, where religion is a great bond that unites everyone and is felt with extreme—with, how can I put it—with great fervor and also great simplicity.

## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00119–U-00157 -->

Sometimes there is even too much simplicity. Joinville recounts an episode with great approval, I must say. He says: once, they recently told me at the monastery of Cluny—you know Cluny in Burgundy, the great Benedictine monastery—at the monastery of Cluny they had organized a great disputation between clerics and Jews. Disputations were something—perhaps I already mentioned it when talking about Friar Salimbene—public intellectual disputations were something that clerics, churchmen of the Middle Ages, liked very much. They liked this opportunity to demonstrate how good they were by facing opponents and arguing with them. Their brothers who had not become priests held tournaments and demonstrated every day that they were the best by defeating an opponent. Churchmen liked doing it too.

So at the monastery of Cluny they organize a theological disputation between some clerics and some rabbis. And there was, Joinville says, they told me, an old knight who lived there at the monastery. The abbot supported him as an act of charity. It often happened that a knight who had spent his life fighting and throwing punches, in old age—by then he could no longer manage physically, and perhaps he had also had enough of that life—often in old age they went to live in a monastery. Those who had something gave their property to the monastery; those who had nothing could also be taken in as an act of charity, and they spent the last years of their lives in the monastery.

So Joinville says, they told me that when the disputation was about to begin, this knight stepped forward and asked to be allowed to dispute with the rabbi first. The abbot was not very pleased, but the other man insisted, and he had to tell him: all right, you begin. Then the knight, leaning on his crutch, very courteously asks the rabbi, Master, do you believe in the Virgin Mary, who gave birth to Our Lord God and remained a virgin? And the rabbi, Joinville says, replied that they did not believe it at all. And the knight replied, you are doing very wrong. And he raised the crutch and dealt the rabbi a great blow on the head. And the Jews carried the rabbi away, and the disputation ended there.

And Joinville discussed this matter with the saintly King Louis. And the two of them agreed that the knight had done exactly the right thing. Because disputing with unbelievers is already dangerous, after all, but learned men can do it, wise men who are able to hold their own against them. Because if it goes badly, if the Jews win the public disputation, well, there might even be some good Christian with confused ideas at that point, and therefore one has to be careful. Laypeople must not involve themselves in these things. Laypeople, the saintly king tells him, when they hear the Christian faith spoken ill of, must draw their swords.

So this faith, so spontaneous, so impetuous too, so violent, so intense at times—well, you will not be surprised if I tell you that it is also a faith very attached to forms, very formalistic. Because, precisely, a naive faith also means that, in short, I care about doing the right things, about observing the Friday fast, the Lenten fast, and if I do not observe these things, then I truly feel terrible. These are things that have lost importance in Christianity today, whereas at the time Christians felt powerfully obliged to observe all of them.

## 4. Meat on Friday: intention versus observance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00158–U-00197 -->

Joinville himself happened to experience this personally. We are during the crusade—or rather, toward the end of the crusade, which has already ended very badly. The Turks have won, massacred half the crusader army, and captured all the others. These are scenes that Joinville, incidentally, describes with extraordinary power: these moments of collapse, flight, chaos, people surrendering—and surrendering is a gamble, because you do not know whose hands you will fall into, and it is difficult to predict what the Saracen Turks will do. There are some who, when they see prisoners who are a little battered, sick, and so on, give them a blow on the head and throw them into the sea. And then there are others who treat the prisoners very courteously. In any case, the king and the great lords are always treated very courteously, that goes without saying, partly because they will pay a large ransom to be freed. But in any case Christian or Muslim kings and great lords understand one another; they belong to the same world and have many subjects in common, as we will see in a moment.

So things are already more or less over. Joinville is a prisoner and is having lunch with an emir who has invited him to eat. The emir has invited him to lunch, and they are chatting through an interpreter, naturally. They are happily discussing their respective family trees and mutual acquaintances, because the emir is very interested in Emperor Frederick II. It emerges that Joinville is related to Emperor Frederick II, and there they are, comparing acquaintances and ancestors as they eat.

At a certain point another captive crusader happens by, a French townsman, Joinville says. He sees me and says to me, “But my lord, what are you doing?” And I say, quite cheerfully, “Why, excuse me, what am I doing?” “For the love of God, my lord, you are eating meat on a Friday.” Joinville jumps up and pushes his bowl away, horrified, because, you understand, amid the chaos, the battle, the capture, and everything else, he had forgotten what day it was. It was Friday, and he was eating meat.

The emir, who witnesses this scene, has the interpreter ask him what happened. Joinville explains it, and the Muslim tells him, “Don't worry; God cannot be angry with you. You did not do it on purpose.” But Joinville feels bad about it. He feels bad about having done this, and as soon as he can, he goes to consult the papal legate attached to the crusader army, a high-ranking prelate. He tells him everything. The high-ranking prelate tells him the same thing the Muslim told him: God cannot be angry with you; you did not do it on purpose; don't worry.

Joinville does not believe it. He feels this thing weighing on him, and he says: I began fasting on bread and water every Friday to free myself, to purify myself of this thing I had done. So forms are important. Forms are important; they must not be violated. And it is a religiosity that, although, as we have seen, it is extremely intense and spontaneous, is also extremely attached to observing formal rules.

## 5. Dangerous words: devils, oaths, and dismissal

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00198–U-00235 -->

The words people utter, for example. This is a curious world, because everyone constantly has God and the devil on their lips. Everyone, constantly—everyone swears. When they have to say something, they say by God, in God's name, by God's head, by God's wounds. Everyone has his own way of swearing. Joinville often notes it: there are great lords who have a particular way of swearing. There is one who swears by God's cap. Constantly.

And on the other hand, he says, everyone always has the devil on their lips: go to the devil, may the devil take you. And this, Joinville says, is an ugly thing; it is one of the tragedies of our fine kingdom of France. The saintly King Louis never mentioned either God or the devil. I never heard him say these things. We, on the other hand, do. And mentioning God in this way is not actually the most serious thing; what is serious is mentioning the devil. In fact, Joinville says: in my household I established a rule. Anyone who lets a mention of the devil slip from his lips either pays a forfeit or does penance, and among my servants this bad habit has practically disappeared.

Mentioning God, by contrast, is acceptable. To reinforce his statements, a person calls God as a witness. Joinville did it too, but sometimes it gets you into trouble. Joinville tells a story, again during the crusade, like most of the things I am telling you this evening. The crusaders were on the move, moving their camp; every evening they pitched their tents in a new place. One evening, he says, one of my knights came to me, the one who had chosen the site for the tent that evening, and said to me, “Sire”—in Old French, lords were called *sire*, not only kings—“Sire, this evening I have lodged you better than last evening.”

The knight who had chosen the previous evening's tent site hears this, jumps up, grabs him by the hair, and says, “How dare you talk about something I do?” Now, this is an enormous discourtesy. In front of their lord, for knights to brawl like this is the height of boorishness, of bad manners. Joinville sees red and says, “So help me God, you will never enter my household again. I dismiss you. You will never again be one of my knights.”

The man goes away crying, then returns and begs him to forgive him. He brings friends who say to Joinville, come on, over a thing like this? And Joinville says, well, as far as I am concerned—but I swore. I said, so help me God, you will never enter my household again. Now I cannot simply let you back in as though nothing had happened. If the papal legate releases me from my oath, I will be delighted to take you back.

They go to the papal legate and explain the matter. The papal legate says, “I am sorry. This oath is a valid oath; there were grounds for it. I cannot simply release you from it as though it were nothing. It cannot be done.” And Joinville says: that is how I learned that you have to be careful about what you say, because after this affair he never took his knight back. Another aspect of this religiosity that is very striking, as Joinville expresses it but that was certainly shared by many people—

## 6. A practical bargain with God

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00236–U-00269 -->

—is another aspect of this concreteness. I said earlier that miracles happen; I said earlier that God is continually called as a witness to what happens. There is something more: people constantly expect to be helped by God, the Virgin, and the saints. They constantly expect that while you are going through trouble or difficulty, if you invoke God, God helps you. And when you are in danger and risking your skin in battle, you invoke God and God helps you. This is why we serve God, Joinville says.

During the crusade they explained to him that many Muslims—the Bedouins in particular, he says; this is how it was told to him—hold a very strange belief. They believe that a man carries his death written on his forehead and therefore can die only on the appointed day. You know, people say this, don't they? This idea of Muslim fatalism, according to which every man already has a predetermined day of death. And Joinville finds this an absurd idea. Because if a person already has an appointed day of death, it is as if God cannot help you change things. God cannot help you when you are there risking your skin in battle and invoke him; invoking him is useless, because everything has already been decided.

And Joinville says, but this is ridiculous. If it really were so—and this is the part that struck me most, the most interesting conclusion he draws from it—he says: if it really were so, wouldn't those who serve God be fools? If we did not believe that he had the power to lengthen our lives and help us in difficulty—that is why we serve him. And to him it seems perfectly obvious. There is a contract with God. That is the logic. Perhaps you are already beginning to glimpse what seems to me a major contradiction in this religiosity. On the one hand it is so warm and sincere, and yet it does not at all exclude attachment to concrete interests, concrete advantages, the help one hopes to receive. Of course.

## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00270–U-00307 -->

Serving God means making sacrifices, even heavy sacrifices at times. And who could know that better than Joinville, who spent six years on crusade? When he recounts his departure for the crusade, he says it in a few words: I left. When King Louis departed, as I told you the evening before last, he crossed half his kingdom on foot, dressed as a pilgrim with staff and bag. Joinville did not do the same thing, but as he left his castle of Joinville, he too humbly made the rounds of the nearby churches on foot with a pilgrim's staff before leaving for good. And he says: while making the rounds, I never turned back to look toward Joinville, because I did not want to be overcome by sorrow for the beautiful castle I was leaving, for my wife and my children. So sacrifices are made. After that departure he is away for six years before seeing his wife, children, and castle of Joinville again.

And yet sacrifices are made, but common sense is always kept in mind. You do not go on crusade for the pleasure of getting yourself killed. On the contrary, you go on crusade hoping in the end to save your skin and return. These crusaders, these crusading knights, are not fanatical martyrs. And this becomes absolutely clear precisely when the crusaders are routed and the Turks begin capturing them.

At that moment Joinville is on a ship. They were trying to escape aboard these ships, but the Saracen galleys bear down on them and surround his galley. He is on this ship with his knights, all his servants, all his people. The Saracens close in and prepare to board the galley, and Joinville says: we asked ourselves, what do we do now? And we all agreed: we surrender. There is absolutely nothing else to do. But one of my servants, he says, a cellarer, raised his hand and said, I do not agree with this decision. And what do you advise? I say we should all let ourselves be killed, and then we will go straight to paradise.

And this really is the Middle Ages as, deep down, we expect it to be, isn't it? It is interesting that it is not a noble knight but an ordinary man who has this idea: here we are, let us go all the way, let us be consistent all the way to martyrdom. This is the Middle Ages we expect—except that he is the only one who says it. Joinville disposes of the question in one short phrase: but we paid no attention to him. And indeed they surrender, and for the most part they survive. Common sense, religiosity, religious commitments, keeping one's word—it is not easy to be consistent in this field. Good King Louis was a saint in part because he always kept his word, even with the Saracens—

## 8. A saint keeps his word—and takes a joke

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00308–U-00361 -->

—which is something that rather surprises Joinville, because, after all, keeping your word, certainly—but with those people? And yet it happened; he saw it himself. After their captivity they are paying the ransom. There have been long negotiations with the Saracens who captured them, and in the end they have agreed on a very large sum, 200,000 livres tournois, an enormous sum, to free the king and the great lords. The money has arrived from France, and it has taken days and days to count and hand it over.

When they have almost finished, one of the king's knights, Sir Philippe de Nemours, arrives very cheerfully and says, “Sire, we cheated them. We gave them 10,000 livres less, and they did not notice.” Everyone is pleased; the king is not pleased at all. He is a saint; he does not do such things. He scowls and says, “But I gave my word that I would pay 200,000 livres, and we are going to pay 200,000 livres.”

Then the following comedy takes place. Joinville, who is present, steps on Sir Philippe de Nemours's foot to make him keep quiet and says, “No, Sire, he was joking. It isn't true. Cheat the Saracens—who could manage that? Everyone knows the Saracens are excellent at counting.” And Sir Philippe de Nemours catches on and says, “Yes, yes, that's right, I was joking.” But the king is not taken in. He remains in a very bad mood and says, “If you were joking, it was a stupid joke, and now I am going to check, because I want to be certain that we have paid everything. I will not carry this burden with me.”

So, as you can see, it was not very easy to live with this king. I began by telling you about a crusading knight with a profound religiosity, but having a profound religiosity is one thing and having to live with a saintly king who pushes things to the extreme is another. Moreover, when Joinville writes as an old man, it is official by then: the king is a saint; he has been canonized. But when these things happened, when they were both young, yes, the king already had this reputation for being a saint, but you understand the difference. Joinville greatly admired him and loved him very much, but working with him was not at all easy. Opportunities for conflict arose constantly.

Fortunately, the good thing was that the king had a sense of humor and could take a joke, because more than once Joinville let something outrageous slip. Once, during a truce with the Saracens, when everything in the crusader camp was calm, some pilgrims arrived—Armenian Christians from the East who were going on pilgrimage to Jerusalem with the Saracens' permission. They crossed the crusader camp, began asking questions, and also went to ask Joinville. They sent him an interpreter, saying, “We have heard that the saintly king is here in the camp. May we see the saintly king?”

Whether Joinville had got up on the wrong side of bed that morning or whether the idea that the king was a saint had not yet quite sunk in, well—he goes to the king. They are friends. The other man is the king and Joinville is one of his officials, but they have a very close relationship. He finds the king sitting under his tent on the sand, without even a carpet beneath him, and says, “Sire, there are people outside who say they want to see the saint, but I have no desire to kiss your bones.”

“Kiss your bones” was something a medieval person immediately understood: the bones of saints, relics. Saints normally are not alive. The great majority of saints are dead, and our relationship with them comes through their relics, which are extremely important to medieval people and are venerated and kissed, certainly. But telling the king, it is not yet time to kiss your bones, is not exactly a polite way to put it. Fortunately, the king bursts out laughing. Another time, a question of money arises.

## 9. Crusading accounts and a payment in patience

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00362–U-00419 -->

And perhaps I should pause here, because I am talking about crusading knights, and shortly we will talk about martial valor, courtesy, and chivalric ideals. So far we have been talking about religious faith, crusades, martyrdom. Money, it would seem, should have nothing to do with any of this. Wrong. Money has a great deal to do with it. A crusade costs an enormous amount and is a great enterprise that must be planned, and the very first thing is to find the money. Once the money is there, you can begin organizing the army, hiring knights, recruiting people, building ships, and so on. It is extremely expensive. Money enters into everything.

Nine times out of ten, a group of knights leaving on crusade in a lord's retinue has been formally hired under a regular contract and paid. It is true that there is supposed to be the feudal system, with every lord's vassals obliged to serve him, but those are old things; no one serves for free anymore, especially not to go far away. The vassals themselves, if they accompany their lords on a dangerous enterprise like a crusade, want to be paid. If someone manages to put together a small group made up entirely of his vassals and relatives, all well and good. Joinville says during one battle that a company of knights fought particularly well: those men were all their lord's vassals or relatives. But by then this is exceptional. Usually you hire and dismiss knights, as Joinville dismissed that discourteous man and then regretted it.

So you have to pay them, and Joinville tells us in detail how much money his lands brought him when he left on crusade and how much he spent to hire his ten knights. Since the king organizes the crusade, a great lord like Joinville, with his ten knights—which is already a unit of some importance, because an armored knight has armor, horses, and servants, and having ten is already something—goes to the king, and the king in turn hires him. He pays a certain sum to be accompanied by Joinville and his knights. Money enters into everything.

Parenthesis closed. Trouble arises when the contracted period between Joinville and the king ends. They have to make a new contract. It is a difficult moment: the king has run out of money, while Joinville is fairly well supplied with means. So he decides to make a grand gesture, as young people say today. He goes to the king and says it is time to renew the contract; I am ready to serve with my knights. The king says, I am delighted; how much do you want? Do not ask too much, because we are in very bad shape.

And Joinville says, “Your Majesty”—no, they did not say Your Majesty yet; they said “Sire”—“Sire, I am willing to make a bargain. Let us make this agreement. Instead of paying me, let us agree that throughout these six months when I remain in your service with my knights, you will never become angry with me. Because whenever someone comes to ask you for something, you become angry, and I am tired of hearing the king become angry with me. Let us make this bargain: if you promise not to become angry for six months, I will serve for free.” That is, I will pay the knights myself.

And this time too the king begins to laugh, and fortunately it ends well. Indeed, the king is delighted by this fine chivalric gesture made by the lord of Joinville and immediately goes to tell everyone. Here Joinville has truly shown that he is not a merchant. He is a noble knight. He knows money matters, matters a great deal; he cares about having it and counts it too. But when the occasion arises, he makes the grand gesture, because a nobleman is not a merchant and does not always merely calculate. So, we were saying that living beside Saint Louis is not very easy, not very easy, partly because sometimes the saint himself does not realize that he is a saint and cannot expect others to do the same things he does.

## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00420–U-00458 -->

Saint Louis constantly demonstrates his holiness; people are astonished. At a certain point, bodies from the last battle have been left outside the crusader camp. They forgot to bury them; several days have passed; they are in Africa in summer, and no one wants to go and bury them anymore. One fine afternoon they see the king come out of his tent, roll up his sleeves, and personally begin burying these bodies. Then they are all ashamed—mortified—and rush to join him.

On more than one occasion they advise the king to save himself, because the crusade is going badly, and to return home. The king says, but do we have the means to take everyone back? No, at this moment there are not enough means to take everyone back, but the king matters more than anyone; we must save the king. And he replies: I see all these people around me, and every one of them loves his life as much as I love mine, and therefore I will not leave and abandon them here to destruction. The interesting thing is not only this trait—not all kings of the period would have shared it—of worrying about saving ordinary people too, but the fact that the king loves his own life and finds it obvious to say so. I love my life, but I know others love theirs too.

Still, as I was saying, living near him is not easy, and Joinville tells many episodes from this point of view. One fine moment he and the king are chatting, and the king thinks of asking him a question. “Lord of Joinville, a question, a problem: tell me, would you rather be a leper or have committed a mortal sin?” Joinville immediately replies, “I would rather have committed thirty mortal sins than be a leper.” And he receives a memorable dressing-down.

Another time, on Maundy Thursday, the king washes the feet of the poor in memory of what Our Lord did. Then he goes to Joinville and says, you know, this washing of the poor's feet is a very fine thing; you should do it too. Why don't you wash the poor's feet on Maundy Thursday, Joinville? And Joinville replies, “By the faith I owe God, I will not wash the feet of those peasants.” Once again, naturally, the king is upset. But Joinville says things as they come into his mind; he does not worry. And with this—“I will not wash the feet of these peasants”—perhaps we can leave aside the religious dimension that has occupied us for rather too long so far, though after all this is a crusader, and talk about the other absolutely central dimension—

## 11. Noble blood against Robert de Sorbon

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00459–U-00506 -->

—of the life and mentality of these knights: this aristocratic mentality according to which society is divided, and there are gentlemen, nobles, knights, lords, and they are the best people there are. Then there are the villains. Everyone else is a villain, everyone who works—not only peasants digging in the fields but also merchants and financiers, people who may have made money, perhaps a great deal of money, perhaps more than the knights, as is beginning to happen. It is an ignoble thing, because villains, everyone knows, are ignoble people. The only respectable people are nobles, gentlemen, knights. This is rooted extremely deeply, almost automatically, in the minds of people like Joinville.

There are countless episodes of this kind. Joinville is at the king's court when the king's chaplain, Master Robert de Sorbon, comes toward him. Robert will later be famous because he founds the College of the Sorbonne, where the University of Paris will later be based. Master Robert sees Joinville entering court, goes to him, takes him by the hem of his garment, and pulls him before the king. Joinville is rather surprised: what the devil is happening—“the devil,” indeed, because sometimes it slips out.

Master Robert calls the king and says, “I wish to complain about the lord of Joinville, who comes to court dressed in fur and precious fabrics when the king—look—is not dressed as well as you.” This was still an age, as I have said before, of relative simplicity and modesty. Luxury had not yet reached such a level, even at courts. The king wore ordinary cloth. Joinville, who is wearing—he hears this and reacts with extreme harshness. He says, “Master Robert, this garment I wear was left to me by my father. It is the garment someone from a family like mine may wear. I am dressed as my father was dressed. The same cannot be said of you, because look at yourself: you too are dressed in precious fabrics, and everyone knows your father was an ordinary villain.”

And it is true: Robert de Sorbon is one of the many who rise from below through a career in the Church. There is an extremely unpleasant scene at court. The king intervenes, defends Robert de Sorbon, and ends the matter. But then there is an aftermath. Later the king takes Joinville aside and says, “Lord of Joinville, I must apologize. I defended Master Robert, and he did not deserve it, because you were right. But I saw him so confused, so humiliated, that I could not bear to leave him in such difficulty. You were right, though, because nobles dress in fur because their fathers and grandfathers dressed that way, and the newly rich villain, the parvenu who has only just made money, must not presume to put himself on their level.”

A mentality that will last for centuries, incidentally. It will take not merely the French Revolution but well into the nineteenth century before this idea—that they are an intrinsically superior class and everyone else is worth little—begins to fade among the European nobility. And yet—

## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00507–U-00549 -->

—Joinville knows, as I said before, that some of these newly rich villains are beginning to make so much money that they become important people, buy castles, and occupy important positions at court. This is difficult to accept. Nothing is more entertaining than seeing these villains who have risen in the world put back in their place.

At the court of the count of Champagne, Joinville relates, there was an extremely rich townsman, Érard de Nogent. He had made a great deal of money and was an important man. One day a poor knight without means arrives at the count of Champagne's court and begs the count to give him something so he can provide dowries for his daughters. Érard de Nogent, who is there, says, “But lord knight, why do you come asking for money? Why do you come asking the count for a gift? The count has already given away far too much and has nothing left to give.” And he sends him away rudely.

The count is there and has heard everything. He intervenes immediately. He turns to the great townsman Érard de Nogent and says, “But Master Villain, how dare you say I have nothing left to give? Indeed I do have something to give: you.” He grabs him by the clothing and throws him into the knight's hands, saying, “I hand him over to you. Release him only when he has paid a ransom.” It is what happens in war when prisoners are captured: prisoners are freed after a ransom has been negotiated.

And this story of how Érard de Nogent was put back in his place naturally makes the rounds of the courts of France, and the knights rejoice. A poor knight may be humiliated by a rich townsman, but then the world, so to speak, dispenses justice. It can dispense justice in much more dramatic ways too, because there can be no compromise here: social hierarchies must be respected.

At a certain point during the crusade, one of the king's sergeants—basically, we might say, something like a police officer today, an armed employee of the king—quarrels with one of Joinville's knights. He quarrels with him and shoves him. When Joinville learns of it, he is beside himself. He goes to the king and demands justice. He checks what justice requires, and custom provides that in a case like this, if a villain presumes to lay a hand on a knight, his hand must be cut off.

The king does not want this, but he says nothing can be done: the custom is never applied, but if its enforcement is demanded, this is the custom. The sergeant must come barefoot and in his shirt, kneel before the knight he shoved, beg his forgiveness, hold out his hand, and say, “Sir knight, I beg your forgiveness. Here is my hand; if you wish, you may cut it off.” Then everyone says, no, all right, let us forgive him, come on, let us forgive him. They forgive him; they really do forgive him. But the principle has been established once again. Now, how is this justified—

## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00550–U-00588 -->

—in their eyes, I mean, because perhaps it cannot be justified, but in their eyes how is this idea justified, that nobles are the salt of the earth, that knights are the best there are and everyone else counts for nothing? Fundamentally, the issue is that nobles see themselves as—and largely are—the people who wage war, the people who risk their skin. Others work and make money, but a knight's profession is risking his life: for his lord, his companions in arms, loyalty, the cause, the cross when he goes on crusade.

A knight's profession is hard. We do not realize it today. Try to imagine what it means to know how to stay on a horse and gallop wearing forty kilograms of armor, strike your opponent with a lance and knock him off his horse; and if he knocks you off instead, survive all that and be ready to begin again. Knightly training is an extremely demanding sport, an extreme sport in which it is also easy to lose your life. You have to begin practicing it as a child. A medieval proverb says that someone who has not mounted a horse by eight is fit only to become a priest. If you want to be a knight, uphold your own and your family's honor, command respect, and serve your lord and king, you must begin as a child and continue throughout your life. You must train constantly.

Being at war means remaining there for hours and hours in heat, cold, and frost, wearing armor, with nothing to drink or eat. Nobles tell themselves these things. In truth, if you look at armies, there are also infantrymen, domestics, servants, people on foot, who are often killed first when things go badly. But nobles do not even see them; they see themselves on their horses, wearing their helmets.

Joinville has an extraordinary page describing King Louis landing in Egypt. First from his galley, on horseback with lance in hand and gilded helmet, he plunges into the sea while still far from shore, splashes ashore, and is already prepared to go forward alone. Where are the Turks? He would charge them alone if they let him. This pride in knightly valor, this pride in being the people who continually risk their lives as a matter of principle and obligation, is what knights feel justifies all their privileges.

Naturally, there are other things that are also supposed to justify their privileges. They should be the best mannered, most courteous and refined, the people who know how to behave. In part it is true: they attach importance to these things. Then the scenes Joinville recounts show us that reality was often different.

## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-00589–U-00649 -->

But when knights behave like villains, others are upset. Joinville recounts precisely such an episode. One of my knights had been killed in combat, he says; the others were keeping vigil over the body. I went there and saw them laughing. “Why are you laughing?” “Nothing; we were thinking that now his wife will have to marry again.” Joinville is appalled. “How dare you? Your comrade is lying there still warm, and you are joking about who will marry his wife. Shame on you.” Afterward, he says, they were punished, because before the crusade ended they had all died and all their wives had remarried.

Behavior with women—behavior in women's chambers, as they sometimes say—knowing how to be with women, being elegant, refined, courteous: this is another characteristic valued and considered important in this social milieu. Once again King Louis stands apart; he is different. Joinville notices it. King Louis is someone who does not think about women at all. He is married, but Joinville says one of the bad things about him is that his wife and children were practically strangers to him. This is striking and does not accord with knightly values and behavior.

If we wanted—well, we still have five minutes. There were reasons for this in King Louis, and he is another figure who would yield interesting results if one applied a little psychoanalysis. The king lost his father very young and remained for years under his mother's guardianship. Queen Blanche of Castile was a formidable woman of power who governed the kingdom for years while he was a child. When he grew up he took control of the kingdom himself, but Blanche of Castile, his mother, was always present and controlled everything. The king had to marry, because a king must marry and produce heirs. But Blanche did not get along with her daughter-in-law and did everything she could to keep her away from her husband.

Joinville always tells these stories, things he saw and did not like at all. They could never be together because his mother-in-law was always there. He says their favorite place to live when they were young and newly married was the castle of Pontoise. At Pontoise the king's room is on one floor, the queen's below it, with a little staircase connecting them. Thanks to this staircase, the king and queen could be together when they were at Pontoise. But each kept ushers in the room by the door, so that if Queen Mother Blanche happened to arrive, the ushers immediately struck the door and the king and queen each returned to their own room. If the mother-in-law caught them together, there would be a scene.

What happens? At a certain point during the crusade, news arrives from France that Blanche of Castile has died. Joinville is struck by the king's and queen's reactions. He has never seen the king as distraught as when the news arrives that his mother has died. The king is an adult, but he is devastated and weeping. He comes to Joinville and says, “Seneschal, I have lost my mother.” I never saw him in that state.

Then Joinville visits the queen, and she too is weeping. Since this is an age when behavior was often spontaneous, when people often did not censor themselves, when they said things as they were and there was less formal hypocrisy, Joinville sees the queen weeping and says, “But why are you weeping? She was the woman you hated most in the world, and now your mother-in-law is dead.” And the queen says, “I am not weeping for her. I am weeping because I see that the king is distraught and I am afraid something terrible will happen, because he looks so unwell. No, no, I do not care either that my mother-in-law is dead—quite the contrary.” These are often spontaneous people, with a spontaneity that can sometimes seem almost childlike to us.

## 15. Adults at play and chivalry recapitulated

<!-- chapter: CH-015; transcript: U-00650–U-00675 -->

Perhaps you know, perhaps you do not, that adults in the Middle Ages play a great deal. Some are the same games we play: chess, draughts, backgammon. They always play for money, it must be said—even chess—because it is more fun. They gamble a great deal, at dice, although naturally the king does not tolerate it, and so—and then they play games that adults today no longer play. Knights and ladies, if they are together and become bored, can play blind man's buff or hide-and-seek. No one is surprised, because people enjoy themselves; adults are not ashamed to have fun like children.

During the crusade, when one might think they had more serious things to do, Joinville tells this story. We were there in the crusader camp, all the tents. The weather was fine. My knights and I were dining in front of the tent. The table is set, the white tablecloth—no knight would ever dine without a freshly laundered white tablecloth; it is another status symbol—our crystal, our goblets, and we were dining there. In the next tent was the count of Eu, a very ingenious fellow. He too was dining outside at a table with his knights. He had made a small catapult, and with this little catapult he threw stones from his table toward ours and smashed our glasses.

Joinville recounts this ecstatically. How amusing the count of Eu was! Imagine the games played today in the halls of power. After that—and here we end, because it is almost midnight—it seems the hour has come when our carriage turns back into a pumpkin. So there is this chivalric culture that not everyone knows how to apply. You must be well mannered; not everyone succeeds, but you must show that you are different from villains. You have many privileges, but it is right to have them because we are braver than others and risk our skin more than others. Sometimes it goes too far.

## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

<!-- chapter: CH-016; transcript: U-00676–U-00698 -->

The crusade ended so badly in part because, during the great attack planned against the Saracens, the group of Templar knights was supposed to hold the vanguard. By rule in the Holy Land, the Templars have the vanguard; they are the first to attack, which is the place of honor. But the king's brother, Robert of Artois, the count of Artois, who had around him some advisers who were, how can I put it, rather overbearing, at a certain point begins to accelerate and overtakes the Templars with his group of knights, breaking the predetermined order.

The Templars see the count of Artois overtaking them and begin spurring their horses too, because heaven forbid they should fall behind; that cannot happen. Everyone behind them accelerates as well, and the entire attack, planned as a succession of interventions by various groups of knights, degenerates into a wild race because no one wants the shame of being the one left behind. It ends very badly. The Turks surround them, the count of Artois is killed, almost all the Templars are killed, and the crusade is lost.

So knightly arrogance sometimes causes serious trouble. But they are not all so arrogant. In their culture there is an eternal conflict between, how can I put it, the cult of honor, showing off, the grand gesture, and the professionalism of the soldier who knows how things work and knows that war must be conducted properly. The cult of honor can also help someone fight a war well: it keeps you going and gives you courage at dangerous moments.

## 17. “We shall speak of this day in the ladies' chambers”

<!-- chapter: CH-017; transcript: U-00699–U-00726 -->

At a certain point during this great disaster, the Christians are fleeing and retreating, trying to maintain some order. There is a little bridge that must be defended at all costs. Joinville finds himself alone defending it with another great lord, the count of Soissons; there are only the two of them. This too can happen in medieval warfare: two great lords have lost sight of all their knights and servants. Only the two of them are there defending this little bridge. Behind them, the Christians retreat. Ahead, the Turks arrive.

Two well-mounted knights in fine armor—they are great lords with excellent horses and well-sharpened swords—can already hold off everyone approaching for a few minutes. But who is going to come forward first? The Saracens stop a few yards away and begin shouting, baring their teeth, yelling insults, and throwing stones at them. An occasional arrow begins to whistle past. At this point Joinville and the count of Soissons say to themselves, yes, good, now we have stopped them; what exactly do we do next? They are there shouting, but as soon as they move—and Joinville says, the good count of Soissons made fun of me because I was afraid and told me, “Seneschal, let this rabble scream, because by God's cap”—he swore that way, Joinville says in parentheses—“by God's cap, you and I will speak of this day again in the ladies' chambers.”

This is one of those cases in which chivalric culture—honor and pride, but also courtly love, boasting, but boasting after winning, not after losing—comes together. We will win, we will return alive, we will remember this day, and in the ladies' chambers we will make ourselves look splendid by recounting our adventures. And Joinville lived so long that he did indeed manage to tell his adventures, and I have tried, in a small way, to tell them to you too. Thank you, thank you.
