# How a Medieval Man Thought: The Knight

## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00062 -->

Good evening, everyone. We're getting to know the people of the Middle Ages, and I realize I've
been putting them into rather familiar boxes: the friar, the merchant, the knight. And the women?
I've left them out. Most of the people who left us written accounts were men, but there were women
writing too. I could have thought of that. These categories make life convenient for the
historian; they don't contain everybody.

Today, the knight. His name is Jean de Joinville, a Frenchman of the thirteenth century. Strictly
speaking, he's a great lord who commands other knights. Most knights have land, servants, and
horses, but serve somebody else. Joinville owns several castles. In his lordship, he keeps order,
collects taxes, and administers justice. He also serves as seneschal of Champagne, a high official
who helps administer the region.

But he's still a knight, raised with a knight's values. You know the ideals: protect widows and
orphans, be humble. They're in the medieval romances, which these men read too. In practice,
Joinville and his companions care a great deal about courage in battle, loyalty to the men beside
them, and honor. About whether you'll risk your skin when it matters.

We can get to know him because he does something rather unusual for a knight: he writes a book. He
has spent years serving Louis IX of France, the king we call Saint Louis. In 1248 he accompanies
Louis on crusade. They come home six years later—those who come home, that is. When Louis goes on
another crusade, Joinville prefers to stay where he is. Quite a few survivors of the first feel
the same way. The king goes, and dies there in 1270.

By the early fourteenth century, Joinville is an old man in his castle, one of the few people left
who knew the king. And Louis really is a saint now: he's been canonized. Joinville is asked to
write his life. What he gives us is supposed to be a book about Saint Louis, but Joinville himself
keeps getting into it. His adventures, his opinions, his arguments with the king. Above all, his
memories of the crusade.

And in a crusading knight's world, as you might expect, God is never very far away.

## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00063–U-00118 -->

Louis hears several Masses and a whole series of other services every day. I thought of listing
them, but we'd be here for quite a while. He is a saint. Joinville doesn't claim to be one, but he
still hears Mass every morning. That's how the day begins.

What strikes us is how familiar sacred things are to these people. On the voyage home, after six
years away and the deaths of so many friends, they stop at Lampedusa. Joinville writes:

We came to an island called Lampedusa, where we caught a great many rabbits. We found an ancient
hermitage among the rocks and the garden made by the hermits who had once lived there: it
contained olive trees, fig trees, grapevines, and other trees. A stream from the spring ran
through the garden. The king and the rest of us went to the far end of the garden and found an
oratory in the first vault, whitewashed with lime, with a red earthen cross. We entered the second
vault and found the bodies of two dead people whose flesh had entirely decayed; their ribs still
held together, the bones of their hands lay upon their chests, and they lay facing east, in the
manner in which bodies are buried. When we reassembled aboard our ship, one of our sailors was
missing. The shipmaster supposed that he had remained there to become a hermit; and so Nicholas of
Soissons, the king's master sergeant, left three sacks of ship's biscuit on the shore, so that the
man might find them and live on them.

We never hear from the sailor. That explanation is the shipmaster's guess. But notice how readily
it occurs to him: a man comes across a place like this and decides to give his life to God. Yes,
that could happen. Better leave him something to eat.

Miracles belong to this world too. When you expect them, it's quite possible to come away certain
you've witnessed one. And after a crusade like this, you have good reason to hope for a little
help.

On the same voyage, a squire falls from a ship ahead of Joinville's. Joinville misses the fall;
what he sees is something floating in the sea, so still that he takes it for an object. He goes
on:

One of the king's galleys picked him up and brought him to our ship, where he told us how it had
happened. I asked him why he had made no effort to save himself, either by swimming or in any
other way. He answered that there was no need for him to make any effort, because, as soon as he
began to fall, he commended himself to Our Lady of Vauvert, and she supported him by the shoulders
from the moment he fell until the king's galley picked him up. In honor of this miracle, I had it
painted in my chapel at Joinville and in the windows at Blécourt.

He tells it quite calmly. A miracle: these things happen. But that last sentence gives him away.
This one happened before his eyes, as he understands it, and he wants people back home to see it
too. There's tremendous feeling beneath that matter-of-fact account. And he's the lord of these
places—Blécourt is another village that belongs to him. He can have his experience put on the
walls and in the windows for everybody to see. Religion is a bond that holds this whole world
together, something people share with great fervor and great simplicity.

## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00119–U-00157 -->

That certainty can also turn violent. Louis tells Joinville a story set at Cluny, the great
Benedictine monastery in Burgundy. It's about a disputation, a public religious debate. Medieval
churchmen enjoy these contests: their brothers show off at tournaments; they show off by arguing.

Joinville writes: He told me that there was a great disputation between clerics and Jews at the
monastery of Cluny. There was a knight there to whom the abbot had given bread in the house for
God's sake.

Knights often end their lives in monasteries. After a lifetime of fighting, a man may no longer
have the strength for it. He may have had enough of that life, too. If he has property, he gives
it to the monastery; if he has nothing, the monks may take him in out of charity. Either way, he
spends his last years there. Now, Joinville continues:

He asked the abbot to let him speak first, and permission was granted only
reluctantly. Then he rose, leaned on his staff, and asked them to bring him the greatest scholar
and foremost master among the Jews, which they did. He asked him this question: 'Master,' said the
knight, 'I ask whether you believe that the Virgin Mary, who bore God in her womb and in her arms,
gave birth as a virgin and is the mother of God.' The Jew replied that he believed none of it. The
knight answered that he had acted like a great fool by entering her monastery and her house when
he neither believed in her nor loved her. 'And truly,' said the knight, 'you will pay for it.'
Then he raised his crutch, struck the Jew beside the ear, and knocked him to the ground. The Jews
fled and carried away their master badly wounded; and that was the end of the disputation.

Even holding a debate like this seems dangerous. Suppose the Jewish scholars win? Some good
Christian listening may come away with doubts. Learned clerics can take on that risk, but
laymen should keep out of it. Joinville gives us the king's words:

'And so I tell you,' said the king, 'that no one should dispute with them unless he is a very
learned cleric. But when a layman hears the Christian faith spoken ill of, he should defend the
Christian faith only with his sword, which he should drive into the offender's belly as far as it
can go.'

This is the saintly king speaking. We need to keep that in mind when we imagine what holiness
means to these men.

Their faith also makes very precise demands on everyday life: what you eat, when you fast. Break
one of those rules, even by accident, and you may find it very hard to forgive yourself.

## 4. Meat on Friday: intention versus observance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00158–U-00197 -->

The crusader army has been defeated in Egypt. Joinville describes the retreat with extraordinary
force: flight, confusion, men surrendering without knowing whether they'll be spared. Some captors
kill the sick and wounded. A great lord may fetch a ransom, but even he can't count on getting
through this alive.

There is also something these great lords recognize in one another. Christian or Muslim, they
belong to the same social world. They understand rank, family connections, the things that matter
to a man like themselves. They have plenty to talk about.

Joinville is taken prisoner. The chief admiral of the Egyptian galleys summons him and asks about
his claimed kinship with Emperor Frederick II. Now they're eating together. The admiral speaks
through an interpreter, the man Joinville calls "my Saracen." And the legate he'll mention is the
pope's representative with the army. Here's what happens:

While we were eating, he had a citizen of Paris brought before us. When the citizen arrived, he
said to me, 'My lord, what are you doing?' 'Why, what am I doing?' I said. 'In God's name,' he
said, 'you are eating meat on a Friday!' When I heard this, I pushed my bowl away. The admiral
asked my Saracen why I had done that, and he told him; the admiral replied that God would not hold
it against me, since I had not done it knowingly. And you should know that the legate gave me the
same answer once we were out of prison. Yet that did not stop me from fasting afterward on bread
and water every Friday in Lent. The legate became very angry with me about this, because I was the
only great lord who had remained with the king.

The Muslim admiral and the pope's representative agree. Joinville still feels he has something to
make up for. In all that terror and confusion, it's the bowl of meat that stays on his conscience.

## 5. Dangerous words: devils, oaths, and dismissal

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00198–U-00235 -->

Listen to the words people use. In Joinville's world, people are constantly swearing by God or
invoking the devil. Great lords even have favorite oaths of their own. The count of Soissons, for
example, swears by God's cap.

And everyone seems to have the devil on their lips: go to the devil, may the devil take you.
Joinville thinks this is a disgrace to France. In twenty-two years at Louis's side, he says, he
never heard the king swear by God or the saints, or name the devil except when reading a text that
required it. Joinville himself makes anyone in his household who uses the devil's name in this way
accept a cuff or a slap. He says the habit has almost disappeared there.

Joinville is more troubled by casual talk of the devil, but invoking God has consequences too.
When you call God as witness to a promise, your words bind you. Joinville learns this during the
crusade. The army is on the move and pitches camp in a new place each evening. One night,
Joinville says, the knight who has selected that night's campsite comes to him and says, “Sire”—in
Old French, lords were called *sire*, not just kings—“Sire, this evening I have lodged you better
than last evening.”

The knight who chose the previous night's campsite hears this, jumps up, grabs him by the hair,
and says, “You are very bold to speak about something I do.” This is an enormous breach of
courtesy. Two knights brawling in front of their lord is the height of boorishness and bad
manners. Joinville sees red and says, “Out of my household now; for, so help me God, you will
never be with me again.”

The knight goes away in distress. Friends plead for him, and Joinville would be glad to relent.
But there's that oath. He asks the papal legate whether he can be released from it.

The legate says, “I'm sorry. This is a valid oath, and there were grounds for it. I can't simply
release you from it as though it were nothing. It can't be done.” A moment's anger, and Joinville
has bound himself to something he no longer wants. Calling on God isn't just a figure of speech.

## 6. A practical bargain with God

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00236–U-00269 -->

But if God can hold you to your word, surely he can also help you when you're in trouble?
Joinville expects that help, especially in battle. Which is why a belief he attributes to the
Bedouins bothers him so much. Here's how he describes it:

Their belief is that no one can die except on his appointed day, and for that reason they will not
wear armor; and when they curse their children, they say: 'May you be accursed like the Frank who
arms himself for fear of death!' [...] Since returning from overseas, I have seen in this country
some disloyal Christians who held to the Bedouins' law and said that no one could die except on
his appointed day. Their belief is so faithless that it amounts to saying that God has no power to
help us. For those who served God would be fools if we did not believe that he had the power to
lengthen our lives and preserve us from harm and misfortune; and we must believe in him, for he
has the power to do all things.

<!-- quotation-excerpt: Q-009; final repeated passage omitted; Q-010 retained once -->

There's something almost contractual about his indignation. What would be the point of serving God
if God couldn't do anything for you? His devotion is sincere, and he expects it to make a
difference when someone is trying to kill him.

## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00270–U-00307 -->

And he is prepared to give up a great deal. When Louis sets out on crusade, he walks across half
his kingdom dressed as a pilgrim, carrying a staff and a bag. Joinville doesn't go that far, but
begins his pilgrimage much closer to home. Listen to how he remembers his departure. A pilgrim's
scrip is the bag he carries for the journey:

The abbot of Cheminon gave me my pilgrim's scrip and staff; then I departed from Joinville,
without re-entering the castle until my return, on foot, barefoot, and dressed in wool. In this
way I went to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, and to the other holy relics that are there. While I was
going to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, I never once wished to turn my eyes back toward Joinville, for
fear that my heart would soften at the thought of the fine castle I was leaving and of my two
children.

The castle, and the children. He wants to come home to both. So when the army is defeated and his
men are trapped aboard a ship, they have a decision to make. Among them is one of his cellarers, a
household officer. Joinville writes:

It was not long before we saw four of the sultan's galleys approaching, carrying fully a thousand
men. I called my knights and my people and asked what they wished us to do: surrender to the
sultan's galleys or surrender to those on shore. We all agreed that we preferred to surrender to
the sultan's galleys, because they would keep us together, rather than to those on shore, who
would scatter us and sell us to the Bedouins. Then one of my cellarers, who came from Doullens,
said: 'My lord, I do not agree with this counsel.' I asked what counsel he favored, and he told
me: 'I favor letting ourselves all be killed; then we shall all go to paradise.' But we did not
believe him.

Now, the man who wants to go straight to paradise gives us the Middle Ages we tend to expect,
doesn't he? Follow your faith all the way to martyrdom. And notice that it's a servant who says
this, not one of the noble knights. But he's the only one. Everybody else wants to survive.
These men have gone on crusade hoping to come home again, not for the pleasure of getting killed.
Their faith leaves room for common sense.

## 8. A saint keeps his word—and takes a joke

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00308–U-00361 -->

Louis takes his religious obligations further. He keeps his word even to the Saracens, and that
surprises Joinville. Of course you should keep a promise—but to those people? Louis has promised
a ransom for the prisoners. The money is being weighed out in
livres, the French unit of account, when one of his men, Philippe de Nemours, speaks up. Joinville
remembers:

Then Sir Philippe de Nemours told the king that the Saracens had been undercounted by one
balance-load of ten thousand livres. The king became extremely angry and said that he wished the
ten thousand livres to be returned to them, because he had promised to pay them two hundred
thousand livres before leaving the river. I then trod on Sir Philippe's foot and told the king not
to believe him, because what he said was untrue: the Saracens were the most skillful counters in
the world. Sir Philippe said that I spoke the truth, because he had said it only as a joke. The
king said, 'May misfortune befall such a joke. And I command you, Sir Philippe, by the fealty you
owe me as my man, that if the ten thousand livres have not been paid, you are to have them paid
without fail.' Many people had advised the king to withdraw to his ship waiting at sea, to remove
himself from the Saracens' hands. The king would listen to no one; instead, he said that he would
not leave the river, just as he had promised, until he had paid them two hundred thousand livres.

You can admire a man like this and still find him difficult to live with. Joinville loves Louis;
he also has to get through the day beside him.

Fortunately, the king can take a joke. He already has a reputation for holiness, and Joinville
isn't above teasing him about it. Remember that people venerate saints' bones as relics: they even
kiss them. Perhaps Joinville has got out of bed on the wrong side that morning; perhaps he still
hasn't quite taken in the idea that his friend is a saint. Listen to this visit from some Armenian
pilgrims:

In the course of our marches we came to the sands of Acre, where the king and the army encamped.
There a large company from Greater Armenia came to me on pilgrimage to Jerusalem, paying a heavy
tribute to the Saracens who were conducting them. Through an interpreter who knew their language
and ours, they asked me to show them the holy king. I went to the king, who was sitting in a
pavilion, leaning against its pole; he sat on the sand without a carpet or anything else beneath
him. I said to him: 'My lord, outside there is a large company from Greater Armenia going to
Jerusalem, and they ask me to show them the holy king; but I have no intention of kissing your
bones just yet.' He laughed very openly and told me to go and fetch them, which I did. When they
had seen the king, they commended him to God, and the king did the same for them.

## 9. Crusading accounts and a payment in patience

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00362–U-00419 -->

We've been talking about faith, sacrifice, martyrdom. And money? Oh, money enters into all of it.
Horses, equipment, ships, men who expect to be fed and paid. Before you can go on crusade,
somebody has to find the funds.

When a lord sets out with a company of knights, nine times out of ten he's hired them under a
contract. You might think of the feudal system: a lord has vassals, and they owe him service.
Well, that's the old arrangement. Nobody wants to serve for nothing anymore, especially so far
from home. Even your own vassals expect to be paid if they're going to accompany you on something
as dangerous as a crusade.

If you can assemble a company made up entirely of your vassals and relatives, all the better.
During one battle, Joinville singles out a group that fights particularly well: those men are
all their lord's vassals or kinsmen. But that's exceptional now. Usually you hire knights, and
you can dismiss them—just as Joinville dismisses the man who starts pulling hair in front of him.

Joinville mortgages a great deal of land at Metz in Lorraine. His mother is still alive, so he
doesn't yet have the full family income. He tells us how far his money goes:

<!-- quotation-excerpt: Q-015 -->

I, who did not have land worth a thousand livres a year, took responsibility when I went overseas
for a company of ten knights including myself, with two other knights bearing banners. When I
arrived in Cyprus, after paying for my ship, I had only two hundred and forty livres tournois
left. Some of my knights sent word that unless I found money, they would leave me. God, who never
failed me, then provided for me in this way: the king, who was at Nicosia, sent for me, retained
me in his service, and put eight hundred livres in my coffers; and then I had more money than I
needed.

Ten knights may not sound like much, but think of each man's armor, his horses, his servants.
That's already a force worth having. A lord hires his knights, and the king hires the lord with
his whole company. Money runs through the whole arrangement. And to Joinville, the king putting
money in his coffers is also God providing for him. He sees no difficulty in that.

Later, when his contract is about to expire, there's another negotiation. What will he ask His
Majesty for? No, they don't say "Majesty" yet. "Sire," they say. Here's Joinville:

While the king was fortifying Caesarea, I went to his quarters to see him. As soon as he saw me
enter his chamber, where he was speaking with the legate, he rose, drew me aside, and said: 'You
know,' said the king, 'that I retained you only until Easter; so I ask you to tell me what I
should give you to remain with me from Easter for one year.' I told him that I did not wish him to
give me any more of his money than he had given me before, but that I wished to make a different
bargain with him. 'Because,' I said, 'you become angry whenever someone asks you for something, I
want you to agree that if I ask you for anything during this whole year, you will not become
angry; and if you refuse me, I will not become angry either.' When he heard this, he began to
laugh very openly and told me that he retained me on that condition. He took me by the hand, led
me to the legate and his council, and recounted to them the bargain we had made. They were very
pleased, because I was the richest man in the army.

That's a man who knows his employer. Even a saint can be tiresome when you need to ask him for
something.

## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00420–U-00458 -->

And yet Louis does things that leave the people around him awestruck. At Sidon in 1253, on the
eastern Mediterranean coast, there are Christian bodies to bury. They've been lying there for
days, decomposing, and nobody wants to go near them. Then the king gets up and goes to help carry
them into the burial trenches himself. <!-- accuracy: [N-008] --> Now everybody is mortified.
The king is doing it, while they've been hanging back. They rush to help.

Or take the ship that's in danger near Cyprus, with Louis and hundreds of other passengers aboard.
His advisers urge him to transfer to another vessel. Joinville records his answer:

Then the king said: 'Lords, I have heard your advice and the advice of my people; now I will tell
you mine. If I leave the ship, there are five hundred people or more aboard who, fearing danger to
their lives—for there is not one among them who does not love his life as much as I love mine—will
remain on the island of Cyprus, and who may perhaps never return to their own countries. Therefore
I would rather place my own person, my wife, and my children in God's hands than inflict such harm
on so great a number of people as are aboard.'

What an extraordinary thing for a king to say: those people's lives are as dear to them as his is
to him. You can see why Joinville admires him. The difficulty comes when Louis expects his friends
to live up to the same standards.

One day the king puts a question to him:

'Now I ask you,' he said, 'which would you rather: that you were a leper, or that you had
committed a mortal sin?' And I, who never lied to him, answered that I would rather have committed
thirty than be a leper. When the friars had left, he called me aside alone, made me sit at his
feet, and said: 'Why did you say that to me yesterday?' I told him that I still said the same. He
said: 'You spoke like a rash fool, for you should know that there is no leprosy so hideous as
being in mortal sin, because the soul that is in mortal sin resembles the Devil; therefore no
leprosy can be so hideous. It is true that when a man dies he is cured of the leprosy of the body.
But when a man who has committed a mortal sin dies, he does not know and cannot be certain that in
his lifetime he repented in such a way that God pardoned him; therefore he should greatly fear
that this leprosy will last as long as God is in paradise. So I beg you as earnestly as I can, for
love of God and of me, to set your heart on preferring every bodily misfortune—leprosy and every
other illness—to mortal sin entering your soul.'

For Louis, hell is at least as real as the disease. Joinville has a harder time getting past the
disease. And there's a much more ordinary test of humility: washing poor people's feet on Maundy
Thursday, the Thursday before Easter, following Christ's example. Joinville remembers:

He asked me whether I washed the feet of the poor on Maundy Thursday. 'My lord,' I said, 'God
forbid! I will never wash the feet of those common people.' 'Truly,' he said, 'that was badly
said, for you must not disdain what God did for our instruction. So I ask you, first for the love
of God and then for love of me, to accustom yourself to washing them.'

There's a limit to how far Joinville's admiration will take him. Especially when it means bending
down before someone he considers beneath him.

## 11. Noble blood against Robert de Sorbon

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00459–U-00506 -->

To Joinville, noble birth makes you a better sort of person. Everybody else is a "villein"—a word
he uses contemptuously for commoners, whether they work the land or handle great sums of money.
And some of these people are becoming richer than the knights. You can imagine how that feels
to a man who takes his own superiority for granted. Money is changing hands; he wants to be
quite sure that rank doesn't change with it.

Others rise through learning. Robert de Sorbon comes from a non-noble family and becomes a master
of theology and a royal cleric. In 1253 he founds the theological college that will be known as
the Sorbonne; it receives royal confirmation in 1257. <!-- accuracy: [N-009] -->

One day he takes issue with Joinville's expensive clothes. Remember, this is still a relatively
simple, relatively modest world, even at court. Courtly luxury hasn't yet reached the scale we
might imagine. The king himself wears ordinary cloth. Joinville tells it this way:

Master Robert de Sorbon came to fetch me there, took me by the hem of my cloak, and led me to the
king; all the other knights followed us.

You can imagine Joinville wondering what the devil is going on. The devil, yes—it does slip out
occasionally. He goes on:

I asked Master Robert, 'Master Robert, what do you want
with me?' He said: 'I want to ask you this: if the king were sitting in this meadow and you went
and sat on his bench higher than he, should you rightly be blamed for it?' I said yes. He said:
'Then you are indeed to blame when you are more nobly dressed than the king, for you dress in vair
fur and green cloth, which the king does not.' I said: 'Master Robert, saving your grace, I am not
to blame if I dress in green and vair, for my father and mother left me this mode of dress. But
you are to blame, for you are the son of a villein man and a villein woman, and you have abandoned
the dress of your father and mother and are dressed in richer camelin cloth than the king.' Then I
took the hem of his surcoat and the hem of the king's and said to him: 'Now see whether I am
telling the truth.' The king then began to defend Master Robert verbally with all his might.

But later, in private, the king has something to add. Joinville writes:

Then he told me that he had called us so that he could confess to me that he had wrongly defended
Master Robert against me. 'But,' he said, 'I saw him so bewildered that he badly needed my help.
Still, do not attach importance to anything I said in Master Robert's defense, for, as the
seneschal says, you ought to dress well and neatly, so that your wives will love you the more and
your people will esteem you more. For, as the wise man says, one should adorn oneself in clothing
and arms in such a way that the worthy men of this world do not say one does too much, nor the
young people of this world say one does too little.'

Imagine how pleased Joinville is to put that in his book. Even the saint agrees that a gentleman
should look the part. Robert's learning hasn't entitled him to forget where he comes from.

## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00507–U-00549 -->

But what happens when a commoner has enough money to build himself a castle? Joinville has a
favorite kind of story about that. This one takes place at the count of Champagne's court. The
rich man is Artaud de Nogent, a burgess—a townsman of common birth. Joinville writes:

Artaud de Nogent was the burgess in all the world whom the count trusted most, and he was so rich
that he built the castle of Nogent-l'Artaud with his own money. [...] At the foot of the steps a
poor knight came before him, knelt, and said: 'My lord, I beg you for God's sake to give me
something of yours with which I may marry my two daughters, whom you see here.' Artaud, who was
walking behind him, said to the poor knight: 'Sir knight, you are behaving discourteously by
asking my lord, for he has given so much that he has nothing left to give.' The generous count
turned toward Artaud and said: 'Sir villein, what you say—that I have nothing left to give—is not
true. I still have you yourself. Here, sir knight: I give him to you, and I will guarantee him to
you.' The knight was not taken aback; instead he seized Artaud by the cloak and said he would not
release him until Artaud had settled with him. Before Artaud escaped him, he had settled with the
knight for five hundred livres.

It's like taking a prisoner in war: you keep hold of him until you've negotiated a ransom.
You can imagine this story going around the courts of France, and the knights enjoying every
word. A rich townsman may humiliate a poor knight, but then the world puts things right again.
Everybody is reminded of where he belongs.

And money isn't the only thing that fails to protect a commoner. Serving the king has its limits
too. At Caesarea, one of the royal sergeants—armed men in the king's service—gets into a quarrel
with a knight. Here's Joinville:

The third judgment that I saw given at Caesarea was this. One of the king's sergeants, named Le
Goulu, laid hands on a knight of my company. I went to complain to the king. The king told me
that, in his view, I could well let the matter pass, since the sergeant had only shoved him. I
said I would not let it pass, and that if the king did not give me justice I would leave his
service, since his sergeants were shoving knights. He had justice done for me, and the judgment,
according to the customs of the country, was this: the sergeant came to my quarters barefoot,
wearing only his shirt and breeches, with a bare sword in his hand. He knelt before the knight,
took the sword by its point, offered the pommel to the knight, and said: 'My lord, I make amends
to you for laying hands on you, and I have brought you this sword so that you may cut off my fist,
if it pleases you.' I asked the knight to forgive his resentment against the sergeant, and he did
so.

All that for a shove. Joinville is ready to walk out of the king's service over it. The king may
think it's a small matter; to a knight, the whole order of society is at stake.

## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00550–U-00588 -->

How do they justify this? In their own eyes, it's simple: their job is to risk their lives. For
their lord, for the men beside them, for the cross. Other people work and make money. A knight
fights.

And that takes training. Think of riding at a gallop in armor, striking with a lance, being
knocked out of the saddle yourself and getting up to do it again. It's an extreme sport, and one
that can kill you even while you're learning. You have to start young. A medieval poem puts it
bluntly:

Whoever goes on without learning to ride and remains at school until the age of twelve is
thereafter poorly suited to knighthood: he lacks the strength for it. The unridden schoolboy is
fit for nothing but the priesthood.

And you keep at it for the rest of your life. Your own honor, your family's honor, the respect
you expect from other people, your service to your lord and king—all of it depends on your
ability to do this. You have to train continually.

Then come the long hours in armor, in the heat or the freezing cold, with little to eat or drink.
The nobles know what they endure. Of course, infantrymen and servants face danger too. But when
knights tell the story, it's the knights they see.

Listen to Joinville describing Louis landing in Egypt. The standard of Saint-Denis is the king's
battle banner:

When the king heard that the standard of Saint-Denis was ashore, he strode rapidly through his
vessel and would not stop for the legate who was with him. He leapt into the sea, where the water
came up to his armpits, and went with his shield at his neck, his helmet on his head, and his
lance in his hand until he reached his men on the seashore. When he came ashore and saw the
Saracens, he asked what people they were. He was told they were Saracens; he lowered the lance
beneath his armpit, put his shield before him, and would have charged the Saracens if the worthy
men with him had allowed it.

His companions have to hold him back. And later, at Mansurah, Joinville gives us this portrait:

Never did I see so fine an armed man, for he stood out above all his people from the shoulders
upward, with a gilded helmet on his head and a German sword in his hand.

That's the king Joinville sees: magnificent, and ready to risk his life. To a knight, a man like
that has earned his privileges.

They're also supposed to be the best-mannered people in the world. Courteous, refined,
considerate. Joinville takes that seriously. Some of his companions need reminding.

## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-00589–U-00649 -->

One of his knights has died. The body is in the chapel for the funeral Mass. Joinville remembers:

On the eve of Shrove Tuesday I saw a marvel that I wish to recount to you, for that same day Sir
Hugh of Landricourt, who served under my banner, was buried. While he lay on his bier in my
chapel, six of my knights were leaning on several sacks full of barley. Because they were talking
loudly in my chapel and making noise that disturbed the priest, I went to tell them to be quiet,
and told them it was a disgraceful thing for knights and gentlemen to talk while Mass was being
sung. They began to laugh at me and told me, laughing, that they were arranging the remarriage of
his wife. I reproached them and told them that such words were neither good nor proper, and that
they had quickly forgotten their companion. God took such vengeance on them that the next day was
the great battle of Shrove Tuesday, in which they were killed or mortally wounded; consequently
all six of their wives had to remarry.

In his telling, God cares about a knight's manners too.

And courtesy matters in your dealings with women. Here even the king disappoints him. Louis's
wife, Margaret of Provence, joins them at Sidon during the crusade. Joinville writes:

The queen, who had recently recovered from giving birth to Lady Blanche at Jaffa, arrived at Sidon
by sea. When I heard she had arrived, I rose from before the king, went to meet her, and brought
her to the castle. When I returned to the king, who was in his chapel, he asked whether the queen
and the children were well, and I said they were. He said to me: “I know well that when you rose
from before me, you went to meet the queen; that is why I had the sermon held for you.” I remind
you of these things because I had already been around him for five years, yet I had never heard
him speak of the queen or his children, either to me or to anyone else; and it was not good
conduct, as it seems to me, to be aloof from one's wife and children.

He's writing the life of a saint, and he puts that in it. He loves the king, but he has his own
ideas about how a husband should behave.

Now, you can imagine what a psychoanalyst might make of Louis's family. He loses his father young
and grows up under his mother, Blanche of Castile, a formidable woman who governs France while
he's a child. When he's old enough to take charge of the kingdom himself, his mother is still
there, still a very powerful presence.

And he has to marry. A king needs heirs; marriage isn't something he can simply decide to do
without. But Blanche doesn't get along with her daughter-in-law. Joinville takes us to their
residence in Pontoise:

Queen Blanche's harsh treatment of Queen Margaret went so far that, as much as she could, Blanche
would not allow her son to be in his wife's company except in the evening, when he went to bed
with her. The residence where the king and queen most liked to stay was Pontoise, because the
king's chamber was above and the queen's chamber below. They arranged matters so that they held
their conversations on a spiral staircase leading from one chamber to the other. They had also
arranged that when the ushers saw Queen Blanche coming to the chamber of the king, her son, they
struck the doors with their staffs, and the king came running back to his chamber so that his
mother would find him there. The ushers of Queen Margaret's chamber did the same when Queen
Blanche came there, so that she would find Queen Margaret in her chamber.

They're married. It's their own home. And they need lookouts so the king can talk to his wife.

While Louis is away on crusade, news arrives from France. Joinville recalls:

At Sidon news came to the king that his mother had died. He grieved so deeply that for two days no
one was able to speak to him. After that, he sent one of his chamber servants to fetch me. When I
came before him in his chamber, where he was all alone, he saw me, stretched out his arms, and
said: “Ah, Seneschal, I have lost my mother!” [...] My lady Marie of Vertus, a very good and holy
woman, came to tell me that the queen was grieving deeply, and asked me to go to comfort her. When
I arrived, I found her weeping, and I told her that whoever says one should not believe a woman
speaks truly: “For the woman you hated most is dead, and yet you are grieving like this!” She told
me that she was not weeping for Blanche, but because of the distress the king was suffering in his
grief, and for her daughter—who later became queen of Navarre—who had been left in the custody of
men.

Joinville doesn't hesitate to ask a grieving queen a question like that. And she answers him
plainly. There is a spontaneity in these encounters, less self-censorship, less of the formal
hypocrisy we're used to. People say what comes into their heads. To us, that openness can seem
almost childlike at times.

## 15. Adults at play and chivalry recapitulated

<!-- chapter: CH-015; transcript: U-00650–U-00675 -->

These people also spend a great deal of time playing. Chess, checkers, tables—a game related to
backgammon. And they play for money, even at chess, because it's more fun that way. There's a
great deal of gambling at dice too, much to Louis's disapproval.

But they also play games most of us stop playing when we grow up. Knights and ladies get bored
together, and they play blind man's buff or hide-and-seek. Nobody finds that strange. Adults
aren't embarrassed to enjoy themselves like children.

Even on crusade. Joinville's neighbor in camp is the count of Eu. Here's what he remembers:

I will tell you about the games the count of Eu used to play on us. I had built a room where my
knights and I ate by the light from the doorway. The doorway faced the count of Eu's quarters; and
he, being very ingenious, made a small ballista that shot into the room. He had someone watch for
when we were seated at our meal, then set his ballista lengthwise toward our table and had it
fired, breaking our pots and glasses. I had stocked up on hens and capons, and I do not know who
had given him a young bear, which he let loose among my hens; it had killed a dozen before anyone
could get there, while the woman who looked after them beat the bear with her distaff.

The woman is trying to fend off a bear with a spinning rod. And these are the "games."

Imagine games like that in today's palaces of power. Well, we'd better bring this to a close
before midnight comes and our coach turns into a pumpkin.

These men take pride in knowing how to behave, in being braver and better than everybody else.
Sometimes that pride gets them into terrible trouble.

## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

<!-- chapter: CH-016; transcript: U-00676–U-00698 -->

At Mansurah in 1250, the Templars have the vanguard. In the Holy Land, they're entitled to that
position: they attack first, the place of honor. The plan calls for different groups of knights
to go into action one after another. Robert of Artois, the king's brother, commands one of those
groups, and he has some rather overbearing advisers around him. The enemy gives way, and
Robert's men pick up speed and pass the Templars, breaking the agreed order.

Now what are the Templars supposed to do? Let somebody else take their place while they hang back?
They spur their horses on, and the men behind them do the same. Nobody wants the disgrace of
being left behind. What was supposed to be a series of coordinated attacks becomes a headlong
race. The pursuit carries them into the city streets, where they're trapped. Robert is
killed, and so are most of those Templars. Shortages, illness, and a failed withdrawal will bring
the crusade to its final collapse. But already, the army has paid terribly for this race for
honor.

A knight has to show courage. A soldier also has to know when to hold back. It's a difficult
balance—although that same fear of disgrace can sometimes keep a frightened man at his post.

## 17. “We shall speak of this day in the ladies' chambers”

<!-- chapter: CH-017; transcript: U-00699–U-00726 -->

During the battle of Mansurah on February 8, 1250, Joinville and the count of Soissons help hold a
small bridge so attackers cannot strike Louis's forces from front and rear. Other knights and
royal sergeants are nearby. Enemy fighters hurl darts, earth, and Greek fire at the defenders, and
royal crossbowmen eventually drive them off. <!-- accuracy: [N-015] -->

And in the middle of it, Joinville hears this:

The good count of Soissons, there where we stood, joked with me and said: “Seneschal, let this
pack of curs howl; for by God's coif!—that was how he swore—you and I will yet speak of this day
in the ladies' chambers.”

And Joinville does live to tell it. Long enough to put it in his book, so that we can hear it too.
Thank you.
