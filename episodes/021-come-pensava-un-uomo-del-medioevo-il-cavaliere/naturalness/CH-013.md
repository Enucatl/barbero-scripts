## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00550–U-00588 -->

How do knights explain the privilege they claim? In their own eyes, the answer is their work in
war. They train to fight on horseback and risk their lives for a lord, their companions, and, on
crusade, the cross. They see that danger as a service that sets them apart from people who work and
make money in other ways.

Training begins young. Riding and fighting in armor take years of practice. A medieval poem makes
that early start a test of who can become a knight.

A roughly contemporary didactic poem gives the maxim in a different form: Whoever goes on without
learning to ride and remains at school until the age of twelve is thereafter poorly suited to
knighthood: he lacks the strength for it. The unridden schoolboy is fit for nothing but the
priesthood.

The poem makes the choice sound stark: learn to ride early or enter the Church. That is the ideal
behind a knight's claim to authority, though armies contain many other people who face danger too.

Being at war means long hours in heat, cold, and frost, wearing armor, often with little to eat or
drink. Nobles tell themselves these things. Armies also depend on infantrymen, servants, and other
people on foot who face grave danger. Yet a story told by a knight tends to put the mounted warrior
at its center. Joinville's picture of Louis landing in Egypt shows why that image is so compelling.

Joinville describes Louis’s landing and appearance in battle: When the king heard that the standard
of Saint-Denis was ashore, he strode rapidly through his vessel and would not stop for the legate
who was with him. He leapt into the sea, where the water came up to his armpits, and went with his
shield at his neck, his helmet on his head, and his lance in his hand until he reached his men on
the seashore. When he came ashore and saw the Saracens, he asked what people they were. He was told
they were Saracens; he lowered the lance beneath his armpit, put his shield before him, and would
have charged the Saracens if the worthy men with him had allowed it. [...] Never did I see so fine
an armed man, for he stood out above all his people from the shoulders upward, with a gilded helmet
on his head and a German sword in his hand.

Louis is ready to charge even before his men can organize around him. Joinville remembers the
scene as an image of courage, rank, and the physical presence of a king. This is what knights mean
when they say their dangerous calling earns them a special place.

Naturally, there are other things that are also supposed to justify their privileges. They should be
the best mannered, most courteous and refined, the people who know how to behave. In part it is
true: they attach importance to these things. Then the scenes Joinville recounts show us that
reality was often different.
