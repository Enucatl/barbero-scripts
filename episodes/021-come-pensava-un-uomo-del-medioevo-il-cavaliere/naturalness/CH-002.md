## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00063–U-00118 -->

Anyone who pictures a Middle Ages dominated by religion won't be disappointed here. For nearly
everyone, religion is a part of life experienced with an intensity few people today can imagine. And
I'm not talking only about Louis IX, who is eventually made a saint. I thought about giving you the
full list of Masses and divine offices he hears every day, but we'd spend far too long on it. He
hears several Masses and quite a few offices, sung and unsung, every single day. He is a saint.
Joinville is simply a knight and an ordinary lord, yet even he hears one Mass a day. A Christian's
day—a knight's day—begins with Mass.

Religion is always present, and these people have a natural familiarity with sacred things. Dogs
wander into medieval churches, so we certainly aren't going to let one frighten us here. Let me
tell you what happens on the voyage home from the crusade. After six years away, with so many of
their friends dead, Joinville and his companions stop at Lampedusa. Among the rocks they find an old
hermitage, with a garden and an oratory. The place appears to have been abandoned.

Joinville recounts the episode this way: We came to an island called Lampedusa, where we caught a
great many rabbits. We found an ancient hermitage among the rocks and the garden made by the hermits
who had once lived there: it contained olive trees, fig trees, grapevines, and other trees. A stream
from the spring ran through the garden. The king and the rest of us went to the far end of the
garden and found an oratory in the first vault, whitewashed with lime, with a red earthen cross. We
entered the second vault and found the bodies of two dead people whose flesh had entirely decayed;
their ribs still held together, the bones of their hands lay upon their chests, and they lay facing
east, in the manner in which bodies are buried. When we reassembled aboard our ship, one of our
sailors was missing. The shipmaster supposed that he had remained there to become a hermit; and so
Nicholas of Soissons, the king's master sergeant, left three sacks of ship's biscuit on the shore,
so that the man might find them and live on them.

Think about that missing sailor. They have just found an empty oratory and two bodies laid out as if
for burial. When the sailor fails to come back, the shipmaster's explanation is that he has stayed
to become a hermit. No one knows whether that is what the man actually intended. But the crew takes
the possibility seriously enough to leave him food before sailing on. For them, the idea that a man
might be moved by this place to devote his life to God needs no elaborate explanation. Joinville
tells the story as an ordinary part of the voyage.

And miracles belong to that same religious world. If you expect miracles, you can be certain that
you have encountered one; afterward, the memory stays with you. For survivors returning from a
catastrophic crusade, such a story matters all the more.

Joinville has another story from the homeward voyage. A squire on a ship ahead of his falls into the
sea while doing some work aboard. Joinville does not see the fall. From his own ship, he sees a
motionless shape in the water and takes it for an object that has fallen overboard. A royal galley
goes to recover it and finds the squire alive and well. Joinville's next question only makes sense
once we know what he saw.

Joinville reports what the rescued man told him and what he did afterward: One of the king's
galleys picked him up and brought him to our ship, where he told us how it had happened. I asked him
why he had made no effort to save himself, either by swimming or in any other way. He answered that
there was no need for him to make any effort, because, as soon as he began to fall, he commended
himself to Our Lady of Vauvert, and she supported him by the shoulders from the moment he fell until
the king's galley picked him up. In honor of this miracle, I had it painted in my chapel at
Joinville and in the windows at Blécourt.

Joinville has seen the man floating motionless, and now he has heard the man's explanation. For him,
that is a miracle. He says little more about it here, but he has the rescue painted in his chapel
at Joinville and in the church windows at Blécourt. The image lets other people see the event as he
understands it. A sailor might stay behind to become a hermit; a man kept afloat might owe his life
to the Virgin. This is how naturally faith enters their account of what happens around them, with
great fervor and great simplicity.
