## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-00589–U-00649 -->

Joinville recounts the scene and its aftermath: On the eve of Shrove Tuesday I saw a marvel that I
wish to recount to you, for that same day Sir Hugh of Landricourt, who served under my banner, was
buried. While he lay on his bier in my chapel, six of my knights were leaning on several sacks full
of barley. Because they were talking loudly in my chapel and making noise that disturbed the priest,
I went to tell them to be quiet, and told them it was a disgraceful thing for knights and gentlemen
to talk while Mass was being sung. They began to laugh at me and told me, laughing, that they were
arranging the remarriage of his wife. I reproached them and told them that such words were neither
good nor proper, and that they had quickly forgotten their companion. God took such vengeance on
them that the next day was the great battle of Shrove Tuesday, in which they were killed or mortally
wounded; consequently all six of their wives had to remarry.

Joinville explicitly criticizes Louis’s distance from his family: The queen, who had recently
recovered from giving birth to Lady Blanche at Jaffa, arrived at Sidon by sea. When I heard she had
arrived, I rose from before the king, went to meet her, and brought her to the castle. When I
returned to the king, who was in his chapel, he asked whether the queen and the children were well,
and I said they were. He said to me: “I know well that when you rose from before me, you went to
meet the queen; that is why I had the sermon held for you.” I remind you of these things because I
had already been around him for five years, yet I had never heard him speak of the queen or his
children, either to me or to anyone else; and it was not good conduct, as it seems to me, to be
aloof from one's wife and children.

If we wanted—well, we still have five minutes. There were reasons for this in King Louis, and he is
another figure who would yield interesting results if one applied a little psychoanalysis. The king
lost his father very young and remained for years under his mother's guardianship. Queen Blanche of
Castile was a formidable woman of power who governed the kingdom for years while he was a child.
When he grew up he took control of the kingdom himself, but Blanche of Castile, his mother, was
always present and controlled everything. The king had to marry, because a king must marry and
produce heirs. But Blanche did not get along with her daughter-in-law and did everything she could
to keep her away from her husband.

Joinville describes the arrangement at Pontoise: Queen Blanche's harsh treatment of Queen Margaret
went so far that, as much as she could, Blanche would not allow her son to be in his wife's company
except in the evening, when he went to bed with her. The residence where the king and queen most
liked to stay was Pontoise, because the king's chamber was above and the queen's chamber below. They
arranged matters so that they held their conversations on a spiral staircase leading from one
chamber to the other. They had also arranged that when the ushers saw Queen Blanche coming to the
chamber of the king, her son, they struck the doors with their staffs, and the king came running
back to his chamber so that his mother would find him there. The ushers of Queen Margaret's chamber
did the same when Queen Blanche came there, so that she would find Queen Margaret in her chamber.

Joinville records the reactions to Blanche of Castile’s death: At Sidon news came to the king that
his mother had died. He grieved so deeply that for two days no one was able to speak to him. After
that, he sent one of his chamber servants to fetch me. When I came before him in his chamber, where
he was all alone, he saw me, stretched out his arms, and said: “Ah, Seneschal, I have lost my
mother!” [...] My lady Marie of Vertus, a very good and holy woman, came to tell me that the queen
was grieving deeply, and asked me to go to comfort her. When I arrived, I found her weeping, and I
told her that whoever says one should not believe a woman speaks truly: “For the woman you hated
most is dead, and yet you are grieving like this!” She told me that she was not weeping for Blanche,
but because of the distress the king was suffering in his grief, and for her daughter—who later
became queen of Navarre—who had been left in the custody of men.
