## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00062 -->

Good evening, everyone. This is our third episode in this attempt to get to know the people of the
Middle Ages. And as I say that, I realize I haven't talked about women. That's a real problem. The
overwhelming majority of people who wrote in the Middle Ages were men. There were women too, of
course, and perhaps I should have thought of that sooner. Next year, I promise, if you'd like. But
I've been lazy, because when we think about the Middle Ages, we tend to divide it into categories,
don't we? That's exactly what I've done here: the friar, the knight, the merchant, the peasant.

Women are harder to fit into these boxes. They have a social position, but they also have something
more, at least in this period. But again, perhaps we'll come back to them.

In this episode, the knight. The knight we're talking about is a Frenchman named Jean de Joinville.
He's a thirteenth-century knight, a contemporary of Friar Salimbene, whom we discussed in the
previous episode. Strictly speaking, Joinville is more than a knight: he commands knights and is a
great lord. Most knights are gentlemen who live well and have land, servants, and horses, but serve
someone else—a castellan or great lord who is also a knight, only at a higher level.

Joinville is that kind of great lord. He owns several castles and holds a lordship where he is the
public authority. He keeps order, collects taxes, and administers justice. On top of that, he is a
high royal official. For years Joinville serves the king of France as seneschal of Champagne, the
king's administrator and representative in the region.

But for all that, he is still a knight. Being a knight means being raised a certain way and sharing
certain values. Some are the ideals described in the medieval romances we all picture—and that they
read too—where the knight must protect widows and orphans, remain humble, and so on. In practice,
knights value rather different things. The values Joinville shares with his vassals are valor in
war, the physical courage to risk your skin in battle, comradeship among companions in arms, and
honor.

Jean de Joinville is famous among historians, and I can tell you about him because he did something
rather unusual for a knight: he wrote a book. And he wrote it for a specific reason. As I said, he
served the king of France for a long time, especially Louis IX—Saint Louis—the great
thirteenth-century king who went on two crusades and died during the second. Joinville accompanied
him on the first, an expedition that left in 1248 and returned six years later—those few who
returned, that is. Joinville didn't join the king's second crusade. Like many survivors of the
first, he preferred not to go. The king went and died there in 1270.

Joinville outlives his king by many years. By the beginning of the fourteenth century, he is an old
man living in his castle at Joinville, during the same period when Dino Compagni—the subject of the
previous episode—is caught up in Florentine politics. Joinville realizes he is one of the few people
left who remembers the saintly king. By then Louis IX really has become a saint: he has been
canonized and is officially Saint Louis. Joinville wants to write, someone asks him to write, and so
he produces this extraordinary book. It is supposed to be a life of Louis IX, but it is really the
memoir of Jean de Joinville, a great French lord of his time, telling above all the epic story of
the crusade.

So the man before us is not just a knight but a crusading knight. And I don't think it will surprise
anyone that religion occupies a central place in his worldview, especially when he looks back on the
crusade.
