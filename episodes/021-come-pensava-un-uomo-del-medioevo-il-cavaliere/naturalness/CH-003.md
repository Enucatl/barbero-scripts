## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00119–U-00157 -->

Sometimes there is even too much simplicity. Louis IX tells Joinville a story about a disputation
between Christian clerics and Jews at Cluny, the great Benedictine monastery in Burgundy. Joinville
repeats it with approval. Medieval churchmen enjoy these public arguments: they can display their
learning before an audience, much as knights show their skill in tournaments. Perhaps you remember
something similar from our discussion of Friar Salimbene.

Joinville writes: He told me that there was a great disputation between clerics and Jews at the
monastery of Cluny. There was a knight
there to whom the abbot had given bread in the house for God's sake. He asked the abbot to let him
speak first, and permission was granted only reluctantly. Then he rose, leaned on his staff, and
asked them to bring him the greatest scholar and foremost master among the Jews, which they did. He
asked him this question: 'Master,' said the knight, 'I ask whether you believe that the Virgin Mary,
who bore God in her womb and in her arms, gave birth as a virgin and is the mother of God.' The Jew
replied that he believed none of it. The knight answered that he had acted like a great fool by
entering her monastery and her house when he neither believed in her nor loved her. 'And truly,'
said the knight, 'you will pay for it.' Then he raised his crutch, struck the Jew beside the ear,
and knocked him to the ground. The Jews fled and carried away their master badly wounded; and that
was the end of the disputation.

Joinville then gives Louis’s conclusion: 'And so I tell you,' said the king, 'that no one should
dispute with them unless he is a very learned cleric. But when a layman hears the Christian faith
spoken ill of, he should defend the Christian faith only with his sword, which he should drive into
the offender's belly as far as it can go.'

The knight ends the argument with a blow, and Louis tells Joinville that an untrained layman should
go further still. This faith is spontaneous, impetuous, sometimes violent, and intensely felt. It is
also deeply attached to ritual and form. A simple faith means caring about
doing the right things—observing the Friday fast and the Lenten fast—and feeling terrible when you
fail. These practices have lost much of their importance in Christianity today, but Christians then
feel powerfully bound to observe them.
