## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00507–U-00549 -->

Joinville knows that some wealthy commoners now own castles and hold important positions at court.
For someone who treats noble birth as a mark of superiority, their rise is hard to accept. He
enjoys stories in which a powerful commoner is reminded that money cannot make him a knight.

The first story takes place at the count of Champagne's court. Watch what happens when the count's
wealthy associate Artaud de Nogent interrupts a poor knight asking for help.

Joinville’s actual anecdote names Artaud de Nogent and runs as follows: Artaud de Nogent was the
burgess in all the world whom the count trusted most, and he was so rich that he built the castle of
Nogent-l'Artaud with his own money. [...] At the foot of the steps a poor knight came before him,
knelt, and said: 'My lord, I beg you for God's sake to give me something of yours with which I may
marry my two daughters, whom you see here.' Artaud, who was walking behind him, said to the poor
knight: 'Sir knight, you are behaving discourteously by asking my lord, for he has given so much
that he has nothing left to give.' The generous count turned toward Artaud and said: 'Sir villein,
what you say—that I have nothing left to give—is not true. I still have you yourself. Here, sir
knight: I give him to you, and I will guarantee him to you.' The knight was not taken aback; instead
he seized Artaud by the cloak and said he would not release him until Artaud had settled with him.
Before Artaud escaped him, he had settled with the knight for five hundred livres.

The count uses his authority and Artaud's fear of it to force a payment to the knight. That is the
pleasure of the anecdote for Joinville: the rich burgess pays for speaking as if he could limit his
lord's generosity.

The next case concerns one of the king's sergeants and a knight in Joinville's company. A shove at
Caesarea becomes a matter of justice. The public ritual that follows makes the difference in status
unmistakable.

Joinville describes the judgment imposed on the royal sergeant Le Goulu: The third judgment that I
saw given at Caesarea was this. One of the king's sergeants, named Le Goulu, laid hands on a knight
of my company. I went to complain to the king. The king told me that, in his view, I could well let
the matter pass, since the sergeant had only shoved him. I said I would not let it pass, and that if
the king did not give me justice I would leave his service, since his sergeants were shoving
knights. He had justice done for me, and the judgment, according to the customs of the country, was
this: the sergeant came to my quarters barefoot, wearing only his shirt and breeches, with a bare
sword in his hand. He knelt before the knight, took the sword by its point, offered the pommel to
the knight, and said: 'My lord, I make amends to you for laying hands on you, and I have brought you
this sword so that you may cut off my fist, if it pleases you.' I asked the knight to forgive his
resentment against the sergeant, and he did so.

The knight forgives the sergeant; no hand is cut off. But Le Goulu has to kneel, offer the sword,
and ask for mercy. Both stories give Joinville the same satisfaction: rank is publicly affirmed,
even when the person of lower status has wealth or serves the king. What, in a knight's mind,
justifies a privilege this strong?
