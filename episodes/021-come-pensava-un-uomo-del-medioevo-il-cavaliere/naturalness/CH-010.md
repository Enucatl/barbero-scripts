## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00420–U-00458 -->

Louis's holiness is visible to the people around him, sometimes in acts they find hard to match.
At Sidon in 1253, on the Levantine coast, he helps carry decomposing Christian bodies into burial
trenches. <!-- accuracy: [N-008] --> He asks others to face a task they would rather avoid, and does it himself.

There is another example from 1250. A ship carrying Louis and hundreds of others is in danger near
Cyprus. His advisers urge him to leave by galley, but the other passengers have no such escape.

Joinville recalls Louis's answer: Then the king said: 'Lords, I
have heard your advice and the advice of my people; now I will tell you mine. If I leave the ship,
there are five hundred people or more aboard who, fearing danger to their lives—for there is not one
among them who does not love his life as much as I love mine—will remain on the island of Cyprus,
and who may perhaps never return to their own countries. Therefore I would rather place my own
person, my wife, and my children in God's hands than inflict such harm on so great a number of
people as are aboard.'

The king does not claim that his own life matters less. His point is that each passenger values
life as much as he does. Joinville admires that sense of obligation. He also knows that being close
to such a king can be uncomfortable: Louis expects the same moral seriousness from his friends.

One day Louis asks Joinville to choose between leprosy and mortal sin. Joinville answers without
hesitation, and Louis calls him back later to explain why he thinks the answer is wrong.

Joinville records both his blunt answer and Louis’s later rebuke: 'Now I ask you,' he said, 'which
would you rather: that you were a leper, or that you had committed a mortal sin?' And I, who never
lied to him, answered that I would rather have committed thirty than be a leper. When the friars had
left, he called me aside alone, made me sit at his feet, and said: 'Why did you say that to me
yesterday?' I told him that I still said the same. He said: 'You spoke like a rash fool, for you
should know that there is no leprosy so hideous as being in mortal sin, because the soul that is in
mortal sin resembles the Devil; therefore no leprosy can be so hideous. It is true that when a man
dies he is cured of the leprosy of the body. But when a man who has committed a mortal sin dies, he
does not know and cannot be certain that in his lifetime he repented in such a way that God pardoned
him; therefore he should greatly fear that this leprosy will last as long as God is in paradise. So
I beg you as earnestly as I can, for love of God and of me, to set your heart on preferring every
bodily misfortune—leprosy and every other illness—to mortal sin entering your soul.'

Joinville fears the disease; Louis fears what sin could do to the soul after death. Neither gives
way. Their next exchange brings that difference down from an abstract choice to a physical act:
Louis wants Joinville to wash poor people's feet on Maundy Thursday, as Christ did.

On washing the feet of the poor, Joinville records this exchange: He asked me whether I washed the
feet of the poor on Maundy Thursday. 'My lord,' I said, 'God forbid! I will never wash the feet of
those common people.' 'Truly,' he said, 'that was badly said, for you must not disdain what God did
for our instruction. So I ask you, first for the love of God and then for love of me, to accustom
yourself to washing them.'

Joinville's refusal is revealing. He can admire the saintly king and still recoil from touching
people he considers beneath him. That instinct leads us from Louis's religion to Joinville's
aristocratic view of society.
