## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00270–U-00307 -->

Serving God can demand a great deal. Joinville spends six years away on crusade, and he remembers
the moment he leaves his castle and his children. He goes first to visit nearby holy places on
foot, dressed simply, carrying a pilgrim's staff. But he cannot bring himself to look back.

Joinville describes his departure: The abbot of Cheminon gave me my pilgrim's scrip and staff; then
I departed from Joinville, without re-entering the castle until my return, on foot, barefoot, and
dressed in wool. In this way I went to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, and to the other holy relics that
are there. While I was going to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, I never once wished to turn my eyes back
toward Joinville, for fear that my heart would soften at the thought of the fine castle I was
leaving and of my two children.

He makes that sacrifice, but he also hopes to live and return. The distinction becomes clear when
the crusaders are defeated. Joinville and his men are aboard a ship, and the sultan's galleys are
closing in. Surrender is unavoidable. Their practical question is whom to surrender to: the men in
the galleys, who might keep them together, or those on shore, who might separate and sell them.

Joinville records the company’s decision and the lone objection: It was not long before we saw four
of the sultan's galleys approaching, carrying fully a thousand men. I called my knights and my
people and asked what they wished us to do: surrender to the sultan's galleys or surrender to those
on shore. We all agreed that we preferred to surrender to the sultan's galleys, because they would
keep us together, rather than to those on shore, who would scatter us and sell us to the Bedouins.
Then one of my cellarers, who came from Doullens, said: 'My lord, I do not agree with this counsel.'
I asked what counsel he favored, and he told me: 'I favor letting ourselves all be killed; then we
shall all go to paradise.' But we did not believe him.

The man proposing martyrdom is a cellarer, one of Joinville's household officers. He is the lone
voice for letting everyone die, and Joinville answers him dryly: they do not believe him. They
surrender instead. Deep faith and the desire to survive sit side by side here. Louis,
though, will carry one of their religious duties further than Joinville expects: he keeps his word
even to the men who have captured him.
