# Structural outline

## Overview

Barbero reconstructs the mental world of the thirteenth-century knight through Jean de
Joinville's recollections of Louis IX and the Egyptian crusade. The lecture does not present a
systematic doctrine. It accumulates sharply observed stories in which intense and familiar faith,
formal observance, practical self-interest, money, inherited rank, courage, courtesy, play, and
vanity coexist without seeming contradictory to Joinville. Louis IX acts as both the knight's
beloved model and his exasperating opposite: Joinville admires the saintly king but repeatedly
refuses to live at the same pitch of sanctity. The final battlefield stories expose the central
tension in chivalric culture: honor can sustain courage and military professionalism, but the same
competition for precedence can destroy an army.

The narrative moves from Joinville and his book, through religious experience and crusading
common sense, into the economics and social hierarchy of knighthood. It then explains the
aristocracy's self-justification through danger, training, courtesy, and display, before testing that
ideal in two contrasting battle scenes. Barbero's comic asides and delayed reversals are part of the
argument: they prevent the people described from becoming either pious stereotypes or modern
caricatures.

## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

**Range:** U-00001–U-00062
**Original audio:** 00:13–05:09
**Treatment:** preserve

Barbero introduces the lecture series, acknowledges that its social categories have excluded
women, and names Jean de Joinville as the evening's knight. Joinville is not an ordinary dependent
knight but a castle-owning lord, public authority, and seneschal of Champagne. Knightly romances
advertise protection of widows and orphans and humility, while the operative values among
Joinville and his vassals are battlefield courage, comradeship, and honor. Joinville is unusually
accessible because he wrote a life of Louis IX which becomes, in practice, his own memoir of the
king's first crusade. Written in old age after Louis's canonization, it makes the religious outlook
of a crusading knight the lecture's first subject.

- Opening aside: women resist the neat social boxes represented by friar, knight, merchant, and
  peasant.
- Narrative frame: the 1248 expedition, six-year absence, Louis's later crusade and death, and
  Joinville's decision not to accompany him again.
- **Q-001:** The literary prescription that a knight should protect widows and orphans and be
  humble; identify whether Joinville or a specific chivalric text is intended.
- **C-001:** Joinville's offices, landed authority, relationship to Champagne, and social standing.
- **C-002:** Dates and outcomes of Louis IX's two crusades, including Joinville's participation and
  Louis's death on the second.
- **C-003:** Composition, commission, date, genre, and purpose of Joinville's book after Louis IX's
  canonization.

## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

**Range:** U-00063–U-00118
**Original audio:** 05:10–10:13
**Treatment:** preserve

Religion is a daily, intensely lived presence rather than a separate sphere. Louis attends many
masses and offices, while even Joinville begins each day with mass. The homeward fleet finds an
abandoned hermitage and two skeletons on Lampedusa; a sailor spontaneously stays behind to become
its new hermit, and his companions simply leave provisions. Soon afterward a man falls overboard,
remains calm because the Virgin is holding him afloat, and is rescued. Joinville later has the
miracle depicted in his chapel and in the church windows at Blécourt. The stories establish both
the fervor and the unaffected simplicity of this religious world.

- Comic audience aside: dogs entered medieval churches, so a disturbance in the venue is no
  cause for alarm.
- **Q-002:** Joinville's account of the deserted Lampedusa hermitage, its dead hermits, and the
  sailor who remained there.
- **Q-003:** The rescued sailor's statement that he invoked the Virgin, who appeared and kept him
  afloat, and Joinville's statement that he commissioned images of it.
- **C-004:** The daily devotional routines attributed to Louis IX and Joinville.
- **C-005:** The fleet's route via Lampedusa and the historical/textual basis of the hermitage
  episode.
- **C-006:** Blécourt and Joinville as the locations where the miracle was represented.

## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

**Range:** U-00119–U-00157
**Original audio:** 10:16–13:49
**Treatment:** preserve; clarify the violence without muting Barbero's irony

Religious simplicity becomes aggressive and violent in a story Joinville approvingly heard at
Cluny. A retired knight asks to dispute first with a rabbi, asks whether he believes in the Virgin
Birth, and responds to the denial by striking him on the head with his crutch. Joinville and Louis
approve: learned clerics may debate unbelievers, but lay Christians should answer attacks on the
faith with the sword. Barbero links this confidence to a highly formal religion in which fasts and
other outward rules bind the believer powerfully.

- Analogy: clerical disputations are the intellectual counterpart of knights' tournaments.
- **Q-004:** The Cluny story, including the old knight's question, “fate molto male,” and the blow
  delivered to the rabbi.
- **Q-005:** Louis IX's advice that learned people may debate unbelievers but laypeople who hear
  the Christian faith maligned should draw the sword.
- **C-007:** Public Christian-Jewish disputations and the plausibility/provenance of the alleged
  Cluny setting.
- **C-008:** The place of Friday and Lenten fasting in thirteenth-century lay religious practice.

## 4. Meat on Friday: intention versus observance

**Range:** U-00158–U-00197
**Original audio:** 13:51–16:47
**Treatment:** preserve

After the crusaders' defeat, Joinville dines as a prisoner with an emir while they compare
genealogies and discuss Frederick II. A French prisoner suddenly points out that it is Friday.
Joinville recoils from the meat; both the emir and the papal legate reassure him that an unintended
act cannot offend God, but he nevertheless adopts a bread-and-water Friday fast as penance. The
episode makes the lecture's distinction precise: sincere, spontaneous faith is also inseparable
from formal rules.

- Social aside: Christian and Muslim kings and lords share status, acquaintances, and topics of
  conversation despite the war.
- **Q-006:** The prison meal dialogue: the warning about Friday meat, the emir's reassurance, the
  legate's ruling, and Joinville's self-imposed fast.
- **C-009:** The crusaders' defeat, capture, and differential treatment of ordinary prisoners and
  high-status captives.
- **C-010:** Joinville's claimed kinship to Frederick II and the emir's interest in the emperor.

## 5. Dangerous words: devils, oaths, and dismissal

**Range:** U-00198–U-00235
**Original audio:** 16:51–19:43
**Treatment:** preserve

Everyone invokes God and the devil in ordinary speech, though Joinville praises Louis for doing
neither and penalizes servants who mention the devil. Calling God as witness is acceptable but
turns an angry declaration into a binding oath. When two of his knights quarrel over the placement
of his tent, Joinville swears by God's help that the aggressor will never enter his household again.
He wants to relent, but the papal legate refuses to dissolve a valid oath, and Joinville permanently
loses the knight. His lesson is practical: take care with words.

- Language aside: *sire* could address lords as well as kings in Old French.
- **Q-007:** Joinville's catalogue of habitual oaths, including “the head,” “wounds,” and “cap” of
  God, and his rule against mentioning the devil.
- **Q-008:** Joinville's dismissal oath and the papal legate's refusal to release him from it.
- **C-011:** The religious and legal force of lay oaths and the authority to dispense from them.

## 6. A practical bargain with God

**Range:** U-00236–U-00269
**Original audio:** 19:57–22:02
**Treatment:** preserve; mark Barbero's “contract” as interpretation

Joinville expects God, the Virgin, and the saints to intervene concretely in danger. He rejects an
account of Bedouin fatalism—the belief that the date of death is written on the forehead—because it
would make prayer and divine help pointless. His conclusion, as Barbero draws it out, is startlingly
transactional: people serve God because God can prolong life and help in difficulty. Warm, sincere
faith therefore coexists with attention to tangible advantage.

- **Q-009:** Joinville's report of the Bedouin belief that a person's death is fixed and written on
  the forehead.
- **Q-010:** Joinville's rhetorical conclusion that those who serve God would be fools if God could
  not prolong their lives and help them.
- **C-012:** The textual wording and context behind Barbero's characterization of a “contract with
  God.”
- **C-013:** Whether Joinville specifically attributes this form of fatalism to Bedouins and how he
  understood it.

## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

**Range:** U-00270–U-00307
**Original audio:** 22:07–24:43
**Treatment:** preserve

Crusading entails real renunciation: Joinville leaves wife, children, and castle for six years and
refuses to look back during his pilgrim-like departure. But renunciation is governed by common
sense; knights do not seek death for its own sake. When Saracen galleys surround Joinville's ship,
the company sensibly agrees to surrender. Only a cellarer proposes that all be killed and go
straight to paradise. The supposedly typical medieval fanatic is the solitary exception, dismissed
with Joinville's dry “we did not listen to him.”

- Barbero explicitly plays the audience's expected image of “the Middle Ages” against the group's
  behavior.
- **Q-011:** Joinville's departure without looking back at his castle, wife, or children.
- **Q-012:** The cellarer's proposal to be killed and go directly to paradise, and Joinville's
  laconic rejection.
- **C-014:** Joinville's departure rites, duration away, family circumstances, and travel chronology.
- **C-015:** The naval surrender episode and the survival/capture of Joinville's household.

## 8. A saint keeps his word—and takes a joke

**Range:** U-00308–U-00361
**Original audio:** 25:08–29:03
**Treatment:** preserve

Louis insists that the full agreed ransom be paid even after Philippe de Nemours claims to have
shorted the captors by ten thousand livres. Joinville tries to pass the boast off as a joke, but the
king orders a check. This uncompromising saintliness makes Louis admirable and difficult to live
with. Yet he can laugh: when Armenian pilgrims ask to see the holy king, Joinville tells Louis he
has no desire yet to kiss his bones, an irreverent joke whose logic depends on medieval relic
veneration.

- Delayed reversal: Joinville and Philippe try to save face by claiming the Saracens are too good
  at counting to be deceived.
- **Q-013:** Louis IX's insistence on paying the full 200,000 livres tournois ransom and the exchange
  involving Philippe de Nemours and Joinville.
- **Q-014:** Joinville's “I do not want to kiss your bones” remark to Louis when Armenian pilgrims
  ask to see the saint.
- **C-016:** The ransom amount, negotiation, payment, and identities of the participants.
- **C-017:** The Armenian pilgrims' passage through the crusader camp and medieval practices of
  venerating saints' relics.

## 9. Crusading accounts and a payment in patience

**Range:** U-00362–U-00419
**Original audio:** 29:17–33:07
**Treatment:** preserve

Barbero interrupts the idealized language of faith, martyrdom, and courtesy to insist that crusades
require enormous sums. Knights normally serve under paid contracts; even vassals do not travel and
fight for free. Joinville accounts for his landed income, the cost of ten knights, and the king's
payment for his contingent. When renewal falls due and Louis has no money, Joinville offers six
months' free service in exchange for the king's promise not to become angry with him. Louis laughs
and proudly tells everyone. Money matters, but the aristocrat proves he is not a merchant by making
the magnificent gesture at the right moment.

- Modern aside: Joinville decides to “fare lo splendido.”
- **Q-015:** Joinville's account of his income, expenditure on ten knights, and contractual service
  to the king.
- **Q-016:** Joinville's offer to serve for six months without pay if Louis promises not to become
  angry with him.
- **C-018:** The decline or limits of unpaid feudal service and the use of paid contracts in Louis
  IX's crusading army.
- **C-019:** The scale and organization of crusade finance, recruitment, horses, attendants, and
  shipping.

## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

**Range:** U-00420–U-00458
**Original audio:** 33:29–36:14
**Treatment:** preserve

Louis personally begins burying decaying battlefield corpses until the ashamed army joins him. He
also refuses evacuation unless everyone can be saved, because others love their lives as he loves
his own. His demands then become comic in conversation with Joinville. Asked whether he would
prefer leprosy or mortal sin, Joinville chooses thirty mortal sins and receives a lecture. Invited
to imitate Christ by washing poor people's feet on Maundy Thursday, he swears that he will never
wash the feet of those peasants. The final refusal opens the transition from religion to class.

- **Q-017:** Louis's refusal to leave the expedition without the others: they love their lives as
  much as he loves his.
- **Q-018:** Louis's leprosy-or-mortal-sin question and Joinville's answer of “thirty mortal sins.”
- **Q-019:** Joinville's oath that he will not wash the feet of those peasants on Maundy Thursday.
- **C-020:** Louis IX's personal burial of corpses and the circumstances of the proposed evacuation.
- **C-021:** Royal Maundy foot-washing and the episode's devotional context.

## 11. Noble blood against Robert de Sorbon

**Range:** U-00459–U-00506
**Original audio:** 36:24–39:48
**Treatment:** preserve; clarify that the hierarchy described is the speakers' worldview

For Joinville, society divides automatically into noble knights—the best people—and *villains*, a
category extending beyond peasants to merchants and financiers. Robert de Sorbon publicly rebukes
Joinville for dressing more richly than the king. Joinville answers that his inherited clothes suit
his lineage, whereas Robert wears fabrics unavailable to his low-born father. Louis initially
protects the humiliated cleric, then privately concedes that Joinville was right: nobles may display
what their ancestors displayed, but a rich parvenu may not claim equality. Barbero notes the long
afterlife of this aristocratic conviction.

- **Q-020:** Robert de Sorbon's complaint about Joinville's dress and Joinville's reply about their
  fathers.
- **Q-021:** Louis IX's private apology and alleged endorsement of inherited noble display over the
  dress of a wealthy parvenu.
- **C-022:** Robert de Sorbon's origin, office, and role in founding the college later associated
  with the University of Paris.
- **C-023:** The meaning and social range of *vilain* in Joinville's milieu.
- **C-024:** Barbero's claim that this sense of intrinsic noble superiority endured into the
  nineteenth century.

## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

**Range:** U-00507–U-00549
**Original audio:** 39:55–43:02
**Treatment:** preserve

Wealthy commoners buy castles and gain court office, making their ritual humiliation satisfying to
knightly audiences. At the count of Champagne's court, Érard de Nogent dismisses a poor knight who
needs dowries for his daughters. The count gives Érard himself to the knight as a captive to be
ransomed. In a harsher crusade episode, a royal sergeant shoves one of Joinville's knights. Custom
supposedly allows the knight to cut off the commoner's hand. The barefoot, shirt-clad sergeant must
offer it; everyone then grants the expected pardon, but hierarchy has been publicly reaffirmed.

- **Q-022:** The count of Champagne's words and actions in handing Érard de Nogent to the poor
  knight for ransom.
- **Q-023:** The prescribed apology in which the royal sergeant offers his hand to the knight who
  may cut it off.
- **C-025:** Érard de Nogent's identity, status, and the provenance of the ransom anecdote.
- **C-026:** The custom and legal/social procedure invoked after a non-noble struck or shoved a
  knight.

## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

**Range:** U-00550–U-00588
**Original audio:** 43:08–45:56
**Treatment:** preserve; clarify Barbero's caveat about infantry and servants

The aristocracy justifies superiority through its profession: knights risk their lives for lord,
comrades, loyalty, cause, and cross. Riding, charging, falling, and recovering in heavy armor demand
lifelong training begun in childhood. Knights emphasize their endurance while overlooking the
infantry and servants often killed first. Joinville's image of Louis leaping from his galley into
the sea, armed and eager to charge alone, embodies the courage that supposedly licenses privilege.
Courtesy and refinement provide a second justification, though the stories repeatedly show a gap
between ideal and conduct.

- **Q-024:** The medieval proverb that a boy not yet mounted at eight is fit only to become a priest.
- **Q-025:** Joinville's description of Louis IX landing in Egypt on horseback, lance in hand and
  wearing a gilded helmet.
- **C-027:** Childhood training, armor, horses, attendants, and the physical demands of knightly
  warfare; verify rather than simply retain the “forty kilograms” figure.
- **C-028:** The tactical and casualty roles of infantry, household servants, and mounted knights in
  the crusading army.
- **C-029:** The landing in Egypt and Louis's place in the assault.

## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

**Range:** U-00589–U-00649
**Original audio:** 46:08–50:31
**Treatment:** preserve; label psychoanalytic speculation as Barbero's aside

Joinville rebukes knights who laugh over a still-warm comrade's widow and retrospectively reads
their deaths and their wives' remarriages as punishment. Grace in women's chambers is another
chivalric value, making Louis's distance from wife and children a defect. Barbero speculates that
Louis's attachment to Blanche of Castile helps explain it. Joinville describes the queen mother's
efforts to separate the young couple, their secret staircase at Pontoise, Louis's devastation at
Blanche's death, and his wife's blunt admission that she weeps only because she fears for her
husband—not for the mother-in-law she hated. Their candor seems almost childlike to modern eyes.

- **Q-026:** Joinville's rebuke of the knights joking about their dead companion's remarriage, and
  his claim that their subsequent deaths punished them.
- **Q-027:** Joinville's criticism that Louis treated his wife and children almost like strangers.
- **Q-028:** The Pontoise staircase and doorkeeper story about Blanche keeping Louis and his wife
  apart.
- **Q-029:** Louis's “I have lost my mother” and the exchange in which the queen says she grieves
  for his condition, not for her mother-in-law.
- **C-030:** Louis IX's childhood, father's death, Blanche of Castile's regency and continuing
  political/familial influence.
- **C-031:** The identity of Louis's wife and the couple's residence and room arrangement at
  Pontoise.

## 15. Adults at play and chivalry recapitulated

**Range:** U-00650–U-00675
**Original audio:** 50:35–52:31
**Treatment:** preserve

Medieval adults openly play chess, draughts, backgammon, dice, blind man's buff, and hide-and-seek,
often for money. During the crusade, the count of Eu builds a small catapult at lunch and gleefully
smashes Joinville's glasses across the neighboring table. Barbero uses the prank, the spotless white
tablecloth as status display, and a Cinderella-at-midnight closing joke to recapitulate a culture
that demands manners and distinction from commoners while embracing exuberant play. Its members
claim privilege because they are supposedly braver and risk more than others.

- Modern analogy: imagine comparable games in today's seats of power.
- **Q-030:** Joinville's account of the count of Eu's miniature catapult and the broken drinking
  vessels.
- **C-032:** Adult play, gambling, and the listed games in medieval aristocratic culture.
- **C-033:** White table linen and tableware as aristocratic status markers.

## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

**Range:** U-00676–U-00698
**Original audio:** 52:38–54:14
**Treatment:** preserve; depart-pending on the causal weight assigned to this episode

The crusade's failure illustrates chivalric excess. Templars are assigned the honorable vanguard,
but Robert of Artois and his advisers overtake them. The Templars accelerate rather than accept the
shame of falling behind, every contingent follows, and the planned sequence becomes a headlong
race. Robert and nearly all the Templars die. Barbero concludes that vainglory can cause catastrophe,
while distinguishing it from the professional soldier's knowledge. Honor can wreck discipline,
but it can also sustain courage in danger.

- **C-034:** Vanguard precedence, Robert of Artois's advance, the Templars' response, and the
  sequence and outcome of the battle.
- **C-035:** The degree to which this undisciplined charge caused the defeat of Louis IX's crusade.

## 17. “We shall speak of this day in the ladies' chambers”

**Range:** U-00699–U-00726
**Original audio:** 54:16–56:14
**Treatment:** preserve

During the retreat, Joinville and the count of Soissons alone hold a bridge while enemy soldiers
threaten, stone, and shoot at them. Soissons mocks Joinville's fear and swears “by God's cap” that
they will yet discuss this day together in the ladies' chambers. The boast fuses courage, honor,
courtly love, and retrospective storytelling: first they must win and survive, then they may make
themselves look splendid before women. Joinville survives long enough to tell the story, allowing
Barbero to close by handing it on to the audience.

- **Q-031:** The count of Soissons's battlefield speech: “let this rabble shout,” the oath “by
  God's cap,” and the promise to speak of the day in the ladies' chambers.
- **C-036:** The bridge defense, the identities of Joinville and the count of Soissons, and the
  battle context.

## Recurring terminology

- **cavaliere / chevalier:** knight as mounted warrior, social rank, and moral identity.
- **signore / sire:** lord; *sire* is not restricted to a king in the speech described.
- **siniscalco / sénéchal:** seneschal; Joinville's office and Soissons's battlefield address to him.
- **vassallo:** vassal, distinct from a knight hired by contract though categories may overlap.
- **villano / vilain:** context-dependent commoner or low-born person, not merely a modern moral
  “villain”; sometimes specifically a peasant.
- **borghese:** wealthy town-dweller/commoner, not modern ideological “bourgeois” by default.
- **gentiluomo:** noble/gentleman, carrying hereditary status.
- **chierico:** cleric or learned churchman; not always adequately rendered simply as “priest.”
- **saraceno / turco:** Joinville's and Barbero's historical labels for Muslim adversaries; preserve
  source perspective while clarifying referents.
- **emiro:** emir, here a high-status Muslim captor and dining companion.
- **galera:** galley, not a generic modern ship.
- **lira tornese:** *livre tournois*, the currency unit used for the ransom.
- **cortesia / amor cortese:** courtesy and courtly love as aristocratic conduct and display.
- **onore / posto d'onore:** honor and precedence, both ethical motive and tactical hazard.
