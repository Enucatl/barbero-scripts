# How a Medieval Man Thought: The Knight — assembled faithful translation

## 1. Introducing Joinville and the knight's memoir

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00062 -->

Good evening. Well, good evening, everyone. This evening we are at our third appointment, so to speak, in this attempt to get to know the people of the Middle Ages. As I say that, I realize that I haven't talked about women. There is a big problem here. The overwhelming majority of the people who wrote in the Middle Ages were men. There were women too, and perhaps I could have thought of that sooner. Next year, I promise you, if you like, here. But I have been lazy, because when we think about the Middle Ages, we always think of it as divided up a little like this, into categories, don't we? Just as I have done here. The friar, the knight, the merchant, the peasant—with women it is more difficult. Women are, how can I put it, they have a social position, but they also have something more. They are harder to put into boxes, at least in that period. But I repeat, perhaps we will talk about them again.

This evening, the knight. The knight we are talking about is a Frenchman. His name is Jean de Joinville. He is a knight of the thirteenth century, a contemporary of that Salimbene, that Friar Salimbene we talked about the day before yesterday. To tell the truth, he is something more than a knight: he is a leader of knights, a great lord. You know, most knights are gentlemen who live well, who have land, servants, horses, but who are in someone's service. They are in the service of a castellan, of a great lord, who is also a knight himself, but, in short, a knight at a somewhat higher level. Joinville is a man like that. He is someone who owns several castles, and therefore he possesses a lordship where he is the lord, where he is the public authority. He keeps order, collects taxes, administers justice, and on top of that he is also a high official of the king. Joinville served the king of France for years as seneschal of Champagne, that is, as the king's administrator and representative in the region of Champagne.

And with all that, he is a knight. Being a knight means having been educated in a certain way, sharing certain values, which perhaps are also the ones described in the romances, the medieval romances we all have in mind and which they also read, where it says that the knight must protect widows and orphans, be humble, and so forth. Then, in reality, the true values of the knights are rather different. The real values that Joinville shares with his vassals are valor in war, physical courage demonstrated in battle when you risk your skin, comradeship among companions in arms, honor.

That said, Jean de Joinville is famous among us historians, and I can talk to you about him this evening, because he did something that was rather unusual for knights. He wrote a book. He wrote a book for a precise reason. As I was telling you, he served the king of France for a long time, and the king he served most of all was Louis IX, Saint Louis, the great thirteenth-century king of France who took part in two crusades and then died on the second. Joinville accompanied him on the first crusade, an expedition that left in 1248 and returned—those few who returned—six years later. Joinville did not go on the king's second crusade. Like many survivors of the first one, he preferred not to go. The king went and died there in the year Seventy.

Joinville outlived his king by many years. When he was already old, at the beginning of the fourteenth century, in the same years when Dino Compagni, whom I told you about yesterday, was living through Florentine politics, in those same years, in his castle at Joinville, he realized that he was one of the few people left who remembered the saintly king. In the meantime, Louis IX had really been made a saint: they had canonized him, and by then he was officially a saint. And so Joinville felt the desire to write, and someone asked him to write, and he wrote—he wrote this extraordinary book that was supposed to be a life of Louis IX and that is in reality the memoirs of Jean de Joinville, of this great French lord of his time, telling above all this epic story of the crusade.

And so, and so he is not only a knight but a crusading knight, the man we have before us this evening. And I don't think I will surprise anyone if I say that in the worldview of this crusading knight, who moreover is specifically remembering the time of the crusade, well, in this worldview religion occupies a central place.

## 2. Familiar faith, Lampedusa, and a witnessed miracle

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00063–U-00118 -->

Anyone who has in mind, let's say, a Middle Ages dominated by religion will not be disappointed here. It is true that for many people, almost everyone, religion was in any case a dimension of life experienced with an intensity that few people today can imagine. I am not talking only about Louis IX, who was in fact made a saint. Earlier I thought I would tell you, I would give you the list of all the masses and divine offices that Louis IX heard every day of his life. Then I thought we would waste too much time on this long list, but the king heard several masses and quite a few divine offices, sung and unsung, every day. He was a saint. Joinville, who is a knight, an ordinary lord, nevertheless hears one mass a day. A Christian's day, a knight's day, begins with mass.

And religion is always present. There is a spontaneous familiarity with religious things that is characteristic of these people. Dogs used to enter churches in the Middle Ages, so we certainly are not going to let ourselves be frightened here. I will tell you an anecdote that gives you an idea of how they reasoned. They are returning from the crusade after being away for six years and seeing most of their friends die. Returning from the crusade, which took place in Egypt, they travel back by sea and at a certain point stop at Lampedusa, as naturally happens to people who leave Egypt and travel by sea.

Joinville recounts the episode this way: We came to an island called Lampedusa, where we caught a great many rabbits. We found an ancient hermitage among the rocks and the garden made by the hermits who had once lived there: it contained olive trees, fig trees, grapevines, and other trees. A stream from the spring ran through the garden. The king and the rest of us went to the far end of the garden and found an oratory in the first vault, whitewashed with lime, with a red earthen cross. We entered the second vault and found the bodies of two dead people whose flesh had entirely decayed; their ribs still held together, the bones of their hands lay upon their chests, and they lay facing east, in the manner in which bodies are buried. When we reassembled aboard our ship, one of our sailors was missing. The shipmaster supposed that he had remained there to become a hermit; and so Nicholas of Soissons, the king's master sergeant, left three sacks of ship's biscuit on the shore, so that the man might find them and live on them.

Just as it is normal for miracles to happen. You know, if you believe in miracles, they happen. At that time practically everyone believed in them, and so they happened. It was easy to be certain that you had witnessed a miracle. And witnessing a miracle was something important that remained impressed on you, that you carried with you. Especially during a catastrophic crusade like Saint Louis's, they needed many miracles to help the survivors return home.

Joinville reports what the rescued man told him and what he did afterward: One of the king's galleys picked him up and brought him to our ship, where he told us how it had happened. I asked him why he had made no effort to save himself, either by swimming or in any other way. He answered that there was no need for him to make any effort, because, as soon as he began to fall, he commended himself to Our Lady of Vauvert, and she supported him by the shoulders from the moment he fell until the king's galley picked him up. In honor of this miracle, I had it painted in my chapel at Joinville and in the windows at Blécourt.

This means that even if he does not insist on it now—because hearing about a miracle can happen to anyone, after all—when it really happens to you, it is something you feel inside. He is the lord of the village; he has it painted as a fresco in the church, he has it placed in the windows, and so on. This, then, is the world in which they live from a religious point of view, where religion is a great bond that unites everyone and is felt with extreme—with, how can I put it—with great fervor and also great simplicity.

## 3. A disputation ended with a crutch

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00119–U-00157 -->

Sometimes there is even too much simplicity. Joinville recounts an episode with great approval, I must say. He says: once, they recently told me at the monastery of Cluny—you know Cluny in Burgundy, the great Benedictine monastery—at the monastery of Cluny they had organized a great disputation between clerics and Jews. Disputations were something—perhaps I already mentioned it when talking about Friar Salimbene—public intellectual disputations were something that clerics, churchmen of the Middle Ages, liked very much. They liked this opportunity to demonstrate how good they were by facing opponents and arguing with them. Their brothers who had not become priests held tournaments and demonstrated every day that they were the best by defeating an opponent. Churchmen liked doing it too.

In fact, Louis IX told Joinville the story, and Joinville records it as follows: He told me that there was a great disputation between clerics and Jews at the monastery of Cluny. There was a knight there to whom the abbot had given bread in the house for God's sake. He asked the abbot to let him speak first, and permission was granted only reluctantly. Then he rose, leaned on his staff, and asked them to bring him the greatest scholar and foremost master among the Jews, which they did. He asked him this question: 'Master,' said the knight, 'I ask whether you believe that the Virgin Mary, who bore God in her womb and in her arms, gave birth as a virgin and is the mother of God.' The Jew replied that he believed none of it. The knight answered that he had acted like a great fool by entering her monastery and her house when he neither believed in her nor loved her. 'And truly,' said the knight, 'you will pay for it.' Then he raised his crutch, struck the Jew beside the ear, and knocked him to the ground. The Jews fled and carried away their master badly wounded; and that was the end of the disputation.

Joinville then gives Louis’s conclusion: 'And so I tell you,' said the king, 'that no one should dispute with them unless he is a very learned cleric. But when a layman hears the Christian faith spoken ill of, he should defend the Christian faith only with his sword, which he should drive into the offender's belly as far as it can go.'

So this faith, so spontaneous, so impetuous too, so violent, so intense at times—well, you will not be surprised if I tell you that it is also a faith very attached to forms, very formalistic. Because, precisely, a naive faith also means that, in short, I care about doing the right things, about observing the Friday fast, the Lenten fast, and if I do not observe these things, then I truly feel terrible. These are things that have lost importance in Christianity today, whereas at the time Christians felt powerfully obliged to observe all of them.

## 4. Meat on Friday: intention versus observance

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00158–U-00197 -->

Joinville himself happened to experience this personally. We are during the crusade—or rather, toward the end of the crusade, which has already ended very badly. The Turks have won, massacred half the crusader army, and captured all the others. These are scenes that Joinville, incidentally, describes with extraordinary power: these moments of collapse, flight, chaos, people surrendering—and surrendering is a gamble, because you do not know whose hands you will fall into, and it is difficult to predict what the Saracen Turks will do. There are some who, when they see prisoners who are a little battered, sick, and so on, give them a blow on the head and throw them into the sea. And then there are others who treat the prisoners very courteously. In any case, the king and the great lords are always treated very courteously, that goes without saying, partly because they will pay a large ransom to be freed. But in any case Christian or Muslim kings and great lords understand one another; they belong to the same world and have many subjects in common, as we will see in a moment.

So things are already more or less over. Joinville is a prisoner and is having lunch with an emir who has invited him to eat. The emir has invited him to lunch, and they are chatting through an interpreter, naturally. They are happily discussing their respective family trees and mutual acquaintances, because the emir is very interested in Emperor Frederick II. It emerges that Joinville is related to Emperor Frederick II, and there they are, comparing acquaintances and ancestors as they eat.

Joinville’s account is more precise: While we were eating, he had a citizen of Paris brought before us. When the citizen arrived, he said to me, 'My lord, what are you doing?' 'Why, what am I doing?' I said. 'In God's name,' he said, 'you are eating meat on a Friday!' When I heard this, I pushed my bowl away. The admiral asked my Saracen why I had done that, and he told him; the admiral replied that God would not hold it against me, since I had not done it knowingly. And you should know that the legate gave me the same answer once we were out of prison. Yet that did not stop me from fasting afterward on bread and water every Friday in Lent. The legate became very angry with me about this, because I was the only great lord who had remained with the king.

## 5. Dangerous words: devils, oaths, and dismissal

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00198–U-00235 -->

The words people utter, for example. This is a curious world, because everyone constantly has God and the devil on their lips. Everyone, constantly—everyone swears. When they have to say something, they say by God, in God's name, by God's head, by God's wounds. Everyone has his own way of swearing. Joinville often notes it: there are great lords who have a particular way of swearing. There is one who swears by God's cap. Constantly.

And on the other hand, he says, everyone always has the devil on their lips: go to the devil, may the devil take you. And this, Joinville says, is an ugly thing; it is one of the tragedies of our fine kingdom of France. The saintly King Louis never mentioned either God or the devil. I never heard him say these things. We, on the other hand, do. And mentioning God in this way is not actually the most serious thing; what is serious is mentioning the devil. In fact, Joinville says: in my household I established a rule. Anyone who lets a mention of the devil slip from his lips either pays a forfeit or does penance, and among my servants this bad habit has practically disappeared.

Mentioning God, by contrast, is acceptable. To reinforce his statements, a person calls God as a witness. Joinville did it too, but sometimes it gets you into trouble. Joinville tells a story, again during the crusade, like most of the things I am telling you this evening. The crusaders were on the move, moving their camp; every evening they pitched their tents in a new place. One evening, he says, one of my knights came to me, the one who had chosen the site for the tent that evening, and said to me, “Sire”—in Old French, lords were called *sire*, not only kings—“Sire, this evening I have lodged you better than last evening.”

The knight who had chosen the previous evening's tent site hears this, jumps up, grabs him by the hair, and says, “You are very bold to speak about something I do.” Now, this is an enormous discourtesy. In front of their lord, for knights to brawl like this is the height of boorishness, of bad manners. Joinville sees red and says, “Out of my household now; for, so help me God, you will never be with me again.”

The man goes away crying, then returns and begs him to forgive him. He brings friends who say to Joinville, come on, over a thing like this? And Joinville says, well, as far as I am concerned—but I swore. I said, so help me God, you will never enter my household again. Now I cannot simply let you back in as though nothing had happened. If the papal legate releases me from my oath, I will be delighted to take you back.

They go to the papal legate and explain the matter. The papal legate says, “I am sorry. This oath is a valid oath; there were grounds for it. I cannot simply release you from it as though it were nothing. It cannot be done.” And Joinville says: that is how I learned that you have to be careful about what you say, because after this affair he never took his knight back. Another aspect of this religiosity that is very striking, as Joinville expresses it but that was certainly shared by many people—

## 6. A practical bargain with God

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00236–U-00269 -->

—is another aspect of this concreteness. I said earlier that miracles happen; I said earlier that God is continually called as a witness to what happens. There is something more: people constantly expect to be helped by God, the Virgin, and the saints. They constantly expect that while you are going through trouble or difficulty, if you invoke God, God helps you. And when you are in danger and risking your skin in battle, you invoke God and God helps you. This is why we serve God, Joinville says.

Joinville describes and rejects the belief in these terms: Their belief is that no one can die except on his appointed day, and for that reason they will not wear armor; and when they curse their children, they say: 'May you be accursed like the Frank who arms himself for fear of death!' [...] Since returning from overseas, I have seen in this country some disloyal Christians who held to the Bedouins' law and said that no one could die except on his appointed day. Their belief is so faithless that it amounts to saying that God has no power to help us. For those who served God would be fools if we did not believe that he had the power to lengthen our lives and preserve us from harm and misfortune; and we must believe in him, for he has the power to do all things. [...] Another tenet is that they believe no one can die before the day appointed to him. No one should believe this, because God has the power to lengthen and shorten our lives. The Bedouins believe this tenet, and for that reason they will not wear armor when they go into battle, because they would think that they were acting against the commandment of their law.

## 7. Sacrifice, survival, and the lone would-be martyr

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00270–U-00307 -->

Joinville describes his departure: The abbot of Cheminon gave me my pilgrim's scrip and staff; then I departed from Joinville, without re-entering the castle until my return, on foot, barefoot, and dressed in wool. In this way I went to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, and to the other holy relics that are there. While I was going to Blécourt and Saint-Urbain, I never once wished to turn my eyes back toward Joinville, for fear that my heart would soften at the thought of the fine castle I was leaving and of my two children.

And yet sacrifices are made, but common sense is always kept in mind. You do not go on crusade for the pleasure of getting yourself killed. On the contrary, you go on crusade hoping in the end to save your skin and return. These crusaders, these crusading knights, are not fanatical martyrs. And this becomes absolutely clear precisely when the crusaders are routed and the Turks begin capturing them.

Joinville records the company’s decision and the lone objection: It was not long before we saw four of the sultan's galleys approaching, carrying fully a thousand men. I called my knights and my people and asked what they wished us to do: surrender to the sultan's galleys or surrender to those on shore. We all agreed that we preferred to surrender to the sultan's galleys, because they would keep us together, rather than to those on shore, who would scatter us and sell us to the Bedouins. Then one of my cellarers, who came from Doullens, said: 'My lord, I do not agree with this counsel.' I asked what counsel he favored, and he told me: 'I favor letting ourselves all be killed; then we shall all go to paradise.' But we did not believe him.

## 8. A saint keeps his word—and takes a joke

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00308–U-00361 -->

—which is something that rather surprises Joinville, because, after all, keeping your word, certainly—but with those people? And yet it happened; he saw it himself. After their captivity they are paying the ransom. There have been long negotiations with the Saracens who captured them, and in the end they have agreed on a very large sum, 200,000 livres tournois, an enormous sum, to free the king and the great lords. The money has arrived from France, and it has taken days and days to count and hand it over.

Joinville records the exchange and Louis’s response: Then Sir Philippe de Nemours told the king that the Saracens had been undercounted by one balance-load of ten thousand livres. The king became extremely angry and said that he wished the ten thousand livres to be returned to them, because he had promised to pay them two hundred thousand livres before leaving the river. I then trod on Sir Philippe's foot and told the king not to believe him, because what he said was untrue: the Saracens were the most skillful counters in the world. Sir Philippe said that I spoke the truth, because he had said it only as a joke. The king said, 'May misfortune befall such a joke. And I command you, Sir Philippe, by the fealty you owe me as my man, that if the ten thousand livres have not been paid, you are to have them paid without fail.' Many people had advised the king to withdraw to his ship waiting at sea, to remove himself from the Saracens' hands. The king would listen to no one; instead, he said that he would not leave the river, just as he had promised, until he had paid them two hundred thousand livres.

So, as you can see, it was not very easy to live with this king. I began by telling you about a crusading knight with a profound religiosity, but having a profound religiosity is one thing and having to live with a saintly king who pushes things to the extreme is another. Moreover, when Joinville writes as an old man, it is official by then: the king is a saint; he has been canonized. But when these things happened, when they were both young, yes, the king already had this reputation for being a saint, but you understand the difference. Joinville greatly admired him and loved him very much, but working with him was not at all easy. Opportunities for conflict arose constantly.

Joinville tells the episode this way: In the course of our marches we came to the sands of Acre, where the king and the army encamped. There a large company from Greater Armenia came to me on pilgrimage to Jerusalem, paying a heavy tribute to the Saracens who were conducting them. Through an interpreter who knew their language and ours, they asked me to show them the holy king. I went to the king, who was sitting in a pavilion, leaning against its pole; he sat on the sand without a carpet or anything else beneath him. I said to him: 'My lord, outside there is a large company from Greater Armenia going to Jerusalem, and they ask me to show them the holy king; but I have no intention of kissing your bones just yet.' He laughed very openly and told me to go and fetch them, which I did. When they had seen the king, they commended him to God, and the king did the same for them.

## 9. Crusading accounts and a payment in patience

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00362–U-00419 -->

And perhaps I should pause here, because I am talking about crusading knights, and shortly we will talk about martial valor, courtesy, and chivalric ideals. So far we have been talking about religious faith, crusades, martyrdom. Money, it would seem, should have nothing to do with any of this. Wrong. Money has a great deal to do with it. A crusade costs an enormous amount and is a great enterprise that must be planned, and the very first thing is to find the money. Once the money is there, you can begin organizing the army, hiring knights, recruiting people, building ships, and so on. It is extremely expensive. Money enters into everything.

Joinville’s own figures show the financial scale of his commitment: Because I did not wish to carry away any money wrongfully, I went and mortgaged a large amount of my land at Metz in Lorraine. You should know that on the day I left our country for the Holy Land I did not hold land worth a thousand livres a year, because my lady mother was still alive; and I went as one of ten knights and one of three bannerets. I remind you of these things because, if God had not helped me—he who never failed me—I could scarcely have endured for so long as the six years I remained in the Holy Land. [...] I, who did not have land worth a thousand livres a year, took responsibility when I went overseas for a company of ten knights including myself, with two other knights bearing banners. When I arrived in Cyprus, after paying for my ship, I had only two hundred and forty livres tournois left. Some of my knights sent word that unless I found money, they would leave me. God, who never failed me, then provided for me in this way: the king, who was at Nicosia, sent for me, retained me in his service, and put eight hundred livres in my coffers; and then I had more money than I needed.

When the earlier arrangement expired, Joinville and Louis made a new bargain: While the king was fortifying Caesarea, I went to his quarters to see him. As soon as he saw me enter his chamber, where he was speaking with the legate, he rose, drew me aside, and said: 'You know,' said the king, 'that I retained you only until Easter; so I ask you to tell me what I should give you to remain with me from Easter for one year.' I told him that I did not wish him to give me any more of his money than he had given me before, but that I wished to make a different bargain with him. 'Because,' I said, 'you become angry whenever someone asks you for something, I want you to agree that if I ask you for anything during this whole year, you will not become angry; and if you refuse me, I will not become angry either.' When he heard this, he began to laugh very openly and told me that he retained me on that condition. He took me by the hand, led me to the legate and his council, and recounted to them the bargain we had made. They were very pleased, because I was the richest man in the army.

## 10. The saint whom other people cannot imitate

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00420–U-00458 -->

Saint Louis constantly demonstrates his holiness; people are astonished. At Sidon in 1253, on the Levantine coast, Louis helps carry decomposing Christian bodies into burial trenches. [N-008] In a separate episode in 1250, he refuses to escape alone by galley because doing so would leave hundreds of people stranded in Cyprus.

When urged to leave others behind and save himself, Louis answers: Then the king said: 'Lords, I have heard your advice and the advice of my people; now I will tell you mine. If I leave the ship, there are five hundred people or more aboard who, fearing danger to their lives—for there is not one among them who does not love his life as much as I love mine—will remain on the island of Cyprus, and who may perhaps never return to their own countries. Therefore I would rather place my own person, my wife, and my children in God's hands than inflict such harm on so great a number of people as are aboard.'

Joinville records both his blunt answer and Louis’s later rebuke: 'Now I ask you,' he said, 'which would you rather: that you were a leper, or that you had committed a mortal sin?' And I, who never lied to him, answered that I would rather have committed thirty than be a leper. When the friars had left, he called me aside alone, made me sit at his feet, and said: 'Why did you say that to me yesterday?' I told him that I still said the same. He said: 'You spoke like a rash fool, for you should know that there is no leprosy so hideous as being in mortal sin, because the soul that is in mortal sin resembles the Devil; therefore no leprosy can be so hideous. It is true that when a man dies he is cured of the leprosy of the body. But when a man who has committed a mortal sin dies, he does not know and cannot be certain that in his lifetime he repented in such a way that God pardoned him; therefore he should greatly fear that this leprosy will last as long as God is in paradise. So I beg you as earnestly as I can, for love of God and of me, to set your heart on preferring every bodily misfortune—leprosy and every other illness—to mortal sin entering your soul.'

On washing the feet of the poor, Joinville records this exchange: He asked me whether I washed the feet of the poor on Maundy Thursday. 'My lord,' I said, 'God forbid! I will never wash the feet of those common people.' 'Truly,' he said, 'that was badly said, for you must not disdain what God did for our instruction. So I ask you, first for the love of God and then for love of me, to accustom yourself to washing them.'

## 11. Noble blood against Robert de Sorbon

<!-- chapter: CH-011; transcript: U-00459–U-00506 -->

—of the life and mentality of these knights: this aristocratic mentality according to which society is divided, and there are gentlemen, nobles, knights, lords, and they are the best people there are. Then there are the villains. Everyone else is a villain, everyone who works—not only peasants digging in the fields but also merchants and financiers, people who may have made money, perhaps a great deal of money, perhaps more than the knights, as is beginning to happen. It is an ignoble thing, because villains, everyone knows, are ignoble people. The only respectable people are nobles, gentlemen, knights. This is rooted extremely deeply, almost automatically, in the minds of people like Joinville.

There are countless episodes of this kind. Robert de Sorbon rises from a non-noble background to become a master of theology and royal cleric. In 1253 he founds a theological college, royally confirmed in 1257, that becomes known as the Sorbonne; he does not found the University of Paris. [N-009] Joinville is at the king's court when Master Robert sees him arrive, takes him by the hem of his garment, and pulls him before the king. Joinville is rather surprised: what the devil is happening—“the devil,” indeed, because sometimes it slips out.

Joinville records the confrontation with Robert de Sorbon: Master Robert de Sorbon came to fetch me there, took me by the hem of my cloak, and led me to the king; all the other knights followed us. I asked Master Robert, 'Master Robert, what do you want with me?' He said: 'I want to ask you this: if the king were sitting in this meadow and you went and sat on his bench higher than he, should you rightly be blamed for it?' I said yes. He said: 'Then you are indeed to blame when you are more nobly dressed than the king, for you dress in vair fur and green cloth, which the king does not.' I said: 'Master Robert, saving your grace, I am not to blame if I dress in green and vair, for my father and mother left me this mode of dress. But you are to blame, for you are the son of a villein man and a villein woman, and you have abandoned the dress of your father and mother and are dressed in richer camelin cloth than the king.' Then I took the hem of his surcoat and the hem of the king's and said to him: 'Now see whether I am telling the truth.' The king then began to defend Master Robert verbally with all his might.

Joinville says Louis later reconsidered his intervention: Then he told me that he had called us so that he could confess to me that he had wrongly defended Master Robert against me. 'But,' he said, 'I saw him so bewildered that he badly needed my help. Still, do not attach importance to anything I said in Master Robert's defense, for, as the seneschal says, you ought to dress well and neatly, so that your wives will love you the more and your people will esteem you more. For, as the wise man says, one should adorn oneself in clothing and arms in such a way that the worthy men of this world do not say one does too much, nor the young people of this world say one does too little.'

A mentality that will last for centuries, incidentally. It will take not merely the French Revolution but well into the nineteenth century before this idea—that they are an intrinsically superior class and everyone else is worth little—begins to fade among the European nobility. And yet—

## 12. Putting rich commoners in their place

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00507–U-00549 -->

—Joinville knows, as I said before, that some of these newly rich villains are beginning to make so much money that they become important people, buy castles, and occupy important positions at court. This is difficult to accept. Nothing is more entertaining than seeing these villains who have risen in the world put back in their place.

Joinville’s actual anecdote names Artaud de Nogent and runs as follows: Artaud de Nogent was the burgess in all the world whom the count trusted most, and he was so rich that he built the castle of Nogent-l'Artaud with his own money. [...] At the foot of the steps a poor knight came before him, knelt, and said: 'My lord, I beg you for God's sake to give me something of yours with which I may marry my two daughters, whom you see here.' Artaud, who was walking behind him, said to the poor knight: 'Sir knight, you are behaving discourteously by asking my lord, for he has given so much that he has nothing left to give.' The generous count turned toward Artaud and said: 'Sir villein, what you say—that I have nothing left to give—is not true. I still have you yourself. Here, sir knight: I give him to you, and I will guarantee him to you.' The knight was not taken aback; instead he seized Artaud by the cloak and said he would not release him until Artaud had settled with him. Before Artaud escaped him, he had settled with the knight for five hundred livres.

Joinville describes the judgment imposed on the royal sergeant Le Goulu: The third judgment that I saw given at Caesarea was this. One of the king's sergeants, named Le Goulu, laid hands on a knight of my company. I went to complain to the king. The king told me that, in his view, I could well let the matter pass, since the sergeant had only shoved him. I said I would not let it pass, and that if the king did not give me justice I would leave his service, since his sergeants were shoving knights. He had justice done for me, and the judgment, according to the customs of the country, was this: the sergeant came to my quarters barefoot, wearing only his shirt and breeches, with a bare sword in his hand. He knelt before the knight, took the sword by its point, offered the pommel to the knight, and said: 'My lord, I make amends to you for laying hands on you, and I have brought you this sword so that you may cut off my fist, if it pleases you.' I asked the knight to forgive his resentment against the sergeant, and he did so.

## 13. Why knights believe they deserve privilege

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00550–U-00588 -->

—in their eyes, I mean, because perhaps it cannot be justified, but in their eyes how is this idea justified, that nobles are the salt of the earth, that knights are the best there are and everyone else counts for nothing? Fundamentally, the issue is that nobles see themselves as—and largely are—the people who wage war, the people who risk their skin. Others work and make money, but a knight's profession is risking his life: for his lord, his companions in arms, loyalty, the cause, the cross when he goes on crusade.

A roughly contemporary didactic poem gives the maxim in a different form: Whoever goes on without learning to ride and remains at school until the age of twelve is thereafter poorly suited to knighthood: he lacks the strength for it. The unridden schoolboy is fit for nothing but the priesthood.

Being at war means remaining there for hours and hours in heat, cold, and frost, wearing armor, with nothing to drink or eat. Nobles tell themselves these things. In truth, if you look at armies, there are also infantrymen, domestics, servants, people on foot, who are often killed first when things go badly. But nobles do not even see them; they see themselves on their horses, wearing their helmets.

Joinville describes Louis’s landing and appearance in battle: When the king heard that the standard of Saint-Denis was ashore, he strode rapidly through his vessel and would not stop for the legate who was with him. He leapt into the sea, where the water came up to his armpits, and went with his shield at his neck, his helmet on his head, and his lance in his hand until he reached his men on the seashore. When he came ashore and saw the Saracens, he asked what people they were. He was told they were Saracens; he lowered the lance beneath his armpit, put his shield before him, and would have charged the Saracens if the worthy men with him had allowed it. [...] Never did I see so fine an armed man, for he stood out above all his people from the shoulders upward, with a gilded helmet on his head and a German sword in his hand.

Naturally, there are other things that are also supposed to justify their privileges. They should be the best mannered, most courteous and refined, the people who know how to behave. In part it is true: they attach importance to these things. Then the scenes Joinville recounts show us that reality was often different.

## 14. Courtesy, women, and the mother whom Louis cannot escape

<!-- chapter: CH-014; transcript: U-00589–U-00649 -->

Joinville recounts the scene and its aftermath: On the eve of Shrove Tuesday I saw a marvel that I wish to recount to you, for that same day Sir Hugh of Landricourt, who served under my banner, was buried. While he lay on his bier in my chapel, six of my knights were leaning on several sacks full of barley. Because they were talking loudly in my chapel and making noise that disturbed the priest, I went to tell them to be quiet, and told them it was a disgraceful thing for knights and gentlemen to talk while Mass was being sung. They began to laugh at me and told me, laughing, that they were arranging the remarriage of his wife. I reproached them and told them that such words were neither good nor proper, and that they had quickly forgotten their companion. God took such vengeance on them that the next day was the great battle of Shrove Tuesday, in which they were killed or mortally wounded; consequently all six of their wives had to remarry.

Joinville explicitly criticizes Louis’s distance from his family: The queen, who had recently recovered from giving birth to Lady Blanche at Jaffa, arrived at Sidon by sea. When I heard she had arrived, I rose from before the king, went to meet her, and brought her to the castle. When I returned to the king, who was in his chapel, he asked whether the queen and the children were well, and I said they were. He said to me: “I know well that when you rose from before me, you went to meet the queen; that is why I had the sermon held for you.” I remind you of these things because I had already been around him for five years, yet I had never heard him speak of the queen or his children, either to me or to anyone else; and it was not good conduct, as it seems to me, to be aloof from one's wife and children.

If we wanted—well, we still have five minutes. There were reasons for this in King Louis, and he is another figure who would yield interesting results if one applied a little psychoanalysis. The king lost his father very young and remained for years under his mother's guardianship. Queen Blanche of Castile was a formidable woman of power who governed the kingdom for years while he was a child. When he grew up he took control of the kingdom himself, but Blanche of Castile, his mother, was always present and controlled everything. The king had to marry, because a king must marry and produce heirs. But Blanche did not get along with her daughter-in-law and did everything she could to keep her away from her husband.

Joinville describes the arrangement at Pontoise: Queen Blanche's harsh treatment of Queen Margaret went so far that, as much as she could, Blanche would not allow her son to be in his wife's company except in the evening, when he went to bed with her. The residence where the king and queen most liked to stay was Pontoise, because the king's chamber was above and the queen's chamber below. They arranged matters so that they held their conversations on a spiral staircase leading from one chamber to the other. They had also arranged that when the ushers saw Queen Blanche coming to the chamber of the king, her son, they struck the doors with their staffs, and the king came running back to his chamber so that his mother would find him there. The ushers of Queen Margaret's chamber did the same when Queen Blanche came there, so that she would find Queen Margaret in her chamber.

Joinville records the reactions to Blanche of Castile’s death: At Sidon news came to the king that his mother had died. He grieved so deeply that for two days no one was able to speak to him. After that, he sent one of his chamber servants to fetch me. When I came before him in his chamber, where he was all alone, he saw me, stretched out his arms, and said: “Ah, Seneschal, I have lost my mother!” [...] My lady Marie of Vertus, a very good and holy woman, came to tell me that the queen was grieving deeply, and asked me to go to comfort her. When I arrived, I found her weeping, and I told her that whoever says one should not believe a woman speaks truly: “For the woman you hated most is dead, and yet you are grieving like this!” She told me that she was not weeping for Blanche, but because of the distress the king was suffering in his grief, and for her daughter—who later became queen of Navarre—who had been left in the custody of men.

## 15. Adults at play and chivalry recapitulated

<!-- chapter: CH-015; transcript: U-00650–U-00675 -->

Perhaps you know, perhaps you do not, that adults in the Middle Ages play a great deal. Some are the same games we play: chess, draughts, backgammon. They always play for money, it must be said—even chess—because it is more fun. They gamble a great deal, at dice, although naturally the king does not tolerate it, and so—and then they play games that adults today no longer play. Knights and ladies, if they are together and become bored, can play blind man's buff or hide-and-seek. No one is surprised, because people enjoy themselves; adults are not ashamed to have fun like children.

Joinville’s version includes both the small ballista and a second prank: I will tell you about the games the count of Eu used to play on us. I had built a room where my knights and I ate by the light from the doorway. The doorway faced the count of Eu's quarters; and he, being very ingenious, made a small ballista that shot into the room. He had someone watch for when we were seated at our meal, then set his ballista lengthwise toward our table and had it fired, breaking our pots and glasses. I had stocked up on hens and capons, and I do not know who had given him a young bear, which he let loose among my hens; it had killed a dozen before anyone could get there, while the woman who looked after them beat the bear with her distaff.

## 16. Honor destroys the battle plan

<!-- chapter: CH-016; transcript: U-00676–U-00698 -->

The crusade ended so badly in part because, during the great attack planned against the Saracens, the group of Templar knights was supposed to hold the vanguard. By rule in the Holy Land, the Templars have the vanguard; they are the first to attack, which is the place of honor. But the king's brother, Robert of Artois, the count of Artois, who had around him some advisers who were, how can I put it, rather overbearing, at a certain point begins to accelerate and overtakes the Templars with his group of knights, breaking the predetermined order.

The Templars see the count of Artois overtaking them and begin spurring their horses too, because heaven forbid they should fall behind; that cannot happen. Everyone behind them accelerates as well, and the entire attack, planned as a succession of interventions by various groups of knights, degenerates into a wild race because no one wants the shame of being the one left behind. It ends very badly. The Turks surround them, the count of Artois is killed, almost all the Templars are killed, and the crusade is lost.

So knightly arrogance sometimes causes serious trouble. But they are not all so arrogant. In their culture there is an eternal conflict between, how can I put it, the cult of honor, showing off, the grand gesture, and the professionalism of the soldier who knows how things work and knows that war must be conducted properly. The cult of honor can also help someone fight a war well: it keeps you going and gives you courage at dangerous moments.

## 17. “We shall speak of this day in the ladies' chambers”

<!-- chapter: CH-017; transcript: U-00699–U-00726 -->

During the battle of Mansurah on February 8, 1250, Joinville and the count of Soissons help hold a small bridge so attackers cannot strike Louis's forces from front and rear. Other knights and royal sergeants are nearby, and royal crossbowmen eventually drive the attackers off; this is not the army's later April retreat. [N-015]

At the bridge, Joinville attributes this remark to the count of Soissons: The good count of Soissons, there where we stood, joked with me and said: “Seneschal, let this pack of curs howl; for by God's coif!—that was how he swore—you and I will yet speak of this day in the ladies' chambers.”

This is one of those cases in which chivalric culture—honor and pride, but also courtly love, boasting, but boasting after winning, not after losing—comes together. We will win, we will return alive, we will remember this day, and in the ladies' chambers we will make ourselves look splendid by recounting our adventures. And Joinville lived so long that he did indeed manage to tell his adventures, and I have tried, in a small way, to tell them to you too. Thank you, thank you.
