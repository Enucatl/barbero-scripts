# How a Medieval Man Thought: The Merchant

## 1. Dino Compagni: A Merchant, a Politician, and a Defeated Witness

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00076 -->

Let's talk about a medieval merchant and try to understand how he thinks, how he sees the world.

<!-- research: C-001 -->

Our merchant is Dino Compagni, a Florentine who lives in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, roughly the same time as Dante. And he leaves us a little book.

If you heard the previous episode, you'll remember that Fra Salimbene wrote a chronicle nine hundred pages long. Dino's book is much more manageable. He wants to describe some important things that happen in Florence while he's living there—and while he's someone who matters there.

Which makes me think that perhaps I should have promised to explain how a medieval politician sees the world. Because the Dino who tells his story in this chronicle is, above all, a political man. He tells us what it means to be involved in politics in Florence, one of Italy's self-governing cities. The rival parties are called Guelfs and Ghibellines: broadly speaking, the Guelf party backs the pope, the Ghibelline party the Holy Roman emperor. Later, the Guelf party itself splits into two rival parties, the White and the Black. And politics in that world is no joke.

<!-- research: C-002 -->

Of course Dino is a merchant. He's a businessman, a member of the silk guild. This Florentine guild deals in large-scale commerce, importing and exporting cloth. Dino has an important firm of his own. He's wealthy. But for a period of his life, he also goes into politics. He finds himself in the city government.

<!-- research: C-003 Q-001 -->

Then things go wrong. His side loses, and he's pushed out of politics. He goes on living in Florence and running his business, but something keeps eating away at him. For a while he held power. He tried, he tells us, to stop things from going as badly as they're going now. And now that he sees just how badly they're going, he looks back on those years when he occupied one of those seats, when he had some chance to make a difference.

<!-- research: Q-002 -->

At least, he says, he feels the urge to write. To describe what he's seen, if nothing else. And what he's seen is not edifying at all.

“I wanted to write. For a long time I resisted, because I told myself I wasn't capable of it.”

<!-- research: C-004 -->

He's a merchant; he doesn't know Latin. He's unlike Fra Salimbene, whom we met in the previous episode. Salimbene knows Latin perfectly, thinks in Latin, can write in Latin. Dino writes in Italian. He can read and write just as he can keep accounts: a merchant, a businessman, has to know how to keep his books. But he doesn't know Latin—or knows very little. He writes in Italian, in Tuscan.

He thought he wasn't capable. And above all, he says, he put it off for a long time, “believing that others would write.”

That strikes me as significant. Think of the world he's living in. There are no newspapers, no television. Nothing of the kind. Great events happen. Politics produces conflicts of extraordinary violence. Power changes hands; governments change. All these momentous things happen—and what will anyone know about them in the future?

They'll know something if somebody writes it down. If nobody does, the memory of it all will disappear. Dino says that for a long time he leaves it alone, believing someone else will write. Then he feels the urge to write himself, to give his account. And that is what he does.

<!-- research: C-005 -->

But remember whose voice we're hearing. This is a man who has been politically influential, who then loses and gets pushed out. We have to allow for that when we read his judgments.

Our purpose isn't to decide whether the Guelf party was better than the Ghibelline party, or the White party better than the Black party. The challenge is to understand how Dino Compagni, member of the silk guild in Dante's time, sees the world.

So, where do we begin?

## 2. The Prestige of Knights

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00077–U-00168 -->

Let's begin with how a merchant like Dino, a businessman, ends up governing his city. It isn't as obvious as it might seem.

I don't want to keep referring to the previous episode; some of you may not have heard it. But we were talking about a Franciscan friar who came from a noble family, a great family of knights in Parma.

<!-- research: C-006 -->

The Italian communes have a nobility of great families. These are people with ancestors, a coat of arms, land, castles, towers in the city. They have horses and weapons, and they're used to fighting and competing in tournaments.

For a long time these gentlemen, as people call them, are the men who govern the cities. They rule. Merchants stay in their shops and warehouses; craftsmen get on with their work. In the communes, the nobles—the knights—are in charge.

Admiration for knights, for people who belong to a higher world socially, is still part of Dino's outlook. He's a merchant, not one of them. As we'll see, he doesn't really share their interests. In politics he has some very bitter confrontations with them.

Yet to a man of his generation, a gentleman, a knight, still commands a certain instinctive respect. You take your hat off to him.

<!-- research: Q-003 C-007 -->

Consider some of the customs Dino describes in Florence. A woman is being buried in the Frescobaldi square. A crowd has gathered, and according to the local custom, the citizens sit on mats on the ground.

Medieval people have few pieces of furniture. They sit on the ground with an ease and a lack of self-consciousness that we no longer have. So all the citizens at the funeral are sitting on mats—except the knights and the doctors of law. They're on benches, higher up.

The doctors are university graduates. Now, I won't dwell on how much greater the prestige of university professors used to be... Well. Greater than... Anyway. The knights and the law graduates, the judges: they get the benches. Everyone else sits below them.

<!-- research: Q-004 C-008 -->

And it isn't just a matter of honor. Knights are the citizens who count most in every respect. Under one peace settlement, some citizens still have to remain in exile. The commune pays them a daily allowance—but more to the knights, Dino tells us, and less to everyone else.

Being a knight means belonging to the elite.

<!-- research: C-009 -->

And knights are necessary. You need to send an embassy to the pope. Who are you going to send—a cobbler? A cobbler may be able to serve in Florence's government, as we'll see. But for an embassy to the pope, you need knights. People who know how to behave in society, who are accustomed to dealing with the powerful, who have good manners.

You also need knights in war. When the commune goes to war, it calls everyone out to fight: craftsmen, merchants, all the citizens, organized into neighborhood companies. They march on foot with their spears and shields. But the nobles know how to ride. They have armor. They spend their lives fighting in tournaments. Without those mounted noblemen, you don't have an army.

They're the men who win battles, not the mass of ordinary townspeople in their infantry companies. The nobles are essential to communal life, and for a long time they've been in charge.

Now, I realize I'm supposed to be telling you how Dino Compagni sees the world, and instead I'm telling you about everyone else. The people unlike him. The people he looks up to, the people above him. But that is the world a merchant inhabits: an Italian city with lords and noblemen over his head.

<!-- research: C-010 -->

It doesn't matter if the commune is democratic and everyone votes. The nobles count.

They share a system of values, the chivalric ideal. You have to be prepared to sacrifice yourself, to be killed in battle if necessary. Honor is at the center of everything.

<!-- research: Q-005 C-011 -->

Early in his chronicle Dino describes a great battle: the Florentines' victory over Arezzo at Campaldino in 1289. Dante fights there too. He's a nobleman, serving among Florence's cavalry.

Dino tells us it's a desperately hard battle. Some men let themselves be killed rather than give ground. Others, men everyone expects wonders from, run away. But many fight very well.

And that morning, before the battle, new knights have been made. A number of young noblemen undergo the ceremony that turns an ordinary man into a knight: the sword laid on the shoulder.

Imagine a young man knighted that morning, with a battle to fight in the afternoon. He'll get himself killed before he'll disgrace himself.

These values are central to the society. And for a long time merchants like Dino stay in their shops, their warehouses, their businesses. They exchange money and goods, buy textiles, import and export. They know their work brings in plenty of money.

But compared with the life of a knight—riding around, owning estates, not working—a merchant is still something of a poor relation in the eyes of that generation.

For a long time, then, noble knights rule Florence, as they do the other Italian cities. And merchants like Dino obey, work, make money, and pay their taxes.

## 3. Swords, Families, and Kinship

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00169–U-00242 -->

But the nobles are also a burden. Living in a city run by these people is wearing. They go around armed, with a sword at their side at all times, ready to draw it whenever something displeases them.

They think about war. They know how to fight. And they have the unpleasant habit of pulling a sword in situations where two merchants might start talking and try to reach an agreement. Not the nobles.

<!-- research: Q-006 C-012 -->

They occupy the important positions, not only in city government but in the Church. The bishops are all nobles. And sometimes they're like that bishop of Arezzo whom Dino describes, Guglielmino degli Ubertini. He “knew the duties of war better than those of the Church.”

He will, in fact, get himself killed in battle, leading Arezzo's cavalry at Campaldino. These are the nobles we're dealing with.

<!-- research: Q-007 C-013 C-014 -->

They're organized into families. When Dino describes society, at the top he sees what he calls the families. If that suggests something a little like the Mafia, you're not far off.

The nobles are the families because they have so many relatives, and an important surname. Often only nobles have a surname. Dino has one because he's already a wealthy townsman. But most Florentines don't even have that. They're called Dino, son of Giovanni—or Dino, son of Andrea, or of Bartolomeo.

The nobles, by contrast, are the Cavalcanti, the Brunelleschi, the Tosinghi. The families.

And a family can be large. Dino describes a quarrel between several families and the Cavalcanti. The Cavalcanti are powerful. Their family alone has sixty men capable of bearing arms.

Think what that means when politics takes you into the square. “We have to go out there. And just from our own family, there are sixty of us riding into the square in armor.” That carries some weight.

These families are ready to kill to defend their power, their honor, their interests. They are associations of kin bound together by power and common interests. It isn't that they all love one another. Interests hold them together.

<!-- research: Q-008 C-015 -->

Dino gives us an extraordinary example from Arezzo. Remember the bishop who knows more about war than the Church? At one point a political problem arises. The bishop betrays his side, betrays his family. He tries to make a deal with the Florentines while his friends and relatives want war against Florence.

People in Arezzo are rather annoyed that the bishop is negotiating with the enemy. So, Dino tells us, as though it were perfectly ordinary, they decide to have him killed.

There is Arezzo's government, sitting in council: “The bishop is becoming a nuisance. Let's have him murdered.”

<!-- research: Q-009 -->

But one of the councillors is a relative of the bishop, Messer Guglielmo de' Pazzi. Dino calls him his consorto: a member of the same kinship group.

When Guglielmo hears them discussing the bishop's murder, he says he would have been very pleased if they'd done it without his knowing. But now that they've asked him, he cannot agree.

In other words: “If you'd done it without telling me, that would have suited me perfectly. I agree, this bishop really has become a nuisance. But he's my relative. You can't ask me to vote in the city council to have him killed. I'm sorry. I can't vote for that.”

That's how nobles do politics. They always have a sword at their side, and they're ready to draw it.

<!-- research: C-009 C-016 -->

People like Dino don't have a sword at their side. Merchants work and make money. They don't carry swords, they don't ride around on horseback, and they don't have anything like as many relatives as the nobles.

Throughout his chronicle, Dino never mentions a relative of his own. For the nobles, family is everything. Relatives appear constantly; everything revolves around kinship. Dino has no relatives. He's made his own way. He's a self-made man, like so many of these Florentine townspeople outside the nobility.

## 4. Factions and Followers

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00243–U-00286 -->

<!-- research: C-017 -->

The nobles have run Florence for a long time, but they haven't run it peacefully. They tear one another apart. Families form alliances to pursue their interests.

These alliances become the Guelf and Ghibelline parties: those who would like the pope to rule the world, and those who would prefer the emperor.

But in practice, you join the Guelf party because the rival noble family belongs to the Ghibelline party. “They did us an injury years ago. We can't...”

“What injury?”

“Well, we don't really remember anymore. But everyone knows we're enemies. If they're in the Ghibelline party, we're in the Guelf party.”

<!-- research: Q-010 -->

Dino couldn't care less about all this. He's a businessman thinking about making money, importing cloth from France. But he knows that his city has long been dominated by these rival groupings of great noble families: “the accursed factions.”

For people like Dino, that division is where all the trouble begins. If only we were united... We'll come back to that. Instead, they're divided.

The networks around the great families are powerful and deeply rooted. It isn't just the nobles themselves. They have followers. A noble family owns houses and shops all over the city and rents them out. If you're a tenant of the Uberti, you stand with the Uberti. If you're a businessman who works for them, you stand with them too.

<!-- research: C-018 Q-011 -->

The Uberti are the great family of the Ghibelline party, expelled from Florence when the Guelf party wins. They will never return for good. You may remember Farinata degli Uberti, the proud leader of the Ghibelline party Dante meets in the Inferno.

Dino describes one brief truce when something almost unheard-of happens. The sides reach an agreement and let the exiled members of the Ghibelline party back into Florence. It won't last. A few months later they quarrel and throw them out again.

But for that moment the exiles are back. For the first time, the Uberti are seen riding into Florence with their shields and their coat of arms. And Dino describes people running to kiss that coat of arms: old supporters of the Ghibelline party, men and women.

The Uberti have been exiled from the city for fifty years.

That is how deeply these loyalties run. This is a partisan world, ferociously partisan. And unlike our political partisanship today, theirs has an additional outlet. When you really can't stand the other side anymore, you kill them.

## 5. Government of the People

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00287–U-00322 -->

<!-- research: C-019 -->

Eventually people like Dino have had enough. They want to work and make money in a peaceful city. They come to see the nobles as the city's curse. Without them, how well we could live! Everyone working, no Guelf party, no Ghibelline party.

And so they establish what they call a government of the people.

But “the people” is a large expression. In principle it means everyone except the nobility. In Florence, it actually means the respectable people who pay taxes: above all, the business owners, people with a shop or an enterprise.

Imagine the U.S. Chamber of Commerce running the government. Business owners themselves hold power. That's essentially what Florence establishes.

<!-- research: C-020 -->

At the head of the commune they put a board of six priors, selected by the guilds. The guilds are, essentially, their organizations of employers. There are many: the furriers, the silk and wool businessmen, the notaries, the doctors. Government is in their hands.

Now a man like Dino can find himself governing. While the nobles were in charge, men like him stayed at their shops. But in the late thirteenth century Florence tries this experiment in broader democracy.

Broader up to a point, of course. Dino's employees don't get to govern. Dino himself, though, the businessman who takes off his hat to the noble Buondelmonti, now finds himself among those who hold power. He often serves on one governing body or another.

<!-- research: C-021 -->

Keep in mind how many bodies a communal government contains: commissions, subcommissions, councils, boards. Six men, twelve, eighteen, four. Their terms are very short. The membership changes constantly. At most they serve a year, and often much less.

The six priors, the actual city government, the men who make the decisive decisions, if I can put it that way, change every two months.

Imagine the prime minister and the entire cabinet changing every two months.

To them, this seems the only way to stop certain people from acquiring too much power and using it too freely for their own benefit. So the committees rotate. A great many citizens are called upon to govern.

The nobles aren't particularly pleased.

## 6. War and the Ordinances of Justice

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00323–U-00416 -->

<!-- research: Q-012 C-024 -->

The great drama of Florence as Dino sees it is this: we've established a government of the people. We would govern well, in the common interest. But the nobles, with their friends, their parties and factions, think only of their own interests. Nobody thinks about the public good.

Conflicts of interest are constant.

<!-- research: Q-013 C-022 -->

Take the conflict with Arezzo. Dino and many other decent townspeople think: why go to war? Let's talk. Let's reach an agreement. Let's not fight.

But the nobles want war because it suits them. They know how to do it. War means splendid military pay and plunder. It means they will matter. And if the war is won, who wins it? “We do. The nobles. So the merchants had better remember their place.”

In the end the war happens. Dino is in government at the time; he votes against it. But they go to war anyway.

<!-- research: Q-014 C-023 -->

And here the merchant really does come out. Ah, war, Dino says: “More is consumed in one day of war than is earned in many years of peace.”

He knows that very well. This is one of the few times he talks about earnings in his book. Making money is his everyday life, but the book is about politics. Still, money matters in politics too. When the question is whether to go to war, the merchant thinks about how much public money war consumes. How much could be saved by making peace.

But the nobles want war, and war is what they get.

<!-- research: Q-015 C-009 -->

They want it partly because, in wartime, they're the only ones who count. Something else is obvious to Dino and men like him: they dislike war because they don't know how to fight it, don't want to fight it, and aren't interested in it.

In peacetime a merchant may be richer than a nobleman, or better at administration. In war the merchant stays home and the nobleman commands. The merchants don't interfere in military affairs.

So the war is fought, and the nobles become more overbearing than ever.

<!-- research: Q-016 C-024 -->

Their bullying has one defining feature: they don't obey the law. This disgusts Dino. We have good laws in Florence. If only people observed them, the city would prosper. But they don't. Why?

Part of the blame—and I have to say it, because there's blame for everyone—lies with the judges. “The accursed judges,” Dino calls them. They interpret the law in favor of their friends and their faction.

But even if that were the only problem! The nobles simply refuse to obey. They're armed. Just try arresting them.

The solution, then, is a tougher government of the people. One that puts them in their place and makes them obey.

<!-- research: Q-017 C-025 -->

It has been tried in Arezzo. I keep mentioning Arezzo because Dino does: it's an important Tuscan city at this time. There, he says, the nobles become intolerable. A government of the people is established, and a man is brought in from outside to lead it. He puts the nobles in their place. He forces them to obey the laws.

Which is already something extraordinary.

In Arezzo, it ends very badly. The nobles can bear no more. They take up arms and seize power, breaking the popular government, as Dino puts it. They shut its leader in a cistern and leave him there to starve to death. That is the end of the government of the people in Arezzo.

<!-- research: Q-018 C-026 -->

But the Florentine townspeople are tougher. They want to try. They enact the Ordinances of Justice.

What are these ordinances? Put simply, an extraordinary law.

First, nobles cannot hold government office. In all the countless commissions, subcommissions, and councils that govern Florence, a nobleman cannot serve.

Second, if a nobleman attacks an ordinary townsman, if he draws his sword, he must be punished. And not just him: his relatives too. They are responsible for one another.

There must also be an armed force. Find the money in the budget; establish a body of police serving the people. When a nobleman is accused of violence, they go in, arrest everyone, tear down the... raze their houses to the ground. That's how it's done. Then the nobles will know their place.

And they establish this system.

<!-- research: Q-019 C-027 -->

From that moment, Florence's six priors have to barricade themselves inside the palace of the Badia. The city hall hasn't been built yet. The city budget includes a special police guard to protect them, because a prior's life isn't worth a penny now. If he goes out into the street and meets a nobleman...

Yet even in this atmosphere of extreme tension, they manage to pass the laws excluding nobles from office.

<!-- research: Q-020 C-028 -->

But who exactly are the nobles? It's easy enough to say, Dino points out, but do we have a list? Let's make one.

They do. Any family that has had a knight among its members is to be considered noble: a family of magnates.

“If you've had a knight, you're not one of us. We're the peaceful people who want to work and make money, who don't go around armed. Families with knights belong on the other side. We don't want them governing Florence.”

Naturally the nobles are furious. You can understand why. They've won the war against Arezzo, and their reward is to be thrown out of government.

<!-- research: Q-021 Q-022 -->

Dino knows the sort of things they say when they get together. “Those dogs of the people have driven us out of the offices and honors of our own city.”

“We're the men who won the war, and now they've thrown us out of office.”

The nobles are ready to do anything to regain power.

And it is in this situation that Dino begins to become an important politician. You can see that he isn't taking on an easy job.

## 7. Politics by Assembly

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00417–U-00476 -->

<!-- research: C-029 -->

Dino is a man of the merchants and the guilds, frequently appointed as one of the priors. How does the politics he's called upon to manage actually work? What happens in practice?

It's all assemblies. Everything is done collectively, because citizens feel that politics belongs to them.

Decisions are made in innumerable councils, and anyone can be appointed. Some councils are even chosen by lot. They put all the citizens' names into a large sack and draw them out. Anyone can come to power. It's a very advanced experiment in democracy—and we're in the late thirteenth century.

<!-- research: C-010 -->

Everything gets discussed. Whatever the occasion, what do you do? Call an assembly. Everybody speaks. And the people who speak loudest win.

That is how everything is decided, including things we'd think absurd to decide that way.

<!-- research: Q-023 C-030 C-031 -->

Before the war against Arezzo, a crucial strategic decision has to be made. We're stronger, so we'll attack. But which route do we take? Along the river valley, or over the mountains and down from the north?

A great deal depends on this choice. Today a commanding general would decide. They call a large meeting in the city's baptistery—the building where Florentines are baptized. It's their usual place for such gatherings. The city hall hasn't been built yet.

So there they all are in the baptistery: the important citizens, the officeholders, the experienced old knights, the military commanders. They spend the whole day discussing, in public, whether to attack Arezzo from the north after crossing the mountains, or take the river-valley route.

Once everyone has spoken and given an opinion, how do they decide?

They vote.

With white and black beans, as they do in all the councils. Each man has a white bean and a black bean. They put their votes into the urn and see which side wins.

The decision, Dino says, is to cross the mountains and approach from the north. And it is the worse route. But fortunately they're protected; it turns out well all the same.

So this is government by assembly, with very broad participation.

As I said in the previous episode, it is also a simple world. It has practical limits we can hardly imagine now.

<!-- research: Q-024 C-032 -->

Dino gives us another example. Florence faces a grave crisis: Charles of Valois, a French prince, is coming to make peace between the factions. The pope sends him. The Florentines have to listen because he comes on behalf of the pope and the king of France.

But they don't trust him. They fear he wants to favor one side over the other. Which is exactly what happens, as Dino says they discover afterward.

At that moment, decisions have to be made quickly. What are we going to do about this lord who's coming to give us orders? We need to decide, and soon.

What do you do when you have to decide quickly? Call an assembly.

<!-- research: Q-025 C-033 -->

They call an assembly, and everyone can speak. There's a raised platform, and the speakers take turns getting up onto it. Dino tells us that while the government desperately needs a decision, people climb up and talk. And talk. And talk.

Bandino Falconieri has nothing to say. But he gets up there anyway and keeps the platform occupied for half the day.

And these are, Dino says, “the shortest days of the year.” It's November.

You see what that means? There's very little daylight. When it gets dark, everyone goes home. They don't stay out talking. Politics is over. In November, at five in the afternoon, you have either made your decision or you haven't. That's it. Nobody stays out after dark.

These are limitations we barely imagine. For them, they're concrete problems in everyday life. And in this political world Dino inhabits...

## 8. Money and Diplomacy: The Cardinal and the Florins

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00477–U-00514 -->

<!-- research: C-034 -->

Money counts for a great deal.

Money circulates in this world. A lot of it circulates, and it matters. Without it, you can't fight a war. You can't conduct politics either.

When the commune needs money, rich citizens and great financiers advance it. And the city has to be grateful. Once you've advanced money to the city, you wait for repayment, certainly. But while you're waiting, there are plenty of ways to be rewarded. Wealthy citizens and bankers are quite happy to lend to the government.

Money matters in foreign affairs too. Everything is done with money.

<!-- research: Q-026 C-035 -->

Someone like Cardinal Matteo d'Acquasparta is a rare exception. At one point the pope sends him to Florence, as popes so often do. Florence is a great papal ally, and the pope is a little disheartened to see the Florentines doing nothing but tear one another apart. The city is forever split by factional conflict and civil war.

The pope wants peace. The Florentines are very nervous about anyone arriving from the pope with orders. They resent cardinals coming to tell them what to do. But Matteo d'Acquasparta comes and tries to make peace.

A great many people find that irritating. Eventually a man whom Dino describes as having “little sense” fires a crossbow bolt at the window of the house where the cardinal is staying. It sticks in a wooden board at the window. No one is hurt, but the cardinal takes it rather badly.

He's about to leave and place Florence under interdict, restricting public church services in the city. They have to calm him down.

How do you calm a cardinal down?

Send him money.

The government checks the law. Without taking the matter to the public councils, it can offer up to two thousand florins.

All right, then. Send Cardinal d'Acquasparta two thousand florins.

Dino and the others bring them to him. In fact, Dino tells us that he carries them personally, in a silver cup. Two thousand new florins, freshly minted.

Two thousand florins is an enormous sum. But still: it's so little.

The cardinal replies that he appreciates them.

He looks at them for a long time.

## 9. Offices, Justice, and Public Money

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00515–U-00578 -->

<!-- research: Q-029 C-036 -->

That is why people enter city politics. The picture Dino gives us of the political world he works in is appalling. Nobody cares about the public interest. Everyone is there to look after themselves, their relatives, and their faction—in that order. Those are the only things that matter.

He describes a constant “rivalry for office.” Offices are seats of power. There are innumerable seats from which you can make important decisions, perhaps for only two months.

People tear one another apart to get them. Once you've secured a seat and know you have only two months in it, you had better make it pay quickly. Anything can happen. Everything is bought and sold. Public contracts are bought and sold. Court cases are bought and sold.

<!-- research: Q-030 -->

And in these circumstances, Dino says, no decision ever sticks. Anything decided one day is undone the next.

<!-- research: Q-031 -->

Let me quote him again: “Wrongdoing goes unpunished by law. Once the wrongdoer has friends and money to spend, he gets off for the crime he has committed.”

The stakes are enormous. Being in government means you won't have trouble with the courts. Your cases will be buried. And remember: we're still describing Florence in the early 1300s.

It also means you're the one putting your hands into the citizens' pockets, to use another expression from today's politics.

Because government taxes people.

What are the great issues there? Who pays the taxes, and what happens to public money.

<!-- research: Q-032 -->

The city has a treasury. Those in power swear to safeguard it. Instead, Dino says, “they found ways to steal it more effectively.” They take money out under the pretext of rewarding people who have served the commune.

Up to two thousand florins, of course, no vote is needed. Just hand it over. And out the money goes.

<!-- research: Q-033 C-037 -->

Some cases are scandalous. One in particular catches Dino's attention because people can barely stand it anymore.

A great faction leader dies: Messer Rosso della Tosa, one of the richest and most influential men in Florence. His party has his two sons knighted, together with a young relative, and showers them with money.

Now, making someone a knight costs a fortune. There must be great celebrations, public banquets, gifts. Horses and armor have to be bought. The expense is enormous.

That is one reason knights enjoy such prestige: there are relatively few of them. You need a great deal of money to become a knight.
