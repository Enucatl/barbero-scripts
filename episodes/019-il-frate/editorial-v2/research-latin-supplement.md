Evidence only; no historical changes have been approved. Original Latin is transcribed from the **actual page images visually inspected**, not back-translated from Cantarelli. ## Frederick's request and Elias's conditional instruction — confirmed

Lecture targets U452–469, subject to frozen-source re-anchoring.

**Printed page39, lines14–30**, [actual scan](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/img/300/mgh_ss_32_00070.jpg); [reader at page39](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/index.htm#page/39/mode/1up). Image downloaded and visually read in full on2026-10-02. Local convenience copy `/tmp/il-frate-research/mgh-p39.jpg`.

### Emperor's request, reported by Salimbene — continuous original span

> Tunc scripsit imperator fratri Helye generali ministro ordinis fratrum Minorum, quod, si caram habebat gratiam suam, sic exaudiret eum, ut me redderet patri meo.

**Direct translation:** Then the emperor wrote to Brother Elias, minister general of the Order of Friars Minor, that, if he valued his favor, he should grant his request by returning me to my father.

This is **Salimbene's reported content**, not a complete surviving imperial letter quoted verbatim. No condition concerning Salimbene's consent occurs in this sentence.

### Actual quoted incipit of imperial letter — separate boundary

Narrator introduces `Quarum exordium tale fuit:` followed by:

> Ad Guidonis de Adam fidelis nostri suspiria mitiganda

**Direct translation:** To relieve the sighs of Guido de Adam, our faithful subject.

The chronicle immediately says `et cet.`; only this opening is directly quoted. Salimbene explains that Brother Illuminato, Elias's secretary/scribe, copied fine letters from rulers into a separate book and later showed him this epistle while they lived at Siena. **Edition footnote5 explicitly says the imperial letter is not extant** (`Quae non exstant; Reg. Imp. V,1, nr.2409`). Distinguish the retrospectively reported letter, its surviving incipit within the chronicle, and an independently surviving original document.

### Elias's instruction — continuous original span, narrator's indirect report

> Lectis igitur imperialibus litteris statim scripsit frater Helyas fratribus de conventu Fanensi, ubi habitabam, quod, si de voluntate mea procedebat, me sine mora per obedientiam redderent patri meo, alioquin, si cum patre meo ire nolebam, me karum custodirent sicut pupillam oculi sui.

**Direct translation:** So, after reading the imperial letter, Brother Elias immediately wrote to the friars of the convent at Fano, where I was living, that, if it was my wish, they were under obedience to return me to my father without delay; otherwise, if I did not wish to go with my father, they were to cherish and guard me like the pupil of their eye.

**Finding:** The explicit consent condition belongs to **Elias's instruction**, not the emperor's request. This is now confirmed in the Latin critical edition, independently of Cantarelli. It supports an exact factual-intervention proposal if needed; it does not authorize silently changing the lecture or transcript. Both full sentences are narrated indirect speech, so a draft should not put them in quotation marks as verbatim letters written in English.

**Limit:** Page39 gives Salimbene's retrospective documentary recollection. It establishes which speaker/document the chronicle assigns the condition to; it does not independently prove the original imperial letter's entire wording.

## Frederick's infant-language experiment — original account recovered

Lecture targets U805–823, subject to frozen-source re-anchoring.

**Printed page350, lines13–24**, [actual scan](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/img/300/mgh_ss_32_00381.jpg); [reader at page350](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/index.htm#page/350/mode/1up). Image downloaded and visually read on2026-10-02. Local convenience copy `/tmp/il-frate-research/mgh-p350.jpg`.

### Continuous Latin account, from alleged experiment through explanation

> Secunda eius superstitio fuit, quia voluit experiri, cuiusmodi linguam et loquelam haberent pueri, cum adolevissent, si cum nemine loquerentur. Et ideo precepit baiulis et nutricibus, ut lac infantibus darent, ut mammas sugerent, et balnearent et mundificarent eos, sed nullo modo blandirentur eis nec loquerentur. Volebat enim cognoscere, utrum Hebream linguam haberent, que prima fuerat, an Grecam vel Latinam vel Arabicam aut certe linguam parentum suorum, ex quibus nati fuissent. Sed laborabant in cassum, quia pueri sive infantes moriebantur omnes. Non enim vivere possent sine aplausu et gestu et letitia faciei et blanditiis baiularum et nutricum suarum.

**Direct translation:** His second superstition was that he wished to discover what language and manner of speech children would have when they grew up if they spoke with no one. He therefore ordered the attendants and wet nurses to give the infants milk so that they would suck at the breast, and to bathe and clean them, but in no way to caress them or speak to them. For he wished to find out whether they would have the Hebrew language, which had been the first, or Greek or Latin or Arabic, or perhaps the language of their parents, from whom they had been born. But they labored in vain, because the children, or infants, all died. For they could not live without the clapping, gestures, cheerful expressions and caresses of their attendants and wet nurses.

**Translation detail:** `aplausu` is the spelling on this page (with editorial `sic` footnote). “Clapping” renders it conservatively; in affectionate infant care its sense may overlap soothing/patting. `gestu` means gesture/movement; “facial expressions” alone would obscure the separate `letitia faciei`, cheerfulness of face. `blanditiis` covers caresses/affectionate endearments. The original has **no explicit “rocking” in this particular sentence**; rocking the cradle appears in the immediately following sentence. If a proposed English extract includes it, its quotation boundary must include the following sentence, rather than attributing a loose conflation to the shorter span.

### Immediately following sentence — separate continuous boundary

> Unde fascenine, -narum, dicuntur carmina, que mulier dicit movendo cunas, ut sopiat puerum, sine quibus puer male posset dormire et quietem habere.

**Direct translation:** Hence the songs which a woman sings while moving the cradle to lull a child to sleep are called *fasceninae*; without them the child would have difficulty sleeping and resting.

**Textual detail:** Edition prints the lemma `fascenine, -narum` and footnotes its unusual spelling and supplied grammatical ending. The Latin quotation reproduces the printed headword rather than silently standardizing it to *fescenninae*. No need carry this grammatical digression into an adaptation if the selected excerpt ends before it.

**Speaker/document boundaries:** All of this is Salimbene's narration. Frederick's commands and question are **indirect speech**, not words quoted from an imperial order. The final explanation is Salimbene's statement about why the infants died. “His second superstition” connects it to a hostile sequence of anecdotes; the next sentence after the cradle discussion begins the third. Editorial footnote1 points to the comparable Herodotus story (II,2).

**Verdict and limit:** The Latin attribution and precise wording are now recovered and an exact source excerpt could be proposed. This does **not** establish that the experiment occurred, that Salimbene witnessed it, or that its alleged mortality supports modern causal inference. The historiographical limits recorded in research-discovery.md remain. No editorial change is authorized by this recovery.

### Arrival on foot — original continuous sentence

Printed p222, lines23–28:

> Et veniebat non eques, sed pedes; et fratres sui germani, qui tres comites erant, quorum primus Robertus, ultimus Karulus dicebatur, qui fecit magna et laude dignissima, consimili humilitate et habitu sequebantur, ut vere illud propheticum dicere possent: Hi in curribus et hi in equis, nos autem in nomine domini Dei nostri invocabimus.

**Direct translation:** And he came on foot, not on horseback; and his brothers, who were three counts—the eldest was called Robert and the youngest Charles, who did great and highly praiseworthy deeds—followed in similar humility and dress, so that they could truly say those prophetic words: “Some in chariots and some on horses, but we shall call upon the name of the Lord our God.”

**Boundaries:** Salimbene describes the king coming to the Franciscan church; immediately preceding sentence says the king wore a pilgrim's dress with bag and staff. The final words are Salimbene's scriptural comparison (Psalm19:8 Vulgate /20:7 common English numbering), **not an attested speech by Louis or his brothers**. For a shorter source excerpt, ending at `sed pedes` faithfully isolates the on-foot observation; do not extend its scope to every mile of the journey.

### Louis's actual request — indirect narration

Printed p223, lines5–9:

> Cum autem essemus in capitulo congregati, rex cepit dicere facta sua recommendando se et fratres suos et dominam reginam, matrem suam, et totam suam societatem, et devotissime genuflectendo petiit orationes et suffragia fratrum. Et aliqui fratres de Francia, qui erant iuxta me, ex devotione et pietate quasi inconsolabiliter flebant.

**Direct translation:** When we had assembled in the chapter house, the king began to explain his affairs, commending himself, his brothers, the queen his mother and his whole company; and, kneeling with the greatest devotion, he asked for the friars' prayers and intercessions. And some French friars who were beside me wept from devotion and piety, almost inconsolably.

**Finding:** No gold-or-silver formulation appears in this report of Louis's speech. Those crying are specifically **some French friars beside Salimbene**, not every French person. This is narrated summary, not verbatim royal speech. `suffragia` here means spiritual assistance/intercessions.

### Speaker sequence before gold/silver — exact identifying sentence

After Louis, **Cardinal Odo**, former Paris chancellor, speaks briefly. Then p223, lines14–16 explicitly identifies the third speaker:

> Post istos duos locutus est frater Iohannes de Parma, generalis minister, cui ex offitio incombebat responsio, dicens:

**Direct translation:** After those two, Brother John of Parma, the minister general, whose office required him to reply, spoke, saying:

The edition prints `incombebat` with a footnote marking the manuscript form (rather than silently normalizing to *incumbebat*). John's quoted response begins with scriptural introductory sentences, continues through the gold/silver passage and the grant of spiritual services, and ends on p223 line38, before the narrator resumes `Audiens hec rex...`.

### John of Parma's gold/silver passage — continuous excerpt within his direct speech

Printed p223, lines17–23:

> Rex noster et dominus et pater et benefactor, et qui congregationi pauperum affabilem se fecit, venit ad nos humiliter et utiliter, curialiter et benigne, et primo locutus est nobis, sicut eum decebat; nec petit a nobis aurum et argentum, quibus per Dei gratiam eraria sua sufficienter abundant, sed postulat orationes et suffragia fratrum et pro tali negotio, pro quo multipliciter commendandus videtur.

**Direct translation:** Our king and lord and father and benefactor, who has made himself gracious toward the congregation of the poor, has come to us humbly and helpfully, courteously and kindly, and he has spoken to us first, as befitted him. He does not ask us for gold and silver, with which, by God's grace, his treasuries are sufficiently stocked; instead he asks for the friars' prayers and intercessions, and for an undertaking for which he appears deserving of praise in many ways.

**Finding:** The chronicle assigns the gold/silver contrast unambiguously to **John of Parma**, speaking **about the king in the third person**. Replacing Louis's lecture speech with this recovery would require both speaker and grammatical-person changes, not merely improving translation. A proposal should retain the distinction between John's direct speech in the chronicle and Louis's indirectly summarized request. No such change is authorized by research alone.

### Subsequent travel: direct evidence against all-journey walking

Printed p225 describes Salimbene following the king after the chapter, visiting friars at Vézelay and seeing the king wait while Charles continued to pray. Lines26–28:

> Et dictum est ei, quod Karulus ferventer orabat. Et rex gaudebat et patienter expectabat fratrem orantem nec ascendebat equum.

**Direct translation:** And he was told that Charles was praying fervently. The king rejoiced and patiently waited for his brother as he prayed, and did not mount his horse.

**Finding:** The broader narrative explicitly contemplates Louis mounting a horse on this journey. This is stronger evidence than mere absence of a months-long walking claim: the observed on-foot entry at Sens must not be generalized into exclusive walking throughout the kingdom. The scene is at **Vézelay**, a later visit, not the Sens chapter-house scene. The p225 editor dates that visit21 June1248 and footnote8 identifies the eventual embarkation port as Aigues-Mortes; those are editorial notes, not words in Salimbene's quoted sentence.

**Limits common to this group:** The chronicle is retrospective eyewitness narration with literary/scriptural framing. “Direct speech” means the chronicle prints a speech as spoken; it does not establish a stenographic record. Exact recovery permits responsible excerpt proposals, while the lecture's current narration remains subject to explicit editorial decisions.

## Coordinator-requested addendum: Pisa dream — critical Latin confirmed

- [p46, bread-giving action and dream dialogue](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/img/300/mgh_ss_32_00077.jpg)

Local convenience copies `mgh-p45.jpg`, `mgh-p46.jpg` in `/tmp/il-frate-research/`. Distinguish these from the nearby **first** dream of Mary and infant Jesus on p40 onward; the present scene is the Pisa dream after the insulting encounter.

### Transition establishes a dream, not a continuation of the real encounter

Printed p45, lines29–33, continuous sentence:

> Cumque talibus cogitationibus quasi totam noctem duxissem insomnem, sicut Deo placuit, accidit michi modicus somnus, in quo visionem michi Deus ostendit pulcherrimam, que consolationem contulit anime mee et iocunditatem atque dulcedinem inauditam.

**Direct translation:** And when I had spent almost the whole night awake with such thoughts, as God willed, a little sleep came upon me, in which God showed me a most beautiful vision that brought my soul comfort, joy and unheard-of sweetness.

The real Parmesan's reproach occurs earlier on p45, following the departure from the musical performance. The narrator then recalls that night worrying about the prospect of fifty years of begging, before this explicit sleep/vision transition.

### Street and merchants' hostel — bounded clause within dream description

Printed p45, lines34–37; excerpt ends at the clause boundary before `et illam partem vitabam`:

> Videbatur siquidem michi, quod irem panem hostiatim mendicando, sicut soliti sunt facere fratres, et ibam per contratam Sancti Michaelis de Pisis ex parte Vicecomitum, quia ex alia mercatores Parmenses domum habebant ad hospitandum, quam Pisani fundicum appellant

**Direct translation:** For it seemed to me that I was going from door to door begging for bread, as the friars customarily do, and I was going through the district of San Michele in Pisa on the Visconti side, because on the other side Parmesan merchants had a lodging house which the Pisans call a *fondaco*.

The same sentence continues into p46: he avoids that side out of embarrassment, since he was not yet sufficiently strengthened in Christ, and fear of hearing words on his father's behalf that might shake his heart. Thus the **narrative placement** of the detailed street/fondaco scene is unambiguously within the dream. This does not prove he never avoided the merchants in waking life; it establishes how the chronicle narrates this instance.

### Jesus, Mary and Joseph give bread — full continuous passage

Printed p46, lines7–14:

> Cum autem ex parte Arni per burgum Sancti Michaelis descenderem, ecce subito prospexi et vidi, quod filius Dei veniebat ab una domo et portabat panem et ponebat in sporta. Idem faciebat beata virgo, et idem Ioseph, nutritius pueri, cui beata virgo fuerat desponsata. Et sic fecerunt, quousque et circa completa et sporta impleta fuit. Est autem consuetudo in partibus illis, quod sporta ponitur inferius panno cooperta, et frater ascendit domos, ut panem petat et portet et ponat in sporta.

**Direct translation:** But as I went down through the quarter of San Michele on the Arno side, suddenly I looked and saw that the Son of God was coming from a house, carrying bread and putting it into the basket. The Blessed Virgin was doing the same, and so was Joseph, the child's foster father, to whom the Blessed Virgin had been betrothed. They continued in this way until the begging round was completed and the basket was filled. In those parts it is customary for the basket to be left below, covered with a cloth, while the friar goes up into the houses to ask for bread, bring it down and put it into the basket.

**Lexical details:** `sporta` is a basket/carrier; “bag” is a contextual simplification, not its only possible English equivalent. `circa` is the begging/collection round, not an adverb meaning “about.” `nutritius` is the child's foster father/nurturer. The passage explicitly names all **three**, including Joseph.

**Nuance for the proposed correction:** The holy figures are carrying bread from houses and putting it into Salimbene's collection basket; they are **helping with his begging round**, not simply handing him a loaf from nowhere. The following description explains that asking at houses and bringing bread to the waiting basket constitutes the friars' round. So an overstrong proposal saying they “do not beg at all” would go beyond the evidence. What is absent is the lecture's staging of Jesus and Mary as separately equipped beggars with bags around their necks. The recorded action and number of participants differ; preserve the spirituality of shared poverty when explaining the factual departure.

### Christ's explanation — separate dream-speech boundary

Printed p46, lines20–22:

> Non erubescas ergo, fili, amore meo mendicare, ut vere quod scriptum est dicere possis: Ego autem mendicus sum et pauper, Dominus sollicitus est mei.

**Direct translation:** Therefore, my son, do not be ashamed to beg for love of me, so that you may truly say what is written: “But I am a beggar and poor; the Lord cares for me.”

Christ later says, p46 lines24–26:

> Et modo experimento probasti, quod tui sollicitus fui iuvando te in circa tua et tibi sportam implendo.

**Direct translation:** And now you have learned by experience that I cared for you by helping you on your begging round and filling your basket for you.

**Boundary/limit:** These words are what **Salimbene reports Christ saying in his dream**. The Psalm citation is embedded within that dream speech. They are not independently recorded words of the historical Jesus. The critical Latin now confirms both the dream placement and the bread action; it does not authorize an unapproved rewrite of the lecture.

## Coordinator-requested addendum: Sens ladies comparison — Pisa and Bologna

Lecture target U795–799. Actual MGH p222 scan previously inspected; [direct image](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_32/img/300/mgh_ss_32_00253.jpg). Printed lines16–20:

> Et si rex Francie per Pisas vel per Bononiam transitum fecisset, totus flos dominarum de civitatibus nominatis obvius occurrisset. Tunc recordatus sum, quod vera est Gallorum consuetudo. Nam in Francia solummodo burgenses in civitatibus habitant, milites vero et nobiles domine morantur in villis et possessionibus suis.

**Direct translation:** And if the king of France had passed through Pisa or Bologna, the whole flower of the ladies of the cities named would have come out to meet him. Then I recalled what the French custom really is. For in France only the townspeople live in the cities, while knights and noble ladies remain in their country settlements and estates.

**Finding:** The comparison names **Pisa and Bologna**, not Parma. This is now confirmed in MGH, not merely in Cantarelli. The second sentence leads into Salimbene's social generalization; it must remain an attributed observation rather than an exhaustive demographic fact. `ville` here need not mean fortified castles; “country settlements/estates” is safer than silently equating every residence with a castle.
