# The Friar

## 1. Inside Salimbene’s Mind

<!-- chapter: CH-001; transcript: U-00001–U-00048 -->

This episode begins a trilogy about three men of the Middle Ages: a friar, a merchant, and a knight. We’ll explore who they are, how they think, and how they see the world.

<!-- research: C-001 -->

How can we know these people? How can we get inside their heads? Well, all three of them write. They leave behind books, chronicles, works into which they put a great deal of themselves.

The objection is obvious. These aren’t exactly ordinary people. A knight who writes a book isn’t quite like all the other knights. A merchant who writes a book—even less so. A friar, perhaps. Friars do quite a bit of writing. Even so, these are three exceptional individuals.

But their works are so extensive that the person is there inside them. You find the man himself: his character, his way of reasoning, his values, his prejudices, his view of the world.

We begin with the friar, who leaves us the biggest book of the three.

<!-- research: C-002 -->

His chronicle runs to about nine hundred printed pages. Out of that ocean of stories, which ones reveal who he is and how he thinks?

<!-- research: C-003 -->

His name is Salimbene of Parma. Strictly speaking, Salimbene de Adam: that’s his family name. But once he leaves Parma, everybody knows him as Salimbene of Parma.

He’s a Franciscan friar of the thirteenth century, a follower of Saint Francis. In some respects, he may think much like other friars. In others, his outlook is entirely his own.

Let’s see what goes on inside the head of this thirteenth-century friar.

## 2. A World Explained by Scripture

<!-- chapter: CH-002; transcript: U-00049–U-00143 -->

<!-- research: C-004 -->

The first thing that strikes me when I read Salimbene is his certainty that he lives in an ordered world. A rational world.

The world of an Italian friar in the thirteenth century is orderly and rational, and human beings deserve none of the credit. Human beings are scoundrels and criminals. They do everything they can to destroy that harmony.

But the world itself is orderly because God has made it that way. There is a profound confidence that the world has a logic. God has built it, and God has also given human beings the means to interpret it.

Those means are the Holy Scriptures: the Bible. Scripture is an enormous instruction manual for the world.

To someone like Salimbene, everything is in the Bible. Every question has an answer there. Whatever happens, someone who knows the Bible can find not one passage but five, ten, fifteen that speak to what is happening and tell him how to behave.

It’s a labyrinth of an instruction manual, full of contradictions. You have to know how to interpret it. But everything is there. If you know it, you can find your way through life.

<!-- research: C-005 -->

And here’s the first astonishing thing we discover when we enter the head of Brother Salimbene, this Franciscan from the Po Valley. He practically knows the Bible by heart.

Say that today, and people may not believe you. But that’s how these intellectuals work. Their capacity for memorization is staggering compared with ours.

And they need it. Salimbene isn’t just any friar. He’s a preacher, one of the men selected and trained by the Church to speak to people. To stand on a platform in a room with a thousand people and make them listen. Make them understand.

What does he speak about? Faith, theology, religion. He has to fight heresy. He has to convince people that the Church is right and the heretics are wrong. To do that, he must constantly quote the Bible in support of what he says.

There’s more to it than that. In this intellectual world, knowing your subject is a source of pride. Being good at it. Being able to hold your own in a discussion. These men pride themselves on their skill in public debates.

There are plenty of things to discuss, plenty of questions without settled answers. Intellectuals meet and argue in public. The best man wins. And the best man is also the one who can produce one biblical passage, or five, or ten, to support his argument.

You don’t have the book there in front of you. You have to know it by heart.

How can anyone know the Bible by heart? Because that’s how they work.

<!-- research: Q-001 -->

There’s a very revealing passage in Salimbene’s chronicle. At one point he quotes a little satirical song making fun of a Dominican, a friar from a rival religious order. Making fun of Dominicans is always a good thing, as far as Franciscans are concerned. We’ll come back to that.

He quotes eight lines of this little Latin song. Then he says, “I don’t remember any more. It’s been so long since I read it. And when I did read it, I wasn’t particularly interested, so I didn’t learn it properly by heart.”

Do you see what that means? Normally, when you read something, you memorize it.

You have to. There are so few books. Suppose you’ve been sent to the friary at Fano. In its library there’s a wonderful book that interests you. You read it. Who knows whether you’ll ever see that book again?

Every book is written out by hand. So you read it, and you learn it by heart.

Only occasionally can you afford to have a book copied. You borrow it, find a scribe, find the money, and pay him. Or, if you’re really interested, you sit down and copy it yourself. An intellectual of this period may spend three months copying a borrowed book that matters to him.

Books have immense value.

<!-- research: C-006; Q-002 -->

“Take notes,” someone might say.

It’s not that easy. On what? Paper has only just been invented, and it’s expensive. Parchment costs even more.

At one point Salimbene says, “When I was young, I began writing a chronicle.” Not the chronicle that survives, which he writes when he’s old. An earlier one. “I got as far as the Lombards. Then I had to stop. I was so poor I couldn’t even get hold of parchment.”

In those circumstances, the only storehouse available to you is your head. And you put into it everything it can hold.

So Salimbene knows his Bible by heart. That means he can find his way in the world. He has answers ready for his own anxieties and for the doubts the faithful bring to him.

Why do the friars matter? Why do preachers matter? Because the faithful are full of doubts. Someone has to be able to answer them. Salimbene belongs to that small group of professionals who know how.

And if you know the Bible well enough, it even lets you predict the future.

That interests Salimbene enormously. It interests many people in his world. Predicting the future—just think of it. They’re fairly convinced it can be done. Everything is already in the Bible. It foreshadows what has yet to happen. Work at it carefully enough, and it becomes a manual for predicting the future too.

## 3. Prophets, Disappointments, and Erased Books

<!-- chapter: CH-003; transcript: U-00144–U-00263 -->

<!-- research: C-007; Q-003; Q-004 -->

One doctrine is particularly influential in this period: the teaching of Abbot Joachim of Fiore.

Joachim is a Calabrian monk from the previous century, long dead by Salimbene’s time. He has built an immense system of biblical interpretation that is supposed to reveal the future.

The Church has never officially said, “Yes, that’s true.” Nor has it said, “It’s all nonsense.” People are left to make up their own minds.

Many become followers of Joachim. Salimbene, writing as an old man, says, “I don’t believe in it anymore. I’ve seen for myself that the predictions didn’t come true. They don’t work. But I did believe. I believed for a long time.”

<!-- research: C-008 -->

Salimbene is born sometime between about 1220 and 1230. Long before, Joachim has predicted that in 1260 the world will change.

Don’t ask me what calculations he has made using biblical chronology. They are formidable calculations.

What is supposed to happen? First, the Antichrist comes into the world. That much will happen: Revelation says so. That part of the future is already written. The Antichrist will come, commit the most terrible crimes, and plunge the world into bloodshed.

But in 1260, Joachim says, the Antichrist will be defeated. The world will enter a new age of grace, happiness, and harmony.

The young Salimbene believes it. What do you think is the main concern of people who believe it?

Let’s see who the Antichrist is. He has to turn up sooner or later. As soon as he appears, let’s try to recognize him.

<!-- research: C-009; Q-005; Q-006 -->

Eventually a candidate emerges who seems a certainty: Emperor Frederick II.

Frederick II of the Hohenstaufen dynasty. Known as “the wonder of the world.” A great enemy of the papacy. A powerful, learned ruler, passionately loved by some and deeply feared by others. We’ll return to him later.

For many people, Frederick is the Antichrist. He’s already committing crimes. He’ll get worse.

Salimbene believes it.

Years later, he writes, “At one point, word spread that Emperor Frederick had died. I refused to believe it. I was so convinced he was the Antichrist, and the full catastrophe the Antichrist was supposed to cause hadn’t happened yet. So I didn’t believe it.”

Eventually he has to believe it. “I heard it from the pope’s own mouth. I was in Ferrara. The pope came through and preached to the people. I was on the balcony beside him, touching him. And the pope announced publicly that Emperor Frederick II was dead.”

That’s 1250. Thirty years later, Salimbene says, “I was devastated. The whole system I had imagined for predicting the future was collapsing.”

<!-- research: C-010 -->

Salimbene may no longer believe Joachim’s system, but he hasn’t lost his fascination with predicting the future.

We, of course, think we know how much the economy will grow next year. Which is just as ridiculous. Probably more so.

Salimbene, too, is ready to give another prediction a chance.

<!-- research: C-011; Q-007 -->

One day people tell Salimbene that a new prophet has appeared. Who is he? A poor man from Parma, Salimbene’s own city. A weaver.

He has begun making prophecies, and the things he says seem to come true. Eventually he retires to Fontevivo, a Cistercian monastery outside Parma. The Cistercians take him in.

“I’ve heard so much about this man,” Salimbene says. “He spends all day shut in his room, writing.”

An artisan, you understand. A working man. A weaver, predicting the future.

Salimbene hears these stories, and eventually he can’t stand it any longer. He has to meet the man.

<!-- research: Q-008; Q-009; Q-010 -->

He sets out for Fontevivo. When he arrives, he finds a friend there, another Franciscan, who is a physician. He’s staying with the Cistercians, a different religious order, because they need a doctor.

“Salimbene,” his friend says, “I can lend you all the books you want. But the man you’re looking for is dead.”

“But he must have left his books! I know he was always writing.”

Salimbene has already mentioned a revealing detail: this man writes and writes, and, to use his exact expression, “He willingly publishes his works.”

That’s another extraordinary thing. We tend to associate publishing a book with printing it. But how do you publish a book in the thirteenth century, before the printing press?

And yet they have this idea of publishing. You can write for yourself. You can write for your family: a book of family memories, for example. But some people want their writing to become known. They circulate it. If someone comes along, they let him read it. They let him copy it. Everything has to be copied by hand.

This prophet willingly circulates his work. So Salimbene arrives and asks, “Surely his books are still here?”

<!-- research: Q-011; Q-012 -->

“Nothing is left of his books,” his friend says.

“Why not?”

“Well, lots of people believed in him. But I thought there were serious problems with these prophecies. To make a long story short, there’s an old monk here who works in our scriptorium, the workshop where manuscripts are copied. He has a particular skill: he knows how to erase manuscripts.”

Why erase manuscripts? We come back to the same problem. Parchment is expensive, and it isn’t always available. A library that produces books can find itself with a chance to copy an important new work—and no parchment.

What do you do? You erase an old manuscript you don’t need anymore.

That takes skill. Someone has to know how to scrape off the writing without ruining the parchment. We tear our hair out today, thinking of everything that may have been lost. For them, it’s essential.

“There was this old monk who knew how to do it,” Salimbene’s friend continues. “He said, ‘One of these days I’ll die. I’d like to pass the technique on to someone.’ Nobody wanted to learn. So I did. I learned how to scrape manuscripts. And since I needed some manuscripts to practice on, and I thought it would be better if this prophet’s books didn’t circulate too widely, I erased them all.”

Once again, Salimbene is terribly disappointed.

Notice the outlook involved here. It is already the outlook that, much later, will lead the Inquisition to burn books and the Church to publish its Index of Forbidden Books.

What’s interesting is that in Salimbene’s time this still isn’t something imposed by the power of the Church. There is still considerable freedom. Ideas move around. Books circulate.

But every member of the Church’s learned establishment—everyone charged with guiding the faithful toward salvation—takes individual responsibility for deciding whether a book is good or bad. And if it is bad, he takes responsibility for preventing it from circulating.

## 4. Latin, the Devil, and the Danger of Pride

<!-- chapter: CH-004; transcript: U-00264–U-00352 -->

<!-- research: C-012; Q-013; Q-014; Q-015 -->

Salimbene belongs to a highly educated social group. It isn’t just the Bible he carries in his head. His head is a library. These are people who guide the world, morally and spiritually rather than politically. Learning is their identity.

They write, speak, and think in Latin. Salimbene thinks in the dialect of Parma too. You can see that very clearly in the Latin he writes. But the ability to think, speak, and write in Latin distinguishes you from ordinary people. It is an important part of a friar’s identity.

So important that their chief enemy, the devil, shares this pride in learning and good Latin.

Salimbene tells a perfect story about it.

Someone brings a man possessed by the devil to a friar Salimbene knows. The man is a peasant, an uncouth countryman.

The friar stands before him. But first he has to check. You can’t always assume it’s really possession.

“Devil, if you’re in there, prove it. Speak to me in Latin.”

The devil begins speaking Latin through the peasant’s mouth. And makes grammatical mistakes.

The friar starts mocking him. “Oh, don’t make me laugh! You think you can speak Latin? Listen to yourself! You’re no good at all.”

The devil, who shares the friars’ system of values perfectly, gets angry.

“I can speak Latin as well as you,” he says. “But how am I supposed to do it with this peasant’s mouth? His tongue is so uncouth I can’t speak properly through it.”

<!-- research: Q-016 -->

Of course, this ideal of a learned elite, proud of its culture, carries a danger. Pride in learning easily becomes conceit.

Salimbene knows it. He falls into the trap himself often enough. There are countless moments when he says, in effect, “I know my Bible. They’re not going to fool me.”

But he recognizes the risk, and tells stories showing that even a great preacher has to be careful. Conceit is always waiting.

<!-- research: Q-017 -->

A couple of these stories concern a famous preacher of his time, Brother Giovanni of Vicenza. A Dominican. And, as I said, people are happy to tell stories that don’t show the Dominicans in the best light.

Giovanni is one of those men who can stand on a stage or balcony before a packed square and make people weep whenever he chooses. People that good at something can become rather proud of themselves.

Eventually Giovanni becomes convinced he is a saint. All right, let that pass. But he also becomes convinced he can perform miracles on his own, without God even needing to intervene. And above all, he wants other people to admire him as a saint.

“One day,” Salimbene says, “Brother Giovanni comes to one of our Franciscan friaries. While he’s there, he has the friary barber shave him. Then he takes offense because the friars don’t collect the hairs from his beard as relics.”

<!-- research: C-013; Q-018; Q-019 -->

After a while, the Franciscans have had quite enough of Giovanni. Salimbene describes what happens next with enormous pleasure.

One of their friars is a Florentine, Brother Diotisalvi. And Salimbene says something very interesting: everybody knows Florentines love making fun of people. They love playing tricks. They’re tremendous practical jokers.

That’s already their reputation: they enjoy a joke, even a cruel one.

So Brother Diotisalvi goes to the Dominican friary where Giovanni is staying. They invite him to lunch.

“Yes, certainly, I’ll stay. But you have the saint here. You have the saint. I can’t leave without a relic. If you want me to stay for lunch, give me a relic of the saint. A piece of his tunic. Just a scrap.”

<!-- research: Q-020; Q-021 -->

The Dominicans are delighted that their saint is so admired. They find an old tunic, cut a piece from it, and give it to him.

After a good lunch, Brother Diotisalvi heads for the latrine.

Medieval writers can move from lofty ideas to bodily functions without missing a beat. Salimbene is no exception.

There, Diotisalvi wipes himself with the piece of the saint’s robe they have just given him. Then he drops it in.

“Oh! Oh! Help! Help! I’ve lost the relic! Come and help me get it out!”

All the Dominicans come running. Diotisalvi is stirring around in the latrine with a stick, trying to retrieve it. Eventually the Dominicans catch on. They realize what is happening and slink away, thoroughly humiliated.

Salimbene is delighted by stories like this.

What does it mean? It means: be careful, because learning can so easily become conceit.

It means: we may live in the world of spirit and intellect, but the body exists too. Material things exist. They matter. We can’t ignore them.

And it also means that the Dominicans are, in any case, a little worse than us Franciscans.

That is beyond dispute.

## 5. Franciscans and Their Competitors

<!-- chapter: CH-005; transcript: U-00353–U-00411 -->

<!-- research: C-014; Q-022 -->

That brings us to another great certainty in Salimbene’s mind: the Franciscans are the most wonderful organization in the world.

They do all the work. They keep the Church going. Being a Franciscan is the most important thing imaginable. Nobody else comes close.

Often that is the measure by which Salimbene judges people. A bishop. A pope. Did he love the Franciscans? If he loved them and treated them well, he can be forgiven anything. The Franciscans are the best there is.

Competition, naturally, is a nuisance.

The Dominicans—well, the Dominicans are tolerable. I’ve been joking about them, but they’re a great sister order. They too live by begging. There is much worse competition around.

In Salimbene’s lifetime, a lot of people are copying Saint Francis’s idea: a movement of people who renounce wealth, go barefoot or wear sandals, practice the poverty of the apostles, and imitate Christ.

And something else is very clear in Salimbene’s mind. We Franciscans have a precise model. Saint Francis has taught us how to do this. You can’t just have the first person who comes along put on a pair of sandals and claim to be doing what we do. It’s a disgrace. A scandal.

Thinking about it makes Salimbene lose all sense of proportion. He writes page after page denouncing this unfair competition.

<!-- research: Q-023; Q-024 -->

There is one movement he knows particularly well because it begins in Parma: the Apostolics. Its founder is a man called Brother Gerardino Segalelli.

Salimbene says this man wanted to become a Franciscan. But he was a worthless fellow, and they wouldn’t have him. So he founded his own movement. Created his own order.

“In our friary he had seen a picture of the apostles,” Salimbene says. “You know how we depict them: sandals, a cloak wrapped around the shoulders. He saw that and thought he ought to do the same.”

So he creates a movement of clowns, illiterates, swineherds, shepherds who have got it into their heads that they can do what the Franciscans do.

“They know nothing. They can’t read. They’re useless. They don’t say Mass. They don’t pray for their benefactors. They don’t give good advice. They can’t debate. They don’t know the Bible. They’re completely useless. They spend all day in the square looking at women.”

“And yet,” Salimbene says, “people give more to them than they give to us.”

The world turned upside down.

## 6. The Knight’s Son Chooses the Friars

<!-- chapter: CH-006; transcript: U-00412–U-00529 -->

So far, Salimbene may not sound particularly likable.

Certainly he’s an arrogant intellectual, convinced he belongs to an elite of men who rule the world, have an answer for everything, and ought to preach and speak and be listened to.

But we have to say this: to become a Franciscan, Salimbene gives things up that I think very few people would be willing to give up.

<!-- research: C-016; Q-026; Q-027 -->

He is born into a prominent family of knights in Parma. An aristocrat.

When we picture the self-governing cities of medieval Italy, we shouldn’t imagine only industrious merchants and artisans busily making money. The merchants, the artisans, and the money are certainly there.

But beside them stand the nobles. Knights. Great aristocratic families, proud of their blood, their horses, their weapons. Men who know how to fight. The men who build the towers in Italian medieval cities. Men accustomed to taking justice into their own hands and avenging wrongs with violence.

That is the world Salimbene grows up in.

At one point, something slips out of him about revenge. “In Parma, a revenge that is thirty years old is not considered too late. And I, too, am from Parma.”

Salimbene’s father, the knight Messer Guido de Adam, goes out of his mind when he learns that his son has become a Franciscan.

He has two sons. The elder has already become a friar. Well, all right. There’s still the younger one.

Then the younger one, Salimbene, gets the same idea.

For a nobleman, it’s a catastrophe. You’re left without sons to carry on the family. Without anyone to inherit your estate.

Think what family means to the nobility of this period. The dynasty, the lineage, the blood, the descendants. Leaving someone who will succeed me, carry my name, bear my emblem. That is fundamental to the aristocratic outlook.

Salimbene, heir to a noble knight, decides at seventeen to give it all up and go off with the friars. We can hardly appreciate how enormous a decision that is. How much it costs.

And it costs him dearly, because his family tries to stop him.

When he runs away from home and joins the Franciscans, they are already a great, influential religious order. But to a knight, they are still people who go barefoot, begging from door to door. A shameful way of life. And this is my only remaining son.

<!-- research: C-017; Q-028; Q-029 -->

Messer Guido acts. As a member of the highest aristocracy, he goes straight to Emperor Frederick II.

“My son has run off with the friars. Make them give him back.”

That is the level on which Salimbene’s family operates.

Frederick writes to Brother Elias, the head of the Franciscan order. In effect: “This is what I’ve been told. See what you can do. Let’s get this boy back to his father, unless he absolutely insists on staying.”

Armed with the letters, Guido goes to the friary where Salimbene is living, at Fano in Romagna.

He arrives brandishing letters from the emperor and the head of the order, accompanied by several knights. He bursts in.

<!-- research: Q-030; Q-031; Q-032; Q-033; Q-034 -->

“I want to speak to my son.”

They bring the boy.

“You’re my heir,” his father says. “You inherit everything. What are you doing here? Come home.”

Salimbene tells this story fifty years later, when he’s old. He is proud that he stood firm.

“The Gospel says to leave father and mother to follow Christ. That’s what I’m doing.”

Plus an assortment of other biblical quotations I’ll spare you.

Imagine it. He’s seventeen. That is an age when people fight furiously with their fathers now, and they fought furiously with them then, as they have in every age. Salimbene says the worst things he can think of. “No. I’m not coming.”

“It’s these people who have put it into your head,” his father says. “The friars. I want to speak to him alone.”

The friars have to agree. They leave. But, Salimbene tells us, they stand on the other side of the wall, listening.

<!-- research: Q-035; Q-036; Q-037 -->

His father is alone with his seventeen-year-old son. And these are his words:

“My son, don’t believe these piss-in-tunics who have bewitched you.”

Salimbene even explains the insult in his Latin chronicle: it means people who piss in their tunics.

“They’ve bewitched you. They’ve put these ideas in your head. Come home.”

Salimbene holds firm.

“The friars on the other side were trembling like reeds,” he says, “afraid I would give in. If I gave in, who else would ever agree to enter the friary? But I held firm.”

His father leaves, cursing him. He curses both sons and consigns them to the devil. Salimbene will never see him again.

Fifty years later, he still tells the story proudly.

<!-- research: C-018; Q-038 -->

Then he adds, “Fortunately, I was rewarded that very night. I had a dream that showed me I’d done the right thing.”

However proudly he tells the story now, there is a trauma here. A complete break with his father, who curses him and sends him to hell.

Fortunately, that night, the Virgin appears to him in a dream, holding the child. They stretch out their arms, inviting him to embrace them.

“In the dream I went to embrace them, and I felt a sweetness such as I have never felt again in my life. Then I woke up, and that sweetness was still inside me. I couldn’t describe it.”

You know how certain dreams leave something inside you.

If you told these stories to Dr. Freud, there would be quite a bit to say. Salimbene doesn’t know Dr. Freud. For him, it really is the Virgin and child. She has seen everything. She has come to tell him that he has done right, that she is with him, and to reward him.

<!-- research: Q-039 -->

His father tries again. He goes to the pope. They’re even distantly related to the pope. That’s how important the family is.

Later, people tell Salimbene, “Your father went to the pope too, to try to get you out of the Franciscan order.”

Here Salimbene says something characteristic.

“I don’t think the pope would have agreed. But perhaps, to please my father, he might have given me a bishopric.”

Made him a bishop.

You can hear him lingering over the possibility. I might have become a bishop. Well, it didn’t happen. Never mind.

And the trauma of entering the Franciscan order doesn’t end there.

I think this matters. We see Salimbene as an important friar, in touch with popes and bishops. A man with influence and power.

But this man with power walks barefoot and goes out begging for his living. And he experiences that as a very painful ordeal.

## 7. Begging, Shame, and Cabbage

<!-- chapter: CH-007; transcript: U-00530–U-00571 -->

<!-- research: C-019; Q-040; Q-041; Q-042 -->

At one point they send him out to beg. He is desperately ashamed to go begging from door to door.

He is in Pisa. He belongs to the friary there. And one day he meets someone from Parma.

“A man came up to me whom I didn’t know. But he knew me.” Salimbene comes from an important family.

The man grabs him by his robe.

“You wretched boy! What are you doing here? Look at the state you’ve got yourself into. Your father’s house is full of servants who have more than enough to eat. And here you are, begging, taking bread away from people poorer than you.”

He goes on. Salimbene reports it word for word.

“You ought to be in Parma, riding around on horseback, showing yourself off in tournaments, delighting the people who come to watch.” Because that is what noble knights do. “You ought to be admired by the ladies and handing out money to the minstrels. That’s what you should be doing. Not going barefoot from door to door, begging.”

Salimbene pushes him away. Sends him off angrily. Quotes a few biblical passages at him.

But then he says, “That evening, back at the friary, I began to think about it. If I have to go on like this for another fifty years. Begging.”

That time, he really does wonder whether he has made a terrible mistake with his life.

<!-- research: Q-043 -->

Fortunately, another dream comes that night.

In the dream, he is begging in Pisa, keeping to one side of a street because the lodging house of the merchants from Parma is on the other. He is ashamed to meet them, afraid of hearing more words that might shake his resolve.

Then he sees Jesus carrying bread from a house and putting it into Salimbene’s basket. The Virgin does the same. So does Joseph. They help him finish his begging round and fill the basket.

Then he understands. After all, he is doing the right thing.

These dreams always arrive at the right moment. As I say, Dr. Freud might be of assistance. They always arrive at just the right moment.

<!-- research: C-020; Q-044 -->

There is one final sacrifice we cannot leave out. A very serious one.

“The first evening I joined the Franciscans,” Salimbene says, “they gave me a magnificent dinner. From the next day on, nothing but cabbage. I have eaten cabbage every day of my life.”

Remember, this is a young nobleman, raised in comfort.

“Before that, cabbage disgusted me so much I wouldn’t even eat meat if it had been cooked with cabbage.”

And from then on: cabbage every day.

## 8. Courtesy and Bad Manners

<!-- chapter: CH-008; transcript: U-00572–U-00621 -->

<!-- research: C-021 -->

Salimbene is the son of a great noble family, and his choice takes him a long way from that family. Yet he keeps certain habits of thought, certain values that come with his birth.

What strikes me most is that, in one respect, he remains a noble knight. His real measure of people is courtesy or boorishness.

He likes people who are courteous, chivalrous, generous, well brought up. The ill-mannered, the coarse, the stingy, the boorish—those he cannot stand.

These are the values of the chivalric world in which he is born. They are what he uses to judge people. Whether they are sinners is another matter. Everybody is a sinner. Salimbene has seen everything. He has no illusions about people, or about the clergy.

<!-- research: Q-045; Q-046; Q-047 -->

He has even seen an atheist bishop. The bishop of Parma, on his deathbed, is offered the sacraments.

“No, I don’t want them. I don’t believe in any of it.”

“Then why did you become a bishop?”

“For the wealth and the honors.”

<!-- research: Q-048 -->

Salimbene has seen everything, heard everything about popes, archbishops, city governors, emperors, princes, kings.

He has a favorite expression. When he reports something truly outrageous about an important person, he says, in Latin, “He’ll have to answer for it himself.”

Because everyone will answer individually, when the time comes to stand before his creator. Even the pope.

We don’t have to respect anyone if there is no reason to. That creates an important freedom of judgment in Salimbene.

<!-- research: Q-049; Q-050 -->

A powerful man may have plenty of vices, but if he is generous, openhanded, and courteous, then perhaps he’s all right.

Take Filippo, archbishop of Ravenna. He has two nephews, although one of them is actually his son. The son and the other nephew take bribes. Give them presents, and you can get whatever you want from the archbishop: contracts, concessions.

He also has a daughter whom he puts in a convent.

And yet, to Salimbene, the archbishop has impeccable manners. Hospitable, generous, pleasant to people. Salimbene knows him. He has always treated Salimbene well, invited him to dinner. He gives magnificent dinners, feeds everyone, drinks good wine, shares it with everyone.

When he says his daily prayers in his palace, the archbishop walks up and down the room. At each corner stands a little flask of wine, kept cool in icy water.

Whenever he reaches a corner, he takes a little drink. Then he goes on with his prayers.

But he invites people in. He is courteous and well mannered.

<!-- research: Q-051 -->

Unlike Brother Elias, the head of the Franciscan order.

One day a nobleman comes to visit Elias. Elias receives him while seated at the table, on a couch piled with cushions. There is a fine fire burning. He has his cap on. And when this important guest enters, the head of the Franciscan order doesn’t even get up to greet him.

For Salimbene, that is unforgivable. You simply cannot treat a guest that way.

Bad manners, boorishness, and stinginess are the worst things imaginable.

## 9. Wine and Generosity

<!-- chapter: CH-009; transcript: U-00622–U-00669 -->

<!-- research: C-022; Q-052; Q-053 -->

For Salimbene, generosity is something you can see at the dinner table. Who gets invited? Who gets the good wine?

In a world where many people struggle to get enough to eat, having plenty of food is a sign of wealth. Sharing it matters enormously.

What sort of behavior does Salimbene think is wonderful?

He has heard a story about the king of England having a picnic. Picnic is our word for it. He is out in the countryside, eating on the grass with his knights, beside a spring. They bring the wine. There is only one flask.

“Excuse me,” the king says. “Is this all the wine we have?”

“Yes. There isn’t any more.”

All his knights are around him.

“Then we’ll all drink together,” the king says, and pours the flask into the spring. Everyone can drink the wine mixed with the water.

For Salimbene, it’s a magnificent gesture. Here is a truly courteous, well-brought-up man who puts himself on the same level as everyone else.

<!-- research: Q-054 -->

Not like certain bishops, Salimbene says. They sit at home eating and drinking and never invite anybody. Or, if they have guests at their table, the bishop drinks the best wine and everybody else gets the bad stuff.

Or the bishop drinks and everybody else gets nothing, though they would all welcome a drink. As Salimbene puts it, “all throats are sisters.”

<!-- research: C-023; Q-055; Q-056 -->

Someone tells Salimbene that Burgundy produces more wine than Cremona, Parma, Reggio, and Modena put together.

“I didn’t believe it. It seemed impossible.”

Then, on a long journey through France, he visits Burgundy and sees for himself.

“It’s unbelievable. The whole country is one enormous vineyard. There are no fields. They don’t grow grain. Nothing. They just make wine, sell it in Paris, and live very well on the proceeds.”

Burgundy wine. Salimbene writes lyrical pages about it.

White Burgundy, because in that period the nobility like white wine. Red wine is for peasants. But the white Burgundy: golden, fragrant.

<!-- research: Q-057; Q-058 -->

The French do overdo it, though. Salimbene says they’re tremendous drinkers. They drink too much. When they’ve had a few, they think they can take on the world. Frankly, they’re unbearable.

They wake in the morning with red, crusty eyes from all the wine. They go to the first Mass and ask the priest for some of the water he has used for his ritual washing. Would he please put a little in their eyes? They think that this consecrated water will cure the redness caused by too much drinking.

“Once, when I was in France,” Salimbene says, “I knew a friar who had a man come and ask him for water to put in his eyes. The friar told him, ‘Oh, go away! Put water in your wine, not in your eyes.’”

<!-- research: Q-059 -->

And do you know how the Franciscans are organized in France? Salimbene knows, and tells us.

The French province of the order is divided into eight custodies, eight local divisions. Four drink wine. Four drink beer.

That is the fundamental division. One of the great divisions of Europe: the Europe of wine and the Europe of beer.

## 10. A Saintly King at Dinner

<!-- chapter: CH-010; transcript: U-00670–U-00739 -->

Sharing a meal can reveal as much about someone as sharing a bottle of wine. Salimbene sees this when he sits down to dinner with a king.

<!-- research: C-024; Q-060 -->

On that journey through France, Salimbene has all sorts of adventures and meets all sorts of people. Several times he meets King Louis—Louis IX, Saint Louis—who is setting out on crusade.

It makes a tremendous impression on him. Salimbene may be disillusioned. He may expect the worst of people. But he can recognize a saint, and he profoundly admires anyone who manages to be one.

King Louis is such a man. He sets out from northern France to embark for the crusade at Aigues-Mortes. When Salimbene sees him arrive at Sens, the king comes on foot, carrying a pilgrim’s staff and bag.

Because going on crusade means, first of all, going on pilgrimage. Going to Jerusalem.

So the king of France travels across his kingdom dressed as a pilgrim. He stops in towns, stops at the friars’ houses, eats with them, discusses things with them, talks to them. Salimbene meets him several times on this journey.

<!-- research: C-025; Q-061 -->

At Sens, Salimbene goes out to meet the king. As the king arrives, a gift comes for him from the treasurer of the church of Sens.

What do you give a king?

The gift is a huge live pike, in a tub full of water.

A gift fit for a king.

Salimbene says the French like pike. Evidently he doesn’t. But the French like it.

The fish is in the sort of tub used for bathing babies. Salimbene even notes the Tuscan word for it. He has an eye for the smallest details.

<!-- research: Q-062; Q-063 -->

Then the king enters the Franciscan friary and asks the brothers to pray for him and his companions. Salimbene says that some of the French friars beside him are crying almost inconsolably.

When the head of the order, John of Parma, replies, he makes the point: the king isn’t asking them for gold or silver. His treasury has enough of that. He is asking for their prayers.

Salimbene, an Italian visitor, is a little less moved. After all, extra taxes for the French king’s crusade wouldn’t be coming out of his pocket. But the French friars beside him are crying.

Then they go to dinner with the king.

<!-- research: C-026; Q-064 -->

Many decades later, Salimbene still remembers exactly what they ate that day with the king.

People’s idea of a meal is quite different from ours. Their tastes are different. In particular, they don’t have our notion of a sequence in which you begin with savory dishes and move on to sweet ones. They are quite happy to mix different things together.

It is a meatless day, so they eat fish.

“A magnificent dinner,” Salimbene says, “worthy of a great king.” Yet, as you’ll see, it is no rich glutton’s feast. It is worthy of a great king who is also a great saint.

“First that day, we ate cherries. Then they brought white bread, the whitest bread, and wine. Then fresh fava beans cooked in milk. Then fish and crayfish, and eel meatballs. Then rice with almond milk and powdered cinnamon. Then grilled eels with an excellent sauce. Savory tarts. Fresh curds. Fruit.”

A great dinner. But compared with other dinners Salimbene describes, it is grand enough for a king and modest enough for a pilgrim.

Salimbene knows plenty of other dinner stories and menus that are less dignified.

<!-- research: C-027; Q-065; Q-066 -->

For example, the way the patriarch of Aquileia observes Lent.

“The patriarchs of Aquileia really ought to change their ways,” Salimbene says. “But this is what I hear they do.”

Lent lasts forty days. It prepares people for the great sorrow of Good Friday, followed by the joy of the Resurrection. Forty days: a gradual approach.

The patriarch observes it this way. Forty days? On the first day he has a dinner of forty courses served. On the second, thirty-nine. On the third, thirty-eight.

In this way, he arrives at Holy Saturday with a single course. He has truly made a gradual approach to fasting and penance.

“Christ fasted forty days and forty nights in the wilderness,” Salimbene says. “The patriarchs of Aquileia would do well to remember that.”

## 11. A World Full of Differences

<!-- chapter: CH-012; transcript: U-00770–U-00803 -->

Food reveals something else about Salimbene: his extraordinary curiosity. He notices everything, from a new dish to an unfamiliar custom. Whatever catches his attention finds its way into the chronicle.

<!-- research: C-030; Q-071 -->

Writing in his sixties, he remarks that human greed for good food really knows no limits.

“We become more and more greedy, and new things are always being invented. This year, on the feast of Saint Clare, for the first time I ate ravioli without a crust of pasta.”

What does that mean? Perhaps just the filling, without the pasta wrapped around it. Or perhaps he usually ate ravioli baked inside a savory pie, and this time the outer pastry was missing. Medieval cooks often baked food in pastry cases; the English meat pie preserves that tradition.

I’m not sure which he means. What matters here is that Salimbene notices something new at the table and writes it down.

<!-- research: C-031; Q-072; Q-073 -->

He goes to France. In northern France he notices that at certain times of year the days are longer than in Italy, and at other times they are shorter. He writes it down.

He boards a ship at Genoa to go to Provence and notices a difference in the climate.

“When I left Genoa, the almond trees were in blossom. When I reached Provence, there were already big almonds on the trees.”

Clearly, it’s warmer in Provence.

<!-- research: C-032; Q-074; Q-075 -->

During that great journey as a young man, he is outside Sens, welcoming the king. The women of the city come out in procession to greet him.

“Look at the ladies of the city,” someone tells him.

“I looked at them,” he says, “and thought: they look more like serving women to me.”

“Then I remembered that in France the gentlemen and nobles don’t live in the towns. They live in their castles. It isn’t like Italy, where noble knights live in the cities. Here the cities are inhabited by merchants and craftsmen. So naturally their women—well, if we’d been in Parma, the ladies of Parma would have made quite a different impression.”

His curiosity doesn’t free him from prejudice. He still looks at people through the eyes of a nobleman from Parma. Yet alongside those judgments, he has a capacity for sympathy, for understanding what people need.

## 12. Frederick’s Infants and Salimbene’s Humanity

<!-- chapter: CH-013; transcript: U-00804–U-00825 -->

In this, Salimbene is very different from Emperor Frederick II.

He has known Frederick. He has even been fond of him. Then he thinks Frederick is the Antichrist. Then he learns Frederick has died, and is terribly disappointed.

Frederick is an extraordinary man. He too is curious. But some of that curiosity takes a cruel form.

<!-- research: C-033; Q-076; Q-077; Q-078; Q-079 -->

I’ll tell you just one story, a story Salimbene tells.

Frederick gets it into his head to discover the original language of humanity. He takes newborn babies and orders their nurses to raise them without ever speaking to them or showing them affection.

He wants to see what language they will speak. Greek? Latin? Hebrew? Arabic? Or perhaps the language of their parents?

“But he wasted his time,” Salimbene says, “because the babies all died.”

Then Salimbene says something that I find extraordinary.

“For they could not live without the clapping, gestures, cheerful expressions and caresses of their attendants and wet nurses.”

<!-- research: C-034 -->

Emperor Frederick, who was a genius, apparently wasn’t human enough to understand that.

Salimbene, with all his faults, was human enough to know it.

For all his faults, and all the ways he differs from us, Salimbene leaves us a chronicle in which a whole human being emerges. A person seen from every angle.

These stories give us only glimpses of him. But when you read his chronicle, you come to know him. He becomes someone you know.
